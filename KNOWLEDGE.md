# KNOWLEDGE.md — Deal Craft for Buying a Canadian Business

**Built from 61 long-form YouTube transcripts. Every claim is cited to the video it came from.**

This is the Track A half of the knowledge base: how to find, value, structure, diligence and finance
the acquisition of a small Canadian business. `OPERATORS.md` covers what it's like to *run* one — read
that before you believe any seller's numbers.

**The standing caveat.** Roughly 80% of sources are American — 48 of 61. **SBA structures, IRS rules, state law and US
lending norms do not apply in Canada**, and §4 exists specifically to translate them. Sections marked
🇨🇦 are Canadian-sourced or Canada-specific. Everything marked ⚠️ is a place where following US advice
in Canada will cost you money.

**Nothing here is legal, tax or financial advice.** It is a distillation of what practitioners said on
camera, with the errors flagged where I could find them.

# 1. The Top 20 Deal Lessons

## 1. The opportunity exists because there is no one to sell to

**Fewer than 10% of small businesses have a formalised succession plan**, and *"the most common
succession option is no succession at all — the company simply winds down."*
[`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`]

🇨🇦 The Canadian version is blunter: *"Even if your business is saleable, it's hard to find the right
person to buy it. And even if you find the perfect person, **they don't want to pay a lot of money for
a job.** This is why the vast majority of businesses do not ever sell — they usually just wind down."*
[`Canadian-succession/Ep133-Canadian-Business-Succession-Planning-What-You-Need-To-Know.md`]

**Consequence: your realistic competitor for a small Canadian service business is not another buyer. It
is the business quietly closing.** That is leverage, and it is why cold outreach to unlisted owners
works.

## 2. Four of the five reasons a business sells are unplanned

**Burnout/boredom/fatigue · divorce · poor health · need to relocate · retirement.**
*"**Retirement is the only one people plan for.** The other four out of five reasons that businesses go
up for sale is stuff in life that happens to people."*
[`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`]

**Expect to meet sellers mid-crisis, not mid-plan.** And expect roughly **1 in 10 owners** to have
thought about their business as an asset at all.

## 3. Win on the seller's confidence, not on price

> "I have had deals where there were multiple offers and **the seller chose the lowest-priced one,
> because they had the greatest degree of confidence in that particular buyer.**"
> — `David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`

The reason is structural: *"the only way a buyer could pay a truly reasonable price — meaning the
highest price possible — was with a good degree of seller financing. **Those seller note payments
depend on the success of the buyer.**"*

**Three concrete actions:** meet the seller in person, probably twice, *before* offering; **present the
offer yourself** to broker and seller together so you can explain the *whys*; and **make more than one
offer simultaneously** — present two or three structures and let them choose.

The corroborating case: a seller **countered with a better deal than the buyer had asked for** — a
higher headline price but seller financing at 5% instead of a 7% SBA loan — because they wanted an
income stream for their children and disliked a large one-time tax hit.
[`Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`]

## 4. Position yourself as a succession plan, not a buyer

Divitkos won a competitive situation on non-price terms by framing himself as *"a succession plan
looking for the right small business to succeed."* What the sellers actually wanted:
- To **look into the whites of the eyes** of the person who would run their company
- A buyer who was **a portfolio of one** — all their attention on this business
- Commitments to **keep the company name, retain the employees, grow it, and continue the local charity sponsorships**

[`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`]

## 5. Get valuation expectations on the table in the first conversation

> "If I thought a business was worth roughly **six times cash flow** but the owner thought it was worth
> **six times revenue** — that delta is never going to be bridged. **You move on. You don't even bother
> with a second conversation.**"
> "**The question I wanted to answer with every conversation is: is this worth my time on the next
> conversation?**"
> — `In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Analyzing-Software-Businesses-for-Sale-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`

**Then ask how they arrived at the number.** *"They would say, kind of stone-faced: **'Well, that's just
what I need to retire.'** For a selling business owner, from their perspective, that's a perfectly
reasonable justification."* Girdley's shorthand: **"business is worth my mortgage balance."**

**It cuts both ways.** A retirement number can be *below* fair value as easily as above it. Ask.

## 6. Overpaying hands the seller your own future profits

> "**If you overpay for a business, you take all the future profits that should accrue to you, discount
> them to the present, and hand them to the seller.**"
> And on losing to a higher bidder: *"That's good. It means there's **one more dumb or ill-informed
> buyer** that no longer is out there to compete with you."*
> — `David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`

The rebuttal to "I'm buying to avoid risk": *"If you overpay, you're just inviting all that other risk
back into your life anyway. **So what's the use of buying?**"*

## 7. The day-two problem

> "So many people are just so focused on getting a deal done... **On day two, after the deal is done,
> you need to have a successful profitable cash-flowing business** — service all the debts, give
> yourself adequate compensation for the risk, and make sure there's enough cash flow for the things
> **not accounted for in the typical measures of cash flow that buyers use.**"
> — `David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`

**The specific gap: SDE and EBITDA exclude depreciation, so there is no allowance for capital
investment.** *"If you forget that machines wear out, or that to grow you might need more trucks, then
all of a sudden the cash flow you thought was there really isn't there."*

## 8. SDE divides into three claims, and yours is last

> **(1) Debt service — paid first. (2) The buffer before decline hits your pocket. (3) Owner's comp — last in line.**
> — `Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`

The worked example: **$213K SDE − $122K debt service = ~$90K/year**, which is *both* your salary and
your entire margin of safety. *"If one client cancels you go from making 90 grand a year to 60 grand a
year."*

## 9. Reverse-engineer the price from what the cash flow will service

**Don't start from a multiple. Start from the debt the business can carry at a 1.25–1.5× DSCR, and work
backwards to a price.** [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]

The buyer-side version: *"You can only pay so much, because the cash flow has to support the debt
service... **anything above that is basically predicated on growth** — 'we're just going to grow so much
that the debt service doesn't matter.'"*
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/2M-of-EBITDA-and-50-margins-in-disaster-remediation-AA-194.md`]

The lender's version of the same failure: **"Congratulations, you've structured a deal that doesn't
work."** [`David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md`]

## 10. "Enough right with it and enough wrong with it"

> "It had to have **enough right with it** that I could go in and make first-time CEO mistakes and not
> risk the survival of the business. But **enough wrong with it** that I could actually create value
> over a five to seven year time period."
> — `In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Searcher-to-CEO-and-Exit-to-Search-Fund-Investor-Steve-Divitkos.md`

The buy box that goes with it: **owner succession · service or technology · B2B · asset-light ·
recurring revenue · 20+ years old · already profitable · dozens of employees.** Summarised:
*"you're looking for both a business model and an industry that lends itself very well to a first-time
CEO."* [`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/How2Exit-Episode-54-Steve-Divitkos-Mineola-Search-Partners.md`]

## 11. Separate recurring from *reoccurring* revenue before applying any multiple

> "In some instances companies try to position revenue as recurring, but when you get deep down into it
> it's actually **reoccurring** revenue. There's a very important difference — **the former typically
> justifies a much higher valuation than the latter.**"
> — `In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Analyzing-Software-Businesses-for-Sale-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`

**In Jay's industries this distinction *is* the valuation.** A pest control book of auto-billed
quarterly subscribers is recurring; a book of customers who happen to call every spring is not.
*"Your company isn't valued based on how many trucks you have or how good you are at killing bugs —
it's determined by one key metric, and that's monthly recurring revenue."*
[`Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`]

## 12. Ask the second question: will the cash flow continue under *your* stewardship?

**Question one — "what's the cash flow?" — determines the price. Question two determines whether you
should buy it at all.** [`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`]

The illustration: a fried chicken franchise sold **three times**, the first two deals collapsing at the
eleventh hour. Buyers came for the numbers; then they met Tony, who worked first light to 4pm with his
son-in-law covering 4pm–10pm, **personally cleaned the fry slicer daily** to avoid a workers' comp
claim, and ran a four-day rotating deep-clean regimen he supervised himself.

> "They quickly realised that **if they were going to have that cash flow continue under their
> stewardship, they were going to have to spend 50 hours a week there.**"

**The counterweight, equally true:** *"It's very hard to find a small business to buy with one or two
million bucks of EBITDA that does not rely a little bit on being the owner in the market. **It's just
hard to not do that at this size.**"*
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`]

**So the job isn't to find a business without owner dependency. It's to find out exactly what the owner
does, price the replacement, and structure a transition long enough to absorb it.**

## 13. Price in the replacement of the founder's four hats

> "The founder was happily wearing three or four hats that would otherwise need to be occupied by three
> or four other individuals — because he had 30 years of experience. **But when that founder exits, you
> need to replace those work streams with singular individuals.**"
> — `In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Searcher-to-CEO-and-Exit-to-Search-Fund-Investor-Steve-Divitkos.md`

**This is where most first-time buyers overpay:** the SDE they capitalise contains three or four
salaries that aren't being paid.

The same move applied to a live listing: a 33% EBITDA margin at $4.5M meant *"they either don't have a
service manager, or **the owner IS the service manager.** Probably no install manager. No real
marketing."* Verdict: **"Real, maybe. Sustainable, no."**
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/4.5M-HVAC-Business-With-33-Margins-Is-It-a-Smart-Buy.md`]

## 14. Diligence has a hard ceiling — structure for what you can't see

> "Even if you run the world's best due diligence process, I can almost promise you that **within your
> first month of running your company you will learn more about it than in the 12 months of due
> diligence that preceded it.**"
> — `In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`

Culture in particular is ~90% unknowable from outside, because owners restrict access to staff for
legitimate confidentiality reasons. **That is an argument for a bigger VTB and a longer seller
transition — not for more diligence weeks.**

**The exception worth chasing:** where a seller permits pre-close access to employees, take it. One
buyer spent **10–12 hours with the staff before closing** and calls it his single biggest diligence
advantage. [`Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`]

## 15. Expect your investment thesis to be wrong

Divitkos went in planning to **diversify away** from transportation and logistics — cyclical, low
margin. **Within 18 months he fired over 25% of the customer base and focused the entire company on
transportation exclusively.** *"That was a scary decision. That also was probably the best decision we
made over seven years of ownership."* [`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Searcher-to-CEO-and-Exit-to-Search-Fund-Investor-Steve-Divitkos.md`]

And most searchers don't buy in the industries they set out to buy in — because the world contains more
industries than anyone knows, and deep-diving one surfaces better adjacent ones.
[`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/How2Exit-Episode-54-Steve-Divitkos-Mineola-Search-Partners.md`]

**Hold the industry thesis as a set of characteristics, not a list of categories.**

## 16. "You can't do a good deal with a bad person"

The corpus's one fully documented failure: a trucking/fleet-maintenance business bought for ~$300–350K
in cash with no lender involved. Five landmines followed — **customer concentration accepted knowingly;
a seller who was defrauding his own customers; a seller who immediately tried to poach the employees; a
falsified lease** (believed three years, actually month-to-month) that got them evicted; and no bank
diligence to catch any of it. **Total loss, plus three and a half years and a working partnership with
his twin brother.** [`Acquiring-Minds/How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses.md`]

> "**I paid for an education I didn't know I was getting.**"

By his third deal: *"You can't do a good deal with a bad person. This is a great person, and we got to
do a great deal with a great person."* **Seller character predicted business quality in both
directions — and it's assessable long before the financials are.**

## 17. A lender in the deal is free diligence

*"The scariest deals I come across are when people don't have the need for a bank loan at all."* His
case: a buyer used home equity to write an **$800,000** cheque for a franchise resale. **No banker ever
looked at it as a business deal.** Three years later Barnett valued it at **$300–350K** — *"it wasn't
worth $800,000 then."* [`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`]

**The honest counterpoint:** one buyer found diligence *smoother* without a bank, because he could have
face-to-face conversations about problems as they arose.
[`Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`]

**Both are true. A lender is free diligence *and* an obstacle. Skip it only if you will genuinely do
that work yourself.**

## 18. Unsophisticated advisors are a problem, not an opportunity

> "There's a very common misconception that having an unsophisticated buyer and/or seller is
> unambiguously a good thing. **That's actually wrong.** Unsophisticated brokers might list a company
> worth two times revenue for ten times revenue — that makes the deal very hard to get done.
> **Unsophisticated legal counsel takes a purchase agreement that should take a month and turns it into
> eight**, because they're not familiar with market terms."
> — `In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Analyzing-Software-Businesses-for-Sale-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`

**Barnett's three kinds of broker** [`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`]:
- **The shopkeeper** — gives the seller whatever price they ask for. This is how a 2×-revenue listing reaches you.
- **The dentist** — tells the seller what the business is actually worth and why. *The one you want on the other side.*
- **The clown** — *"an incompetent person who doesn't really know anything, but speaks with a great deal of conviction... **these are the ones who can really mess things up.**"*

**Practical response:** offer to pay for the seller's professional advice. Two buyers in this corpus
ended up doing exactly that.

## 19. Get the operating line before you close — you cannot get it after

**The most under-warned risk in the corpus.** A buyer who doubled revenue in 18 months found that three
correct-looking decisions combined into a year-long problem: **his lender offered no line of credit;
working capital was sized off the *seller's* historical growth (he modelled 10%, delivered ~100%); and
the asset purchase created a new entity with no credit history.**

He approached **~30 banks**, with visible positive cash flow, existing relationships and an offer to
personally guarantee. All refused.

> "**If you don't get that line of credit as part of your [acquisition financing] package, your ability
> to get it afterwards is essentially zero** for a year or two or three."
> — `Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`

The buyer ended up **personally buying a truck and lending it to the business as a shareholder loan**,
and deferring productive equipment purchases. And it's universal, not bad luck: *"What Adrian
experienced is 100% the normal experience. **I haven't met a newly acquired business owner that has not
faced those challenges.**"*

⚠️ **This is worse in Canada**, where CSBFP funds assets only and provides no operating line. **Negotiate
the acquisition debt, the operating line and the equipment facility as one package, before closing —
and size them off your growth plan, not the seller's history.**

## 20. Decide up front: one business held long, or a programme of small ones

The two structures are incompatible.

**Maximum leverage suits a single long hold.** But: *"If you start with a 90% [LTV] loan, **you may run
out of runway really, really quickly**, because you want to do a lot of deals. We've done 15 deals in
two years — that wouldn't have been possible."*
[`Acquiring-Minds/How-to-Acquire-25-Franchise-Units-in-2.5-Years.md`]

Their alternative: raise modest equity, buy the first deal **unlevered**, learn the business, then add
debt **retroactively** to fund the next. It also lets you do deals a bank would refuse — *"that was not
an acquisition an SBA lender would have financed. The stores have been a home run."*

**And consolidation beats accumulation.** Nine acquisitions became five payrolls, five accounting files
and ten bank accounts: *"If I looked at a competitor my same exact size, **I would be a worse operator
than them, because they are more focused.**"* Corroborated: **Chenmark bought ~14 companies in two or
three years, then spent the next two or three figuring out who they were.**
[`Owned-and-Operated/Grow-and-Operate-a-Holding-Company-w-John-Wilson-MI320.md`]

# 2. Valuation Cheat-Sheet

## 2.1 Multiples actually observed, by industry

**Every figure cited. Where sources disagree, both are shown — the disagreement is usually about size,
recurring revenue, or owner-dependence.**

### Home services (HVAC / plumbing / electrical)

| Multiple | Context | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **50–60% of revenue** | John Wilson's own buying rule. Has paid 1× revenue **once**, for an asset-heavy business | `Acquisitions-Anonymous/4.5M-HVAC-Business-With-33-Margins-Is-It-a-Smart-Buy.md` |
| **>1× revenue** | *"If you're paying more than one times revenue for a company this small in plumbing/HVAC, **I think you lost the plot**"* | *same* |
| **6× SDE** | What he sees buyers actually bidding on "dog-shit companies" — *"you should have gone for a two"* | `Owned-and-Operated/Buy-vs.-Build-Start-or-Acquire-Your-First-Business-220.md` |
| **~3× net** | The realistic exit: *"to sell for $1 million is $300,000 of net at a three times. That's a good multiple. **And it's going to take him a decade to get there**"* | *same* |
| **4–5× EBITDA** | Expected trade for a $12M-revenue multi-service business | `Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md` |

**Wilson's reasoning for pricing on revenue rather than earnings is worth understanding:**
> "**I don't care what your EBITDA was.** You have a machine that produces X amount of revenue at X
> gross margin — that's the thing I care about."

Because he replaces the cost structure anyway. **What he actually asks for: phone calls per day, number
of Google Business Profiles, number of reviews, number of active accounts.** *"If you get 50 phone calls
a day, I can do something with this. If you get three, I'm going to tap out."*

### Janitorial / cleaning

| Multiple | Context | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **~1× (small tuck-ins)** | *"As long as I can buy this at a 1x multiple and on seller financing, this could be a deal"* — on $300–500K-revenue targets | `Acquiring-Minds/Stacking-Small-Acquisitions-to-5m-in-Revenue-cleaning-Boyden-Furfaro.md` |
| **4× SDE tested and rejected** | $213K SDE → $852K price → $122K/yr debt service → ~$90K left. **Conclusion: it doesn't trade at 4×** | `Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md` |
| **~3× SDE (implied fair)** | $1.4M revenue at 15% net ≈ $210K SDE ≈ **$600–700K** — against an owner who believed it was worth **$3–4M** | `UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month-in-the-Cleaning-Business.md` |

### Landscaping

**No clean multiple is disclosed, but the value *drivers* are ranked explicitly**
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth-Acquisitions-and-Selling.md`]:

> **Fertilization (70–80% margin) → irrigation → maintenance/mowing (10–15%) → installation last.**
> *"You almost never see a hardscape installation company being bought out."*

Acquisition prices observed: **three landscaping companies at $500–800K revenue each**
[`Acquiring-Minds/Lessons-from-a-Founder-Who-Then-Started-Acquiring-landscaping.md`], one bought where
**$120,000 of street-value assets** underpinned a "few hundred grand" price — *"worst case I lose half
the customers and I've got $120 grand worth of assets."*

### Pest control

**Valued on monthly recurring revenue, not trucks or gross revenue** — stated by an operator, unprompted
[`Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`].
Benchmarks to test any claimed valuation against
[`Pest-control-operators/How-to-Start-a-Pest-Control-Business-450k-per-year.md`]:
- **$400–500 revenue per residential customer per year** (median $418)
- **~250 services per quarter per truck** is the honest ceiling
- **~10% median net margin** at scale; 25–50% for a stabilised owner-operator *who is doing the work*

## 2.2 The five valuation methods that actually get used

1. **Multiple of SDE** — small businesses. **2–3× at the smaller end.** [`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`]
2. **Multiple of EBITDA** — larger businesses, where a management team exists.
3. **Percentage of revenue** — Wilson's method, used when you intend to replace the cost structure.
4. **Debt-service reverse-engineering** — *the one that protects you.* Work out the maximum debt the cash flow supports at a **1.25–1.5× DSCR**, add your equity, and that is your ceiling. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]
5. **Asset floor** — price the trucks and equipment at street/auction value and see how much of the purchase price is genuinely at risk. [`Acquiring-Minds/Lessons-from-a-Founder-Who-Then-Started-Acquiring-landscaping.md`]

**Use 4 and 5 as guardrails on whatever 1–3 produce.**

## 2.3 Adjustments almost everyone forgets

| Adjustment | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital replacement reserve** | SDE/EBITDA exclude depreciation. Trucks, mowers, plows and sprayers wear out | `David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md` |
| **The founder's 3–4 unpaid roles** | The SDE contains salaries that aren't being paid | `In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Searcher-to-CEO-and-Exit-to-Search-Fund-Investor-Steve-Divitkos.md` |
| **Owner's own labour, in owner-operator businesses** | Subtract a fully-burdened technician's wage for the routes the owner drives | `Pest-control-operators/How-much-can-you-make-as-a-pest-control-owner-operator.md` |
| **Marketing spend cut before sale** | Inflates earnings *and* damages the pipeline you're buying. Check spend as % of revenue over 3 years | `UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future.md` |
| **Working capital** | *"Plus inventory — we never like that. There should be working capital left in the business and it should be included in the price"* | `Acquisitions-Anonymous/Money-counting-machines-sold-on-Amazon-Acquisitions-Anonymous-252.md` |
| **10–20% of purchase price as an operating reserve** | *"That's your uh-oh money"* — held **on top of** the purchase price | `David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-that-Makes-Sellers-Love-You.md` |
| **Owner-occupied premises rent** | Rent paid by the business to the owner personally is an add-back **and often a purchase opportunity** | `Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md` |

## 2.4 Reading a listing: seven tells

1. **Reconcile "cash flow" against EBITDA against SDE.** One listing showed **$925K cash flow against $524K EBITDA** on a $1.6M asking price. *"I'd definitely like to understand the delta between those."* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Acquisitions-Anonymous-Ep-41.md`]
2. **Read the listing against itself.** One claimed 80% of sales in "Australia and the US," then said sales peak October–March — *"otherwise it would be counterbalanced by American summer. **So most of the sales are in Australia.**"* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/A-1M-Pest-Control-Business-Ecommerce-Buzz-or-Bust-AA-297.md`]
3. **A cheap multiple is a question, not a discount.** At 1.6× cash flow: *"There is a reason this thing is priced under two times... **this will start feeling like 10 times cash flow inside of a year.**"* [*same*]
4. **"SBA pre-qualified" (or "bankable") means nothing.** From an actual lender: *"It's just marketing. It didn't go to SBA, and probably didn't even go to a credit officer... **there's a big separation of church and state inside banks between the sales side and the credit side.**"* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Money-counting-machines-sold-on-Amazon-Acquisitions-Anonymous-252.md`]
5. **"Owner would like to retire but is willing to stay on in some capacity."** *"That's like 'owner likes pizza but also hates cheese.' **He's hedging so it doesn't scare you away.**"* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`]
6. **"Customary and reasonable transition assistance"** is entirely negotiable — *"could be two weeks or six months, you don't really know."* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]
7. **"This is the best it's ever going to look."** *"As the deal progresses it only gets worse. **You don't find these gold nuggets lying around.**"* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/2M-of-EBITDA-and-50-margins-in-disaster-remediation-AA-194.md`]

## 2.5 Handling a growth spike

**The structural answer, not an argument:**
> "**You value it based on what it looked like before**, and then you have some kind of **extra
> forgivable seller note** wrapped up in the so-called new value. **Because if the new value isn't real
> and doesn't stick around, you can't be paying for it.**"
>
> And to the seller: *"If you want to be paid on these results, we need another two fiscal years. **Once
> we've got three years at the new level**, we can argue the business has materially changed."*
>
> **Why three:** *"Somebody can manipulate one year with accounting tricks, especially with cash-basis
> accounting — pay for things in December that you'll sell in January. **After three years it becomes
> much harder for those games to stick.**"*
> — `David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`

## 2.6 🇨🇦 The free Canadian benchmark nobody uses

> "In the States they've got the RMA data... **And the Canadian data is just on a public website — you
> can just look it up.**" Industry performance **divided into quartiles, based on tax return
> information.** — `David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-What-You-Need-to-Know-about-Buying-a-Business-feat.-David-C.-Barnett.md`

**The resource: Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada's *Financial Performance Data*
tool**, built from CRA corporate filings and searchable by **NAICS code, province and revenue band.**

Why it matters, in his worked example: in one industry, **75% of businesses had revenue under $1.2M and
half were under $480,000.** *"If you looked that up before getting into that industry, it would give you
great pause — you'd realise **half the actors in this industry really aren't making any money.**"*

**Pull the sector benchmark before you spend a day on diligence.** It is the only free, Canadian,
tax-return-derived comparison set available, and it answers: *is this business above or below median,
and what does the top quartile actually earn?*

# 3. The Due Diligence Checklist

Structured on the four headings from `Due-diligence/Small-Business-Due-Diligence-Checklist-What-Banks-Won-t-Tell-You.md`,
with additions from across the corpus. **⚠️ marks items where the Canadian answer differs from the US
source.**

## 3.1 Financial verification

- [ ] **Get three years of tax returns AND financial statements, and reconcile them line by line.** They are different documents with different purposes — returns are built to minimise tax, statements to look healthy. **The classic tell: losses on the return, profits on the statements.** *Someone is lying to someone.*
- [ ] **Reconcile "cash flow" vs EBITDA vs SDE.** Any large unexplained gap is the first thing to chase.
- [ ] **Interrogate every add-back.** One case: consistently high "miscellaneous expenses" that conveniently offset dips in profitability concealed personal travel booked as business development and payments to family members for services never rendered. *Those are add-backs a seller will want credited to SDE.*
- [ ] **Add back owner-occupied rent** — and ask whether the real estate is available. [`Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`]
- [ ] **The "Friday afternoon test"** — stress-test cash flow at the moment payroll and suppliers are paid, not at month-end when the balance looks tidy.
- [ ] **Marketing spend as % of revenue, three years.** A cut in the year before sale inflates earnings and starves your pipeline.
- [ ] **Aged receivables listing**, and the collections history behind it.
- [ ] **Capital replacement schedule:** age, hours and remaining life of every vehicle and major machine, then the annualised replacement cost. **Subtract it from SDE.**
- [ ] 🇨🇦 **Pull the ISED Financial Performance Data benchmark** for the NAICS code and province. Where does this business sit against its quartiles?

## 3.2 Legal and compliance

- [ ] **⚠️ Worker classification.** In Canada the CRA applies a control/integration/tools/chance-of-profit test. **Reclassification brings back unremitted CPP and EI (both portions), interest, penalties, plus WSIB premiums and vacation/stat pay — and can attach to the business and to directors.** Get each contractor's business number, GST/HST registration, WSIB clearance certificate and own insurance. Ask whether any CPT1 ruling was ever sought.
- [ ] **⚠️ CRA arrears and liens.** Payroll source deductions and GST/HST are **held in trust**; unremitted amounts can follow the business and, in some cases, directors personally. **Get a CRA statement of account.**
- [ ] **⚠️ WSIB / WCB clearance certificate** — and the **experience rating and claims history**, not just the current premium. One US operator saw a single injury claim raise his rate for **three years** and visibly cost him five points of net margin. **The experience rating follows the business.** [`UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month-in-the-Cleaning-Business.md`]
- [ ] **⚠️ Employment liabilities travel with the business — including in an asset sale.** Most Canadian jurisdictions deem continuity of employment on a sale; accrued service, notice and severance follow the employees to you. **Calculate the aggregate termination/severance exposure by tenure.** [`David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`]
- [ ] **⚠️ Union certification status and any collective agreement.** Provincial successor-employer rules bind a purchaser — often in an asset sale too. One US deal survived only because attrition dropped the bargaining unit below the threshold; that route is far harder in Canada.
- [ ] **Every employment agreement — check for assignment/transferability clauses.** One buyer found an inherited salesman's agreement had **no transferability clause and simply did not survive the sale.** [*same*]
- [ ] **⚠️ Licences and permits: who holds each one, is it personal or corporate, does it survive a change of control, and how long does re-qualification take?** In Canada: pesticide applicator classes (Ontario/Quebec), TSSA gas fitting, ODP refrigerant handling, water-well contractor licensing (O. Reg. 903). **A solo owner's licence generally does not transfer. This is a closing condition, not a footnote.**
- [ ] **Litigation** — past, present and threatened. *One buyer's third day on site, the sellers were served papers for a four-year-old car accident.* [`Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`]
- [ ] **IP ownership** — logos, customer lists, proprietary methods owned by the corporation, not the departing owner personally.
- [ ] **⚠️ Verify the lease with the landlord directly, in writing.** Term, renewal rights, assignment on change of control, and the landlord's intentions for the property. **A falsified lease destroyed one business in this corpus.**
- [ ] 🇨🇦 **Temporary foreign worker compliance** where crews include SAWP/TFW-stream workers — LMIA status, housing and pay obligations.

## 3.3 Operations

- [ ] **Customer concentration.** Name the top customers and their revenue share. **The operator rule: over 15–20% of revenue and it's a liability.** *"When you take one client that's 30, 50, 80% of your business, **they own your business as much or more than you do — but you're on the hook for all the risk.**"* [`Cleaning-operators/Losing-a-Major-Client-How-to-Rebuild-Your-Cleaning-Company.md`]
- [ ] **Revenue by client, three years.**
- [ ] **Recurring vs reoccurring vs one-time revenue split, in dollars, by month, 36 months.**
- [ ] **Contract file:** are they written? assignable on change of control? auto-renewing? auto-billed? **When did each client last sign?**
- [ ] **Churn: every account lost in 36 months, with reasons.** Especially in cleaning, where **clients leave silently rather than complain.**
- [ ] **Vendor contracts under change of control** — same question as permits.
- [ ] **⚠️ Subcontractor agreements with nationals or brokers** — hunt for **pay-when-paid**, reporting-forfeiture, all-damages, and first-failure-voids-everything clauses. **Revenue under those terms should not be valued at face.** [`Landscaping-snow-operators/Snow-Contracts-EXPOSED-Read-THIS-Before-You-Sign.md`]
- [ ] **Re-price every contract from scratch.** A signed contract proves nothing about whether it was priced correctly — one operator's first bid omitted labour taxes, supplies and any profit line, and the client signed instantly. [`Cleaning-operators/Filthy-Rich-Cleaners-E21-Breaking-into-Commercial-Cleaning-Cold-Calls-to-7-Figures.md`]
- [ ] **Map the customer addresses.** Route density is the difference between profit and loss in pest control, landscaping and janitorial alike.
- [ ] **The parking lot test** — visit early morning, late evening, weekends.
- [ ] **Employee interviews** where permitted (take employment law advice first). Listen for gaps between what staff say and what the seller said. **Where a seller allows pre-close access, this is the highest-value diligence available.**
- [ ] **Recruiting funnel numbers:** applications, interviews, offers, no-shows, 30-day survival.
- [ ] **Walk the sites and ask the crews what they're short of.**

## 3.4 Market position and the owner

- [ ] **Review count and rating**, and whether the profile transfers (⚠️ not in a franchise).
- [ ] **Reputation check:** search local Facebook groups and Nextdoor equivalents for unprompted recommendations. One buyer identified his best acquisition target this way. [`Acquiring-Minds/How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses.md`]
- [ ] **Where do the leads come from?** *Systematised demand (SEO, tracked ads, contracts) is transferable and worth a full multiple. Owner-relationship demand needs contingent consideration.* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/2M-of-EBITDA-and-50-margins-in-disaster-remediation-AA-194.md`]
- [ ] **Mission-critical or discretionary?** *"You want a business that sells something very time-consuming, very disruptive, or very expensive to get rid of."* [`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Analyzing-Software-Businesses-for-Sale-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`]
- [ ] **Are new sales and customer relationships proprietary to the owner or a couple of staff?** *"If that's the case there's huge key-man risk."* [*same*]
- [ ] **"When did you last take two consecutive weeks off, and what broke?"** [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth-Acquisitions-and-Selling.md`]
- [ ] **Where is the owner on the ladder — field → office → estimator → CEO?** *"If they're in any of those first three phases, their company's value is much, much lower."* [*same*]
- [ ] **Age and retirement intentions of every key employee.** In a three-person business, one retirement is a third of the workforce. [`Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`]
- [ ] **"What do you personally do that nobody else can?"** Then price the replacement.
- [ ] **"How many other businesses do you own, and how many have you sold?"** — the serial-flipper screen. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/A-1M-Pest-Control-Business-Ecommerce-Buzz-or-Bust-AA-297.md`]

## 3.5 Deal-breakers

- **The "too good to be true" pattern** — suspiciously consistent profits, or margins that defy industry norms. **Reverse-engineer the org chart from the margin: who isn't being paid?**
- **Documentation gaps.** Inability or unwillingness to produce core documents, or promises to send them "later."
- **Owner behaviour:** overly eager, pushing a fast close, evasive or inconsistent.
- **A seller who resists lender diligence.**
- **A margin that has no room for a line every competitor obviously spends on** (marketing, replacement capex, payroll burden).
- **A price that fails the DSCR test** — where the return depends on growth you haven't yet proven you can deliver.

> "**It's better to miss out on a good deal than to get stuck with a bad one.**"
> — `Due-diligence/Small-Business-Due-Diligence-Checklist-What-Banks-Won-t-Tell-You.md`
>
> "**If you can't walk away from any deal out there, then the seller has all the power.**"
> — `UpFlip/Extreme-Poverty-to-6M-Year-Empire...-Here-s-How.md`

# 4. 🇨🇦 The Canadian Deal-Structure Guide

**This is the section that corrects every US video in the corpus.** If you read only one part of this
document, read this one.

## 4.1 Why Canadian deals are structured differently

**The single structural fact:**

> "All of that goes out the window when you're talking about this program, because **Industry Canada is
> not really concerned at all about what the business is being sold for. What they're concerned about is
> what *stuff* is being financed.**"
> — `Canadian-acquisition-finance/Financing-a-Business-Purchase-in-Canada.md`

**The US SBA lends against the business. The CSBFP lends against assets.** That one difference cascades
through everything: it is why Canadian asset-light service deals need large vendor take-backs, why
Canadian buyers need more equity, and why most US acquisition content misleads Canadian buyers.

## 4.2 CSBFP — the Canada Small Business Financing Program

*Source: `Canadian-acquisition-finance/Financing-a-Business-Purchase-in-Canada.md` (David C Barnett, Canadian).
**Verify all figures with a lender before relying on them — program terms change.***

**How it works:** Industry Canada **guarantees 85%** of the qualifying loan. You apply to a bank, credit
union or participating lease-financing company, and *they* apply for the guarantee.

| Element | Terms |
|---|---|
| **Eligibility cap** | Businesses with **revenue above $10M/year do not qualify** |
| **Total program limit** | **$1.15M**, split as below |
| — Real estate used by the business | up to **$1,000,000**, **90% of purchase financeable** |
| — Equipment, machinery, leasehold improvements, fit-ups | up to **$500,000** (includes flooring, wall coverings, demising walls in a *rented* location) |
| — **Intangibles and working capital** (added 2022) | **$150,000** — goodwill, franchise fees, inventory, operating expenses. Term loan **or guaranteed line of credit** |
| **Amortisation** | **Up to 25 years on real estate; up to 10 years on everything else** |
| **Assumable** | **Yes** — if a target has an existing CSBFP loan and you qualify, the lender can switch the names and the guarantee continues |
| **Personal guarantee cap** | **25% of the loan** — a genuine Canadian advantage with no US equivalent |
| **Lender's own equity requirement, on top** | Expect to need roughly **25% of the total project** as your own money or outside collateral |

**⚠️ The structural problem for a service-business buyer, stated plainly:**
> "It does not offer a whole lot of financing for someone buying a business that has very little in the
> way of tangible stuff. **Your service business that doesn't have these assets — you're not going to be
> able to access very much out of this program except for that $150,000 allowance.**"
>
> "**Traditionally in Canada, people that have businesses that are asset light have come to expect that
> they are going to have to finance more of the transaction through seller financing.**"

**A janitorial or pest control company is exactly this case: a truck, some floor machines, and goodwill.
Plan for a large VTB, not a large CSBFP loan.**

**How bankers actually assemble a deal — "the jigsaw":** they jam as much as possible into the
guaranteed envelope, then write non-guaranteed loans alongside. A real example: one guaranteed loan for
real estate, a second guaranteed loan for equipment under $500K, and **a third conventional loan for the
business itself secured by personal guarantee or a lien on the buyer's house.** *This is why stories
circulate of Canadians "getting the whole thing financed" — it was three loans, only two of them CSBFP.*

**Appraisals you will need:**
- A **certified machinery and equipment appraisal**, using **"fair market value in continued use"** — deliberately the definition that produces the highest number.
- To use the $150K against goodwill, a **second valuation** ("most probable selling price") establishing going-concern value.
- **Watch the denominator when comparing offers:** banks lend a percentage of *fair market value*; leasing companies a percentage of *orderly or forced liquidation value*. **"75% from the bank" can beat "90% from the leasing company."**

**Bank policy sits on top of the program and varies enormously.** *Only two banks in Canada will finance
restaurant acquisitions under it.* **Shop lenders — and "the banker makes all the difference."**

**The real-estate insight worth acting on:** because CSBFP funds real property at up to 90% over 25
years versus 10 years for everything else, **a deal that includes the premises can support materially
more debt and a longer amortisation than one that doesn't.** One US buyer used exactly this logic — the
same monthly debt service, but the $90,000 of rent stayed inside the business, *"a deal I wouldn't have
done without the real estate."* [`Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`]
**When a Canadian seller owns the building, model the deal both ways.**

## 4.3 The VTB (Vendor Take-Back) — the Canadian centrepiece

*Source: `David-C-Barnett/Seller-Financing-To-Buy-A-Business-Vendor-Notes-in-M-A-Explained.md` —
Canadian content, Canadian banks named.*

| Parameter | Typical |
|---|---|
| **Size** | **10–25% of purchase price.** Example: $1M deal = $200K cash equity + $600K senior bank + **$200K VTB** |
| **Interest** | **3–5%** for a low-risk buyer (strategic, competitor, wealthy investor); **5–8%** for thin-equity or higher-risk. *"On average 5–6%, as high as 8, as low as 3"* |
| **Payment frequency** | **Usually annual**, sometimes quarterly. **Monthly amortisation is rare** — the point is breathing room while you learn the business |
| **Amortisation** | **1–3 years non-amortising**, most commonly **2 years interest-only then 2–3 years amortising** |
| **Bank equity requirement** | **20–40% of deal value — and a deferred VTB counts toward it** |
| **Personal guarantees** | Appear in *"only 10% of our deals"* |

**When VTBs go far above 25%:**
- **Industry risk** — *"banks in Canada aren't really favourable of those industries."* One salon/spa seller could have his price only at a **40% VTB**, and accepted.
- **Seller/business risk** — environmental issues, key-owner transition risk. *"I've seen situations where VTBs are 60, 70, 80% of the purchase price."*
- **Seller preference for yield** — one seller took a **100% VTB, no cash down, 8% for five years**, because his bond portfolio returned 4–5%.

### ⭐ The most important structural point in this entire document

**A properly structured VTB counts as equity to the bank.**

> "If structured **interest-only for 2–3 years (3 to be safe) and postponed**, the bank treats it as
> patient capital sitting behind them — **which lets you borrow more *and* pay more.**"
> — `David-C-Barnett/Seller-Financing-To-Buy-A-Business-Vendor-Notes-in-M-A-Explained.md`

The same mechanic, from a US practitioner: *"That $1.8M seller note — **lenders look at that as equity
because it's behind everything else.** A $1.8M seller's note is like a $1.8M investment to the bank."*
[`David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md`]

**The price of that treatment is a standstill/postponement agreement** — the seller agrees the bank can
defer even the *interest* if the business underperforms. **Get the postponement language right or the
benefit evaporates.**

Worked outcome: **VTB at 30% of deal, true cash equity only 10%, producing ~40% annual returns on the
cash actually invested.**

### The five reasons a buyer wants a VTB

1. **Finance the gap** without more equity.
2. **Deferred equity** (above) — the big one.
3. **Skin in the game.** *"If I give you all cash you can go off into the sunset and don't really care if I succeed. But if I'm still owed $200,000, **I want to be damn sure I get repaid** — so I'll provide the right training and stay available."*
4. **Offset post-closing purchase-price adjustments** — the ~90-day working capital true-up nets against the note rather than moving cash.
5. **A de facto escrow for disputes** — *"I thought I was buying a million dollar business when in reality it was worth a hundred thousand"* — you set off against the note instead of suing for money already gone.

### What the seller will ask for (know what you're conceding)

- **General Security Agreement (GSA)** registered against the corporation; a **second mortgage** if real estate is in the deal
- **Pledge of shares**, second charge behind the bank — *"the very first thing a bank does when a loan goes bad is ask the seller to take this over and turn it around"*
- **Personal guarantee** (rare — 10%)
- **A leverage covenant** — debt/EBITDA not to exceed 3–4×, breach triggering acceleration or a rate step-up. *He advises against DSCR covenants as over-complicating*
- **Standstill / postponement agreement** — the price of equity treatment

### Four VTB structures

1. **Plain vanilla** — $500K, 5% annual, 5-year term, 24 months interest-only, then amortising ~$166,667/yr over years 3–5.
2. **Price-adjustment mechanism** — the note flexes with performance. Worked example: $500K note, $100K/yr amortisation, $3M sales target, **20% share of variance.** Sales of $3.1M → seller gets $120K that year; $2.8M → $60K; $2.7M → $40K. *"A very fair way to negotiate uncertainty in the future."*
3. **PIK (paid-in-kind)** — no cash interest; it capitalises and compounds ($500K → $525K → $551K → $579K). Preserves buyer cash; high risk to the seller.
4. **Convertible VTB** — where the seller rolls equity, a pre-agreed formula lets them convert that stake into a secured note and exit cleanly.

### The self-extinguishing note — the answer to what you can't diligence

One buyer **tied his seller note to revenue retention: if revenue dropped past a defined point, the note
goes away.** He had modelled the downside explicitly — a $1.2M firm falling to $500K, note extinguished,
business still saleable as a shell. *"That would suck, it would take a long time to pay back, but I
could do it — it's not going to totally ruin our lives."*
[`David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md`]

**Since Divitkos establishes that culture and much else is ~90% unknowable pre-close, a retention-linked
VTB is the correct structural response** — not more diligence weeks.

### ⚠️ Two terminology traps

1. **A VTB is not an earnout.** A VTB/seller note is **fixed debt owed regardless of performance**; an earnout is **contingent consideration that may never be paid.** One US video in this corpus uses the terms interchangeably throughout. **The tax treatment, the bank's willingness to count it as equity, and the seller's security position all differ.** [`David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-that-Makes-Sellers-Love-You.md`]
2. **⚠️ A tax claim to distrust.** One Canadian source states that VTB interest *"is taxed at the capital gains rate, not the income tax rate."* **This is doubtful — interest income is normally fully taxable in Canada, not a capital gain. Confirm with a Canadian tax advisor before using it in a negotiation.**

### Earnout design, if you do use one

**Revenue-based milestones favour the seller** (they get paid whenever revenue clears the bar,
regardless of your profitability). **Profit-based favour the buyer — and are gameable**, which the
source admits: *"you can just spend money every month to make sure you're always at $4,999 in profit."*
He calls doing that *"a huge no-no,"* and notes most sellers insist on revenue for exactly this reason.
The legitimate case for profit-based is protection from exogenous shocks.
[`David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-that-Makes-Sellers-Love-You.md`]

**Better than an earnout in most cases: rolled equity.** One seller rolled a significant stake instead
of taking all cash — *"if I can roll a significant amount, that allows me to get **that second bite at
the apple**"* — which resolved a valuation gap without the gameable mechanics.
[`Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`]

## 4.4 🇨🇦 Share sale vs asset sale in Canada

**Expect to be asked to buy shares. That is the opposite of what you want, and there is a specific
Canadian reason for it.**

### Why the Canadian seller wants a share sale

**The Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption (LCGE): $1.25 million** on qualified small business corporation
(QSBC) shares. [`Canadian-tax/ACCOUNTANT-EXPLAINS-Canada-s-Capital-Gains-Tax.md`]

To qualify as a QSBC the corporation must generally be a CCPC with **≥90% active business assets at
sale** and **≥50% for the 24 months prior.** A seller with excess cash, rental property or investments
sitting in the opco is **not** QSBC-pure — and will discover this mid-deal and either demand a higher
price or restructure, costing months.

⚠️ **A currency warning on that source:** the video also describes a proposed increase in the capital
gains inclusion rate to 66.67% and a "Canadian Entrepreneurs' Incentive." **Both were subsequently
deferred and then abandoned. Do not quote those numbers.** The **$1.25M LCGE did survive** and is the
figure to plan around. **Verify current status with a CPA.**

### Why you want an asset sale

- **A stepped-up depreciable base** on the assets you buy.
- **You don't inherit the corporation's history** — *"some of these businesses have been around 10, 15, 20, 30 years. **You don't know what's behind some closed doors. Everyone has some cobwebs.**"* The live example: **third day on site, the sellers were served papers for a four-year-old car accident.** *"Luckily we did assets."* [`Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`]

### ⚠️ But three US assumptions do NOT hold in Canada

1. **An asset sale does not shed employment liabilities.** Most Canadian jurisdictions deem continuity of employment on a sale of a business — **including an asset sale.** Accrued service, notice and severance travel to you. [`David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`] Barnett states it directly: *"**Where I live, labour issues follow assets, not companies.** So even in an asset sale, certain labour things can actually follow to the new owner."*
2. **An asset sale may not shed WSIB/WCB exposure**, and union successor rights can bind a purchaser in an asset sale too.
3. **There is no Canadian equivalent of the US s.338(h)(10) election** — do not plan around it. [`Valuation-craft/Structuring-a-Business-Sale-to-Minimize-Taxes-Asset-vs.-Stock-Sales.md`]

**Also discard from US sources:** s.1245 recapture (Canada: **recapture of CCA**), 15-year goodwill amortisation (Canada: **Class 14.1**), and all state-level examples.

### The hidden cost of an asset sale nobody warns about

**An asset purchase creates a new entity with no credit history** — which is precisely what left one
buyer unable to obtain an operating line or reasonable equipment financing from ~30 banks. **A share
purchase preserves that history.** [`Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`]

### The practical Canadian resolution

**You will probably end up in a share deal, because the LCGE gives the seller a powerful reason to
insist and gives you something to trade for.** Handle it by:
- **Quantifying the structural tax delta first.** On an **asset-light service business the delta is often trivial** — one worked US example showed **$17,000 on a $10M deal** — so concede structure loudly and take payment terms, VTB size, working-capital peg or non-compete in exchange. On an asset-heavy business the same example showed a **$1.19M swing**, and you should expect a real fight. [`Valuation-craft/Structuring-a-Business-Sale-to-Minimize-Taxes-Asset-vs.-Stock-Sales.md`]
- **Pricing the inherited liabilities** — CRA payroll/GST-HST arrears, WSIB assessments, employment claims, litigation — and backing them with **an indemnity plus holdback or VTB offset.**
- **Getting the holdco structure right before you buy.** In Canada the standard is a **holdco corporation owning opco shares**, enabling the **s.85 rollover**, intercorporate dividends, creditor protection and **multiplying LCGE access via a family trust.** **Retrofitting this later triggers tax.** [`Owned-and-Operated/Grow-and-Operate-a-Holding-Company-w-John-Wilson-MI320.md`]

### One more Canadian seller lever worth knowing

A vendor taking back a VTB may be able to use the **capital gains reserve to spread the gain over up to
five years** (generally minimum 20% recognised annually). **That is a genuine benefit to raise with a
reluctant vendor** — and it is exactly the kind of non-price motivation that wins deals (see Lesson 3).
**Verify with a CPA.**

## 4.5 🇨🇦 Assembling the Canadian capital stack

**Do all of this before closing:**

1. **Model debt capacity first.** A rule of thumb from a US lender: **~3.75× adjusted EBITDA** on a 10-year amortisation. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`] Apply your own DSCR test at **1.25–1.5×**.
2. **Shop at least four or five lenders**, and keep the losers warm. Divitkos had **term sheets from six banks**; two weeks before closing his chosen bank came back from credit committee having **completely changed the terms** — and the bank he'd rejected now had the better deal. [`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Searcher-to-CEO-and-Exit-to-Search-Fund-Investor-Steve-Divitkos.md`] *"Always talk to more than one bank."* [`David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`]
3. **Negotiate the operating line, the equipment facility and the acquisition debt as one package** — sized off *your* growth plan, not the seller's history. **You cannot get the line afterwards.** (Lesson 19.)
4. **Consider BDC** for the layer above CSBFP — subordinate and growth financing, working capital, equipment. *Verify current products directly.*
5. **Size the VTB to fill the gap**, structured interest-only for 2–3 years with a proper postponement so it counts as equity.
6. **Hold 10–20% of the purchase price in cash as an operating reserve, on top of everything above.**
7. **Treat any "bankable" or "CSBFP-eligible" claim in a listing as marketing** — get a credit-side view on *your* structure with *your* equity. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Money-counting-machines-sold-on-Amazon-Acquisitions-Anonymous-252.md`]

**Every one of the deals in this corpus that worked used vendor financing.** *"Every one of our deals
has had seller financing."* [`Acquiring-Minds/How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses.md`]
The most extreme example: **seven acquisitions to just under $5M revenue on roughly $100,000 of the
buyers' own cash**, six of the seven with no bank at all — notes from 3 to 10 years, several at 0%
interest. [`Acquiring-Minds/Stacking-Small-Acquisitions-to-5m-in-Revenue-cleaning-Boyden-Furfaro.md`]

# 5. Glossary

Terms as they are actually used by the operators, brokers and lenders in this corpus — not
dictionary definitions. Where a term means something different in Canada than in the US sources,
that difference is marked 🇨🇦.

## 5.1 The money words

**SDE — Seller's Discretionary Earnings.** Net profit + the owner's salary + the owner's personal
benefits run through the business + one-time and non-recurring costs + interest, tax, depreciation
and amortisation. The standard earnings measure for businesses under roughly $5M of value, because
at that size the owner *is* the management. **The critical point in this corpus: SDE is not your
take-home.** It has three claims on it, in order — debt service first, a buffer second, your
compensation last. [`David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`] If the deal is priced so that SDE barely
covers debt service, you have bought a job with a mortgage attached.

**EBITDA — Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation.** Used instead of SDE once
the business is large enough to carry a real manager, typically above ~$1M of earnings. The
practical difference is one line: **EBITDA is stated after a market-rate manager's salary; SDE is
stated before it.** A seller quoting SDE on a business that needs a $120K general manager is quoting
you a number that is $120K too high for your purposes. Ask which one the multiple is applied to
before you compare any two listings.

**Adjusted EBITDA / normalised earnings.** EBITDA after add-backs. This is the number lenders
actually underwrite to. A rule of thumb heard from a US lender in this corpus: debt capacity of
roughly **3.75× adjusted EBITDA** on a ten-year amortisation.
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`]

**Add-backs.** Expenses added back to profit because a new owner would not incur them. Legitimate:
the owner's above-market salary, a genuinely one-time legal settlement, the owner's personal vehicle
and travel, a family member on payroll who does no work. **Illegitimate but common:** deferred
maintenance dressed as "one-time", owner labour that a buyer would have to pay someone to do,
"marketing we won't need", and equipment replacement treated as non-recurring when it recurs every
few years. The test is simple and worth memorising: *will this cost genuinely disappear the day I
take over, or does it just move?* Every add-back a seller proposes is a claim you should price at
zero until proven.

**Multiple.** The number the earnings figure is multiplied by to get a price. Meaningless without
knowing (a) which earnings figure, (b) what is included — inventory, working capital, real estate —
and (c) whether it is a cash price or a structured one. **A high multiple on a structured deal can
be cheaper than a low multiple in cash.** A cheap multiple is usually cheap for a reason; see
Lesson 6 and the AA 297 warning. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/A-1M-Pest-Control-Business-Ecommerce-Buzz-or-Bust-AA-297.md`]

**Working capital peg / true-up.** The agreed level of working capital the business must have on
closing day. Measured ~60–90 days after closing and settled in cash — or, better, netted against
the VTB. Getting this wrong is the most common way a buyer accidentally pays an extra six figures.

**Enterprise value vs equity value.** Enterprise value is the price of the business's operations,
debt-free and cash-free. Equity value is what actually lands in the seller's pocket after debt is
repaid and cash is swept. Listings quote whichever is larger.

## 5.2 Structure

**VTB — Vendor Take-Back** (Canadian term; the US sources say **seller note** or **seller
financing**). The seller lends part of the purchase price back to the buyer. Typically **10–25% of
price**, **3–5% interest** for a low-risk buyer and **5–8%** for a higher-risk one, usually annual
payments, most commonly **two years interest-only followed by a 2–3 year amortisation**.
[`David-C-Barnett/Seller-Financing-To-Buy-A-Business-Vendor-Notes-in-M-A-Explained.md`] Goes far
above 25% when the industry is one Canadian banks dislike, when there is seller- or transition-risk,
or when the seller simply wants the yield.

**Deferred equity.** A VTB structured interest-only for 2–3 years *and* postponed to the bank, so
the senior lender treats it as patient capital sitting behind them rather than as competing debt.
This is the single most valuable structural trick in the corpus: it lets you **borrow more and pay
more with less of your own cash.** The price you pay for it is the standstill agreement.
[`David-C-Barnett/Seller-Financing-To-Buy-A-Business-Vendor-Notes-in-M-A-Explained.md`]

**Standstill / postponement agreement.** The seller's written agreement that the bank ranks ahead of
them, and that if the business underperforms the bank can defer even the *interest* on the vendor
note. Without it the bank counts your VTB as debt. With it, the bank counts it as equity. **Do not
sign the LOI without knowing whether the seller will postpone.**

**PIK — paid in kind.** Interest that is not paid in cash but capitalised onto the principal.
$500K at 5% becomes $525K, then $551,250, then $578,813. Preserves buyer cash; compounds seller risk.

**Price-adjustment mechanism.** A vendor note whose payments flex with actual performance against an
agreed target — e.g. a $500K note amortising at $100K/year against a $3M sales target, with the
seller taking 20% of the variance. Sales of $3.1M pay the seller $120K that year; $2.7M pays $40K.
The clean way to bridge "I don't believe your revenue will hold."

**Earnout.** Contingent additional purchase price paid only if the business hits agreed targets after
closing. ⚠️ **Not the same as a VTB** — a VTB is a debt you owe regardless; an earnout is money you
only pay if the performance shows up. Some Canadian sources use the words loosely; make the document
say which one you mean, because the tax treatment and the security are different.

**Self-extinguishing note.** A vendor note that shrinks automatically if a defined risk
materialises — most commonly if named customers or key staff leave within a defined window. The
cleanest available answer to customer-concentration and key-person risk.

**Holdback / escrow.** Part of the price parked with a third party pending resolution of a known
risk. A VTB does the same job informally: you set off the claim against the note instead of suing
for money that has already left.

**Asset sale vs share sale.** In an asset sale you buy the equipment, contracts, goodwill and name,
and leave the corporate shell — and its history — with the seller. In a share sale you buy the
company itself, warts included. 🇨🇦 **Buyers want asset; Canadian sellers want share**, because a
share sale is what qualifies for the LCGE. The hidden cost of an asset sale is that you lose the
company's trading history: its bank record, its bonding record, its credit file and — in some
provinces and sectors — its licences and its accumulated experience rating.

**LCGE — Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption.** 🇨🇦 The Canadian seller's reason for insisting on a
share sale: roughly **$1.25M** of capital gain on qualifying small business corporation shares
sheltered from tax, per individual. Understanding this is negotiating leverage — a share sale may be
worth more to the seller than a higher headline price in an asset deal, which you can trade for
better terms. *Confirm current limits and rules with a Canadian tax advisor.*

**QSBC — Qualified Small Business Corporation shares.** 🇨🇦 The test the shares must meet for the
LCGE to apply — broadly, a Canadian-controlled private corporation with substantially all its assets
used in an active business in Canada, held for a period before the sale. Sellers often need
"purification" work done in advance; a seller who has not done it may be *unable* to accept a share
sale on the timeline you want.

**s.85 rollover.** 🇨🇦 A tax-deferred transfer of property into a corporation under section 85 of the
Income Tax Act. Shows up in acquisition structuring when a seller rolls part of their equity into the
buyer's new holdco instead of cashing out. *Accountant territory — flagged here so you recognise the
term when a seller's advisor uses it.*

**Capital gains reserve.** 🇨🇦 The mechanism that lets a seller spread the tax on a gain over up to
five years when the proceeds are received over time. Relevant because it is the reason a well-advised
Canadian seller may actively *want* a multi-year VTB rather than all cash.

**Holdco.** A holding company sitting above the operating company. Used for creditor protection, for
stacking multiple acquisitions, and for tax planning on the way in and out. Several operators in the
corpus run each acquisition as a separate opco under one holdco so a bad one cannot sink the others.

**GSA — General Security Agreement.** 🇨🇦 The registered charge over a corporation's assets that a
lender — or a vendor taking back a note — uses as security. Expect a seller to ask for one, ranked
behind the bank.

**Pledge of shares.** Security over the shares of the company itself, so a defaulting buyer hands the
business back rather than just the assets. Matters because *"the very first thing a bank does when a
loan goes bad is turn around and ask the seller to take this over."*
[`David-C-Barnett/Seller-Financing-To-Buy-A-Business-Vendor-Notes-in-M-A-Explained.md`]

**Personal guarantee.** Your personal liability for the company's debt. 🇨🇦 Under **CSBFP the
guarantee is capped at 25% of the original loan amount** — a materially better position than the
unlimited guarantees common on conventional Canadian small-business lending, and one of the strongest
reasons to fit a deal into the programme.

**Covenant.** A promise to the lender, tested periodically. The common ones are a leverage covenant
(debt/EBITDA not above 3–4×) and a DSCR covenant. Breach usually triggers acceleration or a rate
step-up, not immediate seizure — but it hands the lender control of the conversation.

## 5.3 Financing

**CSBFP — Canada Small Business Financing Program.** 🇨🇦 The federal loan-guarantee programme
Canadian small-business acquisitions get built around. **Guarantees 85% of the lender's loss**; caps
at **$1.15M total** — up to **$1M for real property** (guaranteed at 90%), **$500K for equipment and
leasehold improvements**, and, since 2022, **$150K for intangible assets and working capital**.
Borrower revenue must be under **$10M**. Terms run to **25 years on real property, 10 years
otherwise**. Loans are **assumable**, and personal guarantees are capped at **25%**.
**The point that changes how you shop: CSBFP finances *assets*, not the price of a business.**
Goodwill is not eligible beyond the $150K intangibles bucket. A US-style "SBA will finance the
purchase" plan does not translate. *Verify current limits with a Canadian lender — the programme's
caps have changed and will change again.*

**SBA loan.** The US equivalent, and the reason so much US content does not transfer: the SBA 7(a)
programme lends against the *business*, including goodwill, at up to $5M with ~10% down. When a US
source says "you can buy a $2M business with $200K", that is an SBA fact, not a Canadian one.

**BDC — Business Development Bank of Canada.** 🇨🇦 A Crown corporation lender that plays the layer
above CSBFP: subordinate debt, growth and working-capital financing, equipment. Usually more
expensive than a chartered bank and more flexible. *Verify current products directly.*

**Senior debt.** The bank loan that ranks first against the assets. Cheapest money in the stack and
the most conditional.

**Operating line / revolver.** Revolving credit for working capital — payroll, receivables, the gap
between doing the work and being paid. **Negotiate it as part of the acquisition package, sized off
your growth plan, not the seller's history.** You will not get it afterwards, and running out of
working capital is the most common way a fundamentally sound acquisition fails. (Lesson 19.)

**DSCR — Debt Service Coverage Ratio.** Cash available for debt service ÷ total debt service.
Lenders want **1.25–1.5×**. **Run this backwards to set your ceiling on price:** take the earnings you
actually believe, divide by 1.35, and that is the annual debt service you can carry; from that,
the debt; from that, the price. Any number above it is you funding optimism with your own cash.

**Capital stack.** The layers of money in a deal, ranked: senior debt → subordinate/BDC debt → VTB →
your equity. Each layer is cheaper and more conditional than the one above it.

**Term sheet.** A lender's non-binding statement of terms. **Non-binding is the operative word:** in
this corpus a buyer with six term sheets had his chosen bank change the terms completely two weeks
before closing, after credit committee. [`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Searcher-to-CEO-and-Exit-to-Search-Fund-Investor-Steve-Divitkos.md`] Shop at
least four or five lenders and keep the losers warm until the money is in.

**Credit committee.** Where a banker's enthusiasm meets the institution's rules. Your banker is not
the decision-maker; ask early what credit committee will want to see.

## 5.4 Diligence and deal process

**LOI — Letter of Intent.** The non-binding agreement on price and structure that opens exclusivity
and starts real diligence. Get the seller's price expectation on the first call, long before this
document. [Lesson 5]

**Exclusivity / no-shop.** The seller's agreement not to talk to other buyers for a defined period.
Your protection for the money you are about to spend on diligence.

**QoE — Quality of Earnings.** An accountant's report testing whether reported earnings are real,
repeatable and correctly stated. **Diligence has a hard ceiling** in these businesses — the records
often simply do not exist to prove more — and knowing where that ceiling is matters as much as the
report itself. [Lesson 14]

**Reps and warranties.** The seller's written statements of fact about the business. They matter
mainly as the thing you can point at afterwards — and only to the extent the seller has money left
or the VTB is still outstanding.

**Customer concentration.** The share of revenue from the largest accounts. In janitorial,
landscaping and home services this is the most common valuation killer. The standard mitigant is a
self-extinguishing note tied to named accounts.

**Key-person risk.** Revenue that leaves with one individual. In this corpus: the estimator who holds
every relationship, the one crew lead everyone follows, the licence-holder in a licensed trade. One
operator assigned **$50,000 of valuation to retaining one trainer**, and made hiring a specific
office manager a condition of closing.
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth-Acquisitions-and-Selling.md`]

**Buying a job.** A business whose entire earnings are the owner's own labour. *"There's no value
attached to that entity — the person buying is buying a job."*
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth-Acquisitions-and-Selling.md`]

**The absentee test.** The fastest single diligence question: **when did the owner last take two
consecutive weeks off, and what broke?** An owner who cannot leave for two weeks is selling you a
job. [same source]

**The day-two problem.** Everything that becomes yours the morning after closing that no spreadsheet
priced: the payroll run, the key that only the owner has, the customer who only ever spoke to them.

**Recurring vs reoccurring revenue.** The distinction that drives the multiple. **Recurring** is
contracted and automatic — it arrives unless someone cancels. **Reoccurring** is a customer who
happens to come back — it arrives only if you sell it again. Sellers use the words
interchangeably; buyers must not. Contracted monthly janitorial is recurring. A lawn customer who
called again last spring is reoccurring.

**Silent churn.** Accounts that are technically still active but shrinking — fewer visits, reduced
scope, deferred extras. Does not show up in a customer count. Show up in revenue per account over
time, which is why you ask for **three years of revenue by account**, not a customer list.

## 5.5 Operator vocabulary — the words in OPERATORS.md

**Production rate.** Square feet cleaned per hour — the core pricing input in janitorial. The corpus
sources give figures from **1,000 to 4,000 sq ft/hr** and disagree loudly about it, which is the
lesson: *nobody can quote you a production rate for a building they have not walked.*

**Labour burden.** The true cost of an employee above the wage: payroll taxes, workers' comp,
insurance, holiday pay, training time. In the worked example in the corpus, **$13/hr becomes $16.32**
— roughly **26% on top**. 🇨🇦 In Canada the components are CPP, EI, provincial WSIB/WCB premiums,
vacation pay and statutory holidays; the percentage differs by province and rate group. **Any
seller's margin computed on bare wages is overstated by about a quarter.**

**Experience rating.** 🇨🇦 The workers'-compensation premium adjustment based on a firm's claims
history. Matters in an acquisition because a good rating is an asset — and in an **asset** sale you
may not inherit it. Ask before you assume the seller's premium line carries over.

**Successor rights / employment continuity.** 🇨🇦 In several provinces, employees of a business sold
as a going concern are deemed to have continuous service with the buyer — accrued notice and
severance entitlements come with them. In Ontario, **building-services** work (cleaning, security,
food services at a premises) has specific successor-employer provisions that can bind a new
contractor. **This is the single most under-appreciated Canadian difference for a janitorial buyer.**
*Confirm with an employment lawyer in the relevant province.*

**Worker classification.** 🇨🇦 Whether a cleaner, landscaper or technician is an employee or an
independent contractor. CRA applies a multi-factor control test and is unimpressed by paperwork.
A target using contractors where CRA would find employees carries retroactive CPP/EI, penalties and
interest — and the exposure follows a **share** purchase.

**Route density.** How close a day's stops are to each other. The hidden driver of margin in
pest control, lawn care and any route business: the same revenue with half the drive time is a
completely different business. **Ask for stops-per-day-per-truck, not customer counts.**

**Non-billable time.** Drive time, loading, fuelling, shop time, rework. The corpus puts it at
roughly **$3,000/day** for one landscaping operation — the difference between the margin on the
estimate and the margin in the bank.

**Productivity decay.** The documented drop from **24 lawns/day to 14** as a crew and its routes
degrade — the reason a landscaping business's margin can collapse without a single price change or
lost customer.

**LTV — lifetime value.** What a customer is worth over the whole relationship. **The trap the
corpus flags: quote it in gross profit, never in revenue.** A cleaning account quoted at $10,000
of LTV revenue may be **$3,000 of LTV gross profit** — and only the second number can be compared
to what you spent to win it.

**CAC — customer acquisition cost.** What it costs to win one customer, all-in. Only meaningful
against LTV *in gross profit*.

**MRR — monthly recurring revenue.** The pricing base for pest control and contracted janitorial,
and the number a buyer should be underwriting rather than annual revenue.

**Pay-when-paid.** A subcontract clause making your payment contingent on your customer being paid
by *their* client. The single worst clause in the snow-subcontracting agreements read aloud in this
corpus — a real case ran **$3,900 unpaid from January to August**.
[`Landscaping-snow-operators/Snow-Contracts-EXPOSED-Read-THIS-Before-You-Sign.md`] 🇨🇦 Enforceability
in Canada is affected by provincial prompt-payment legislation (Ontario's Construction Act among
them) — **confirm with a Canadian commercial lawyer.**

**Back-charge.** The customer's deduction from your invoice for a claimed failure — including the
cost of the contractor they hired to redo it, at their price. Contingent liability that does not
appear anywhere in a target's financial statements.

**Seasonal / per-push / per-event contracts.** The three ways snow work is sold. **Seasonal** is a
flat price, usually billed over five equal monthly instalments, and is the best cash-flow structure
available in the trade — but it puts the winter risk on the operator, which is why good contracts
have a **reversion ceiling** (e.g. after 40 inches, billing reverts to per-push).
[`Landscaping-snow-operators/2025-Snow-Removal-Pricing-Secrets-You-Need-To-Know.md`]

**Reversion ceiling.** The snowfall threshold above which a seasonal contract flips to per-event
billing. Its presence or absence in a target's contract book is a direct read on how a bad winter
would hit the P&L.

**Prepay / pre-season pricing.** Discounting to collect before the season. *"I don't want to know if
my bills are paid in November — I want to know my bills are paid in August."* [same source] Verify
prepay is actually **collected in advance** and not simply invoiced early.

**Nine-arounds.** The pest-control practice of a technician working the neighbours of every job.
The cheapest customer acquisition in the trade and a direct test of whether a target's growth is
systematic or accidental.

**Law of 100.** The pest-control rule of thumb that a route needs roughly a hundred accounts to
support itself economically.

**SOPs.** Written standard operating procedures. Worth less than sellers claim: *"in theory you can
make a really good training manual, and if people don't use it, it's useless."*
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth-Acquisitions-and-Selling.md`]
Ask to watch someone actually use them.
