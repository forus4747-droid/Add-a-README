## The deal (Track A content in a Track B video)
- Ex-lawyer, Norwegian, no industry experience. **Searched for 18 months**, bought Seattle Window Cleaners in **spring 2021**.
- **Purchase price ~$400K. Total of his own cash: $45,000.**
- Financed with **SBA at up to 90% leverage** — "10% down payment, and the remainder I was able to borrow partially from the seller in a **seller note** and the rest from a bank."
- Business at purchase: **$500K/year revenue, 3 employees, three trucks (two very old)**.
- One year later: **$100K in the filmed month**, revenue up **>80% year over year**. Now 8 employees.
- **Why he bought it:** "the reason I was buying it was that it had **over 4,000 names in the database**" — previously quoted houses, emails, pricing history, in a working CRM. "I bought a phone number that keeps ringing."
- **The thesis, stated plainly:** "there's a bunch of small businesses for sale in that segment that are good sustainable businesses — **it's just that there's not a lot of buyers for them.**"
- Buy vs build: the seller "talked about working long nights in November, sick as a dog on a roof in the rain, trying to pay rent. I wanted to bypass that step."
- Estimated natural plateau for this business: **$1.5–2M revenue**. Five-year goal is a small portfolio of similar home-services businesses.

## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- **Target net margin 20–25%** once stabilised; he believes that is industry-typical. Currently taking none of it — "plowing it back in."
- Cost structure: **technicians ~35–40% of revenue**, **~30% overhead** (insurance, service, admin), leaving 20–25%.
- **Technician pay: $12/hour base plus 15% commission on their jobs.** Effective earnings **$35–40/hour**, with some over $40 in good weeks. Trainees on fixed pay until they "graduate and get their own van."
- Office manager: fixed base salary plus a **quarterly bonus**.
- **Average ticket ~$600** for window cleaning; "nobody's paying you to drive between locations," so density and speed per site are everything.
- Marketing spend: **~$3,000/month, essentially all Google Ads**, plus an automated five-round neighbour mailing after every job.
- Equipment: water-fed extension pole system **30 ft, ~$1,000–1,200** (reaches third storey without ladders); pressure washer bought used off Craigslist for **$900** versus a $7,000 new one; hose reels ~$1,000/pair.

## Seasonality and cash-flow map — the best in the corpus for this trade
- **Window cleaning ≈ 60% of revenue**, summer-weighted, quieting from late August.
- **Pressure washing ≈ 7–8% of annual revenue**, spring only.
- **Gutter cleaning** — November/December/early January, when the leaves drop.
- **Roof/moss treatment** — late winter to early spring.
- **Christmas lights** — installed from October, "really profitable," sold almost entirely into the existing customer base.
- Rule he learned the hard way: **do not spend marketing money in the slow season.** He tried, and the ROI "was pretty poor." "You can't sell Christmas lights in February… lean into it when there's demand."
- Staffing the trough honestly: **"be very open with employees from the date of hire that there's going to be ups and downs. Sometimes we work 50 hours a week, and then in the fall it's going to be more like 30."** Reduced hours, four-day weeks, or vacation — not layoffs.
- Selection rule for a second service: **it must be counter-cyclical to the first and sellable to the same customers.** Pressure washing fails this test (it coincides with window season); gutters, roofs and Christmas lights pass.
- Margins on gutter and roof cleaning are **better than windows — "in the 40s, 50s"** — but gutter jobs must be priced assuming they're full, since you can't see them from the ground.

## Winning work and systems
- **"Picking up the phone" is his single biggest growth factor.** Hiring a full-time office manager to answer calls was the first change he made — before, "there was nobody to really man the phone."
- **Online self-quoting** (ResponsiBid) replaced physically driving to 20–30 unpaid quotes a week, half of which converted. "Nobody's paying us for that."
- **Reviews: 11 Google reviews in 12 years under the old owner → 119 in 16 months**, purely by asking. "You don't get them unless you ask for them."
- **60% of customers are prior customers** — the CRM is the core system; automated "it's been a year, would you be interested in us coming back" messaging drives it.
- Average ticket increased substantially by **bundling services on one visit** — most existing customers didn't know the other services existed.
- Rebrand: the seller ran **four websites under four different names** for different parts of town, with technicians pretending to be from whichever brand matched the address, and unbranded trucks. Consolidating to one name cost some business in outlying areas (Bellevue) but was "surprisingly frictionless."
- Trust is the product in residential: "they let us into their bedroom to clean their windows" — uniforms, logo, taking your shoes off.

## Staffing reality
- Quality control is data, not vibes: the office manager **calls every customer the day after**; every callback/touch-up is logged in a spreadsheet with employee, date, job type, conditions and hypothesised cause. "It's not the individual technician that's trying to do a bad job — we've set him up for failure to some extent."
- New hires ride with a **different technician every day for three to four weeks** before getting their own vehicle, to socialise them across the team.
- At the time of filming he is back in the field himself at **60 hours a week** — "there's just too much work."

## Canadian-specific
US deal. **US — verify for Canada.** The financing is the key non-transferable element: **there is no Canadian equivalent of 90% SBA leverage.** A Canadian buyer of an equivalent asset-light window-cleaning business would need a much larger vendor take-back (see the CSBFP and VTB extracts). Everything else transfers well, and the seasonality map is *more* pronounced in Canada — a Canadian version would lean harder on snow and Christmas lights to bridge the winter.
