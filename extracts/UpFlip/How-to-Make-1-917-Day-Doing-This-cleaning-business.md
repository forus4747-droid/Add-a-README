**The time-allocation model is the whole business.** A $390/month once-weekly account with 3 hours allocated (~4.33 visits/month) means roughly **$30/hour of billing against a $17–19 wage** — which is where the 20–25% margin comes from, *if* the tech finishes on time. **Overrun is the entire margin.**

**Most profitable service: carpet cleaning**, "because you charge per square foot, and as long as you've got a good process you can bust it out." Only **two of his techs are trained on it** — otherwise he does it himself.

## Staffing reality
- **Recruiting: Indeed (primary), plus Facebook and Craigslist.** Screening is resume review then an in-person conversation about values and where they see themselves. He tried Zoom interviews during COVID and found them "not as personal."
- **Training is task-based, not site-based** — and this is the smartest structural idea here: *"We train our techs based on tasks. How to clean windows, how to clean toilets, how to clean restrooms, proper way to vacuum, proper way to mop. **Once they have these techniques down, they're able to apply it at any account.**"* The checklists reference tasks ("glass"), so a tech who knows how to clean glass applies it to whatever glass exists in that building.
- **Training happens every day** — a huddle before crews go out, plus hands-on correction in the field. "Every day is something new. The toilet's not going to look the same day to day."
- **He is on call 24/7** and names that as the biggest downside of the business. Works 16–18 hour days, "I kind of work every single day."

## Winning work
- **Checklists, in strict order, are the core operating system.** Built to be followed sequentially, split by person (person one / person two) and by task. Signed and dated, left on site (in this case the pastor's office). A **final walkthrough** confirms every task and the return of dumpster and dispenser keys.
- **Presentation over sanitation, explicitly:** *"Yes, we're sanitising, yes we're doing more underneath. **But people don't see sanitising. They smell it. They see how their office is left.** Pushing the chairs in, the knots of the bag are in the back, out of view. It's really the small things that add up."*
- **Referral programme:** discount to the referring client and a discount on the new client's first invoice.
- **Reviews are hard in commercial.** He has **six Google reviews** and says "it's harder to get reviews in the commercial industry — it's a business giving you a review as opposed to a residential person." He waits until a client has been served **for at least a year**, or has bought multiple services, before asking.
- **How he started:** move-out cleans, 10–12 houses a month from a property management company he knew, plus Craigslist. **He has since dropped move-outs entirely for commercial janitorial only.**
- The unglamorous truth about early sales: "We got a lot of nos. **100 nos eventually will turn into a yes.**"

## The grind
- Nine years in and still working every day, on call around the clock.
- On whether he ever wanted to quit: **"Most definitely."** What kept him: sunk effort and family — "I've spent nine years building this, so just giving up isn't really a good option."
- Biggest fear as an entrepreneur: **"Failing and losing all my money."**

## Cash flow traps
- **Paid-per-lead services charge whether or not you win.** At $40–75 per lead, a poor close rate turns lead generation into a straight loss. "Hopefully your selling is on point."
- **Time overrun destroys the margin**, and it is invisible unless someone is measuring against the allocation.
- The **BBB subscription at $87/month** for a rating you start with and can only lose — worth questioning as a line item.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not covered directly. But the profile — solo owner, 16–18 hour days, on call 24/7, personally performing the highest-margin service (carpet cleaning) because only two techs can do it — is exactly the owner-dependency pattern that eventually forces a sale.

## What sellers hide — and the best single warning in this extract
**Commercial clients do not complain. They leave.**
> "A lot of times when the clients come in from the weekend, they see their office — whether or not it was cleaned correctly, the trash was changed, is their toilet cleaned. **And a lot of times they don't let you know. They'll just kind of move on to another cleaning company.**"

And on why he lost accounts early: **"In most cases we lost our clients just due to poor service... either that, or we're just not doing tasks because we don't know, or they're being missed."** He also notes that new business comes almost entirely from clients unhappy with their incumbent — **"most of the time when they're unhappy with their current janitorial company, they'll start searching for another one."**

**This is the single most important diligence implication for a janitorial acquisition: a clean complaint log means nothing.** Combine it with the "no complaints is a red flag" argument in `extracts/Cleaning-operators/2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions-Grow-Profit-Succeed-Ep.-38.md` and the diligence action is clear:

- **Ask for the account-by-account start dates and any accounts lost in the last 36 months, with reasons.** Silent churn is the industry's characteristic failure and it will not appear in a P&L until the revenue is already gone.
- **Ask for the time allocation and the actual clocked time per account.** This is where the margin lives. A seller quoting 20–25% margins on allocations their crews routinely overrun is quoting a model, not a result.
- **Ask whether checklists exist, whether they're signed and dated on site, and whether training is task-based or site-based.** Task-based training means techs are interchangeable across accounts — a genuinely transferable asset. Site-based training means every departure costs you an account.
- **Ask what the owner personally still performs.** Carpet cleaning is his most profitable service and only two techs can do it. **When he leaves, either the margin or the service leaves with him.**
- **Ask for the cost per acquired account through paid lead services**, not the monthly spend. Pay-per-lead economics only work at a high close rate.
- **Check the wage ladder against the market.** A $17–19 ceiling with no medical benefits is a retention risk you inherit — see `extracts/Landscaping-snow-operators/Lawn-Care-Podcast-Landscaping-Business-Secrets.md` on a foreman quitting over a single health benefit.

## Verdict
**The most operationally granular janitorial video in the corpus** — real account prices, real time allocations, a real wage ladder, and a genuine on-camera demonstration of how long a restroom should take. For anyone valuing a Canadian janitorial book, three numbers here are directly usable as benchmarks: **~$390/month for a once-weekly small account, 3 hours allocated including travel, and 20–25% margin contingent on hitting the time.**

Two structural lessons stand out.

**First, task-based training as a transferable asset.** Training techs on *tasks* (glass, restrooms, vacuuming, mopping) rather than on *specific buildings* is what makes staff interchangeable and makes a book survivable through turnover. In an industry with the recruiting ratios documented elsewhere in this corpus, that is worth real money at acquisition — and its absence is a hidden cost.

**Second, silent churn.** Commercial cleaning clients don't complain their way out, they simply hire someone else — which means an acquirer cannot rely on complaint records, satisfaction surveys or the seller's assurances. **The only reliable check is account tenure data and direct conversations with the clients themselves during diligence.**

The startup-cost figure ($1,300–1,500) also frames the buy-versus-build question honestly for janitorial: **the barrier to entry is essentially zero, so what you're buying is the 52 accounts, the checklists, and the trained crew — not the equipment.** Price it that way, and treat the customer contracts and their assignability as the entire asset.

**US (Washington State) — the model transfers; three Canadian checks.** Wages ($17–19) are broadly comparable to Ontario/BC janitorial once payroll burden is added, but **verify the burden rate provincially** (see `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Price-Commercial-Cleaning-RIGHT-Cleaning-Bid-Calculator.md`). **HomeAdvisor/Angi coverage differs in Canada**, so the pay-per-lead channel needs local verification. And the **BBB has a distinct Canadian structure** — treat the $87/month as a discretionary marketing line, not a requirement.
