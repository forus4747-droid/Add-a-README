## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
**Portfolio (~$6M total revenue, "eight tax returns a year"):** two car washes, a self-storage facility, ~100 rental properties, the local newspaper, a laundromat, vending machines, and social media.

**Car wash — US industry averages he cites, then applies to his own site:**
| Component | Average revenue |
|---|---|
| Vacuum machine | **$200/month each** |
| Self-serve bay | **$1,500/month each** |
| Automatic bay | **$10,000/month** |
| **His site** (1 automatic + 3 self-serve + 3 vacuums) | **~$15,100/month top line** |
| **Industry margin** | **40–45%** |

- **Overhead at that site:** water and sewer **$2,000/month**, electric **~$400/month**, natural gas **~$300/month**, soap **~$12,000/year** — enough that they now **manufacture their own soap on site** from bulk chemicals, using their water filtration system.
- **Traffic count is the underwriting metric:** 9,100 cars/day on his main road, 4,900/day at the second location. He notes this is **public ODOT data** — i.e. verifiable before you ever talk to the seller.

**Self-storage — 38 units:**
- Paid **$75,000–100,000** (segmented out of a package deal); generates **~$2,500/month**.
- Average unit **~$85–100/month**; smallest is 10×10, most are 12×16.
- **Management cost: $10/week** — half an hour of someone's time at $20/hour.
- **Property taxes (~$200/month) are the largest expense** — "typically with self-storage, property taxes are your number one expense."
- **His honest verdict: "How easy this business is compared to my other ones. The ROI has been great. The downside is this is not scalable in any way, shape or form — the only way is to physically build more, and I can't."**

**Laundromat:**
- Bought for **$85,000 including the real estate**; he estimates the property alone is now worth **$300,000+**.
- **~$135,000/year gross; net $65,000–75,000** (with some remaining debt service).
- 18 washers, 12 dryers. **Target: 3 turns per day per machine.** A $3 machine at 3 turns = $9/day.
- Pricing: **30 lb machine $4.50; 60 lb machine $9.00.** Customers prefer bigger machines — and he confirms they *do* clean better, because people under-pack them.
- **Equipment cost: ~$3,500 for a 20 lb washer, plus ~$500 per machine to connect** (water and electric). Total equipment spend $105–110K. He warns infrastructure alone can run **$100–150K** for a full build.
- **His biggest mistake: not installing enough large machines.** "If I'd planned this location for more larger machines, I'd probably be closer to $175–185K instead of $125–135K."
- **Accepting credit cards produced an overnight 35% revenue increase**, and roughly doubled revenue over time. Reasoning: "There's people that will drop $40, $50, $60 on their car and they don't want to spend that money in fives. **We made it easier for them to spend more money.**"

**Real estate:** peaked at ~142 rentals in 2019, **~$58,000/month top line.** First deal: **$25,000 acquisition + $25,000 rehab.**

**Social media:** ~1.8M YouTube subscribers, ~6M across platforms; **~$750,000/year revenue**, most of it YouTube.

## What to look for — the financing content, which is the real value here
**Twenty banks said no.** As a licensed real estate agent who knew exactly which local properties would cash flow, he still could not raise money: "The first 20 banks I talked to all wanted 20% down and cash reserves to fix the property up, and that was just never going to happen." Later, a bank that had approved him **withdrew one week before closing** because the bank was acquired and the new owner disliked his risk profile.

**So he built the deal, then went to find money — in that order.**
- He assembled a list of a dozen specific local properties with known rehab costs and modelled **20–25% returns**, then offered investors **a 50/50 split — "you get 12.5% cash-on-cash."**
- **Within a year: ~$500,000 in commitments.** After one publication covered his story, people were calling from around the country.
- **Where to find that money — his specific advice:** **local real estate investing clubs** ("they're all there to make money"), and secondarily Rotary and civic groups ("great networking, but those people aren't all there to make money"). The highest-value targets: **investors holding self-directed IRAs, especially self-directed Roth IRAs**, who want tax-advantaged growth and will lend against real estate as lien holders at 10–14%.
- **He is emphatic about seller financing.** Asked what he'd do starting from zero today: **"Real estate, seller financed."** The car wash he bought was heavily seller-financed, and he chose that site out of six specifically because of the structure.

**The walk-away discipline — the best paragraph in the video:**
> "Be okay with walking away from a deal. People send me DMs — *I found this deal* — and I help them go over the numbers and it's not a deal at all. And I say, you've got to walk away, it's not fixable, go find something else. And it breaks their heart, they're so excited that they finally found a car wash for sale. **If you can't walk away from any deal out there, then the seller has all the power.** As a buyer, you're the one writing the checks every month — you've got a very high level of power and you need to utilise it."

**His diligence checklist for a car wash** is short and sound: *get a good deal, verify the numbers, **read the tax returns**, and **sit in the parking lot** and count cars; check the water bill.* On the deal he did: **"He had tax returns. He wasn't lying to the IRS — which was very good for me, because I felt like I could quantify the money coming in."**

**Demand validation before committing.** Before buying a rental at what would be the highest rent in town ($1,000/month for a five-bedroom), he **ran Facebook polls and test-posted the listing on Craigslist** to confirm demand existed. Cheap, fast, and directly transferable to testing a new service line or market.

## The grind
- Family evicted at 6, again at 18; parents' 1996 tax return showed them **50% below the federal poverty line.** Working a warehouse job at **$11.25/hour** at 21.
- His first car wash winter was the coldest in Ohio history at −35°: "Everything that could freeze up did freeze up." Then unplanned wear on equipment. Then **a customer drove an SUV through the automatic bay — his single largest income producer — three months after purchase**, and the insurance payout covered only a fraction of replacement.
- The laundromat he bought was "an absolute nightmare... the fire department wouldn't let us open until we removed equipment," with a machine breaking every other week for the first year and a half.

## Cash flow traps
- **Deferred maintenance in acquired unattended businesses is the recurring theme.** Both the car wash and the laundromat came with condition problems the seller's numbers didn't reflect.
- **Insurance payouts on commercial equipment fall well short of replacement cost.**
- **Self-storage does not scale without land.** Great ROI, hard ceiling.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not covered — he's a buyer, not a seller. The relevant signal is what he *bought*: **neglected assets in a small town, from owners who had stopped maintaining them.** The local newspaper was available for **$10,000; he offered $6,000 and got it.** An apartment complex (duplex plus house) went for **$250**, was held three years, and resold for $100,000.

## Canadian-specific
- **Nothing Canadian; nothing directly on-thesis.** Four things translate:
  1. **The build-the-deal-then-raise-the-money sequence.** In Canada, where acquisition debt is scarcer than in the US (no SBA equivalent; CSBFP caps at $1.15M and funds assets only), **private investor capital is proportionally more important**, not less. His method — a specific modelled deal, a clear return, a named split — is how you actually raise it.
  2. **The Canadian analogue of the self-directed Roth IRA is a self-directed RRSP or TFSA.** Both **can hold arm's-length mortgages and certain private debt**, but the rules are stricter than the US equivalent — non-arm's-length and "prohibited investment" rules carry punitive taxes. **Verify with a Canadian tax advisor before soliciting anyone's registered funds; getting this wrong is expensive for the investor, not just for you.**
  3. **Seller financing = VTB**, and his experience matches the corpus: for a small local business with a tired owner, vendor financing is often the *only* practical structure. See `extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md`.
  4. **"Talk to more than one bank"** — echoing Terry Lammers (`extracts/David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`). Twenty rejections and a withdrawal one week before closing is not unusual.
- **The small-town thesis is genuinely relevant to Canada.** Circleville, Ohio has 14,000 people and supports four car washes, a laundromat, self-storage and a newspaper — all available cheaply because nobody else was buying. **The equivalent Canadian secondary markets have the same dynamic**, and it is the same argument made about small-market service businesses in `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`.

## Verdict
**Off-thesis industries, on-thesis discipline.** Skip the car wash and laundromat arithmetic unless Jay's search widens — though the benchmarks are clean and worth keeping if unattended businesses ever come up (**$1,500/month per self-serve bay, ~$200/month per vacuum, 40–45% car wash margins, 3 turns/day per laundromat machine, property taxes as the largest self-storage expense**).

Three things genuinely worth carrying forward:

1. **Build the deal before you look for the money.** He raised $500,000 within a year with no capital and no track record, because he brought investors a specific, modelled, verifiable opportunity rather than an intention. In Canada, where the institutional debt ceiling is low, this is the skill that unlocks deals — and **local investment clubs plus registered-account investors are the named source.**
2. **Walk-away discipline is leverage.** The single most valuable line in the video, and it applies to every negotiation in this knowledge base. A buyer emotionally committed to *this* business has surrendered the only real advantage they have.
3. **Verify with public and physical data before you trust the seller's numbers.** Traffic counts from public transport data, water bills, tax returns, sitting in the parking lot counting cars. The service-business equivalents: **map the customer addresses, count the trucks against the service load, walk the sites, read the WSIB claims history.** The principle — find the number the seller cannot manipulate — is the same.

The honest caution: this is a **portfolio of small, unrelated, mostly unattended businesses**, which is close to the opposite of the focused-operator advice from Divitkos, Wilson and Peterman elsewhere in this corpus. His self-storage assessment — great returns, zero scalability — is the tell. **For someone wanting to own an operating service business with employees and a growth path, this is an interesting counter-model, not a template.**
