**Why Google LSA wins:** "These customers' buying intention is higher because they're literally searching *house cleaning in [area]* — when they call you they're ready to roll. **The other companies are more like lead generation — you still need to work those leads.**"

**Why he's dropping Yelp:** "We get a lot of leads — *I want a quote, I want a quote* — and I respond to everyone... but now it seems like we're just spinning our wheels. Google really is taking over after they implemented the reviews."

## Staffing reality — the binding constraint, stated plainly
- **"My biggest hurdle, my biggest impediment, is just finding the right people."** And the consequence: **"Business is out there, we constantly get calls — but we don't have the people to do them, so we turn them away."**
- **The turnover mechanism is specific:** "We don't really suffer when it comes to getting people in. **The problem is once they go in for a couple of days, that's when people realise maybe it's not for me.** House cleaning sounds fairly easy — it's an easy job, but it's physical. After eight hours of cleaning you'll be like, wow." He raised rates to attract better candidates; people still leave after a couple of days.
- **Recruiting channels: Indeed ads + Craigslist ads + Facebook groups.** He notes **two of his best cleaners came from a free Facebook group.**
- **He will not hire from other cleaning companies:** "They usually have their own ways of doing things, and it was hard for me. Everybody can learn — it's not that hard — **just give me the opportunity to teach a different process.**" He prefers hotel-industry experience or none at all.
- **Training:** day one, shadow an experienced team and don't clean — "just get a sense of how it's going to work." Day two, pair with one trained person. Entirely hands-on.
- **Retention levers:** celebrating birthdays, occasional family gatherings, "making everyone part of the family."
- **Non-solicitation agreements** signed at hire, to stop cleaners taking clients directly. He says it's "totally enforceable."

## Winning work
- **Three quoting paths, deliberately, because customers differ:** (1) a **self-serve online booking form** with a card, for people who don't want to talk to anyone; (2) a **quote request form** for people unwilling to enter a card online; (3) **quote over the phone** based on size, frequency and pets, "subject to change based on the actual condition of the home."
- **The bottleneck he removed:** he used to do in-person estimates and was the constraint. Switching to phone quotes unblocked growth — over his wife's objections. "Always try different things."
- **Answer the phone.** He names customer service as the single biggest growth factor: "When people are looking for this type of service **they don't have time to be calling around. So if you answer the phone call, you get it.**" A missed call "could potentially be worth thousands of dollars" — which is why he moved from Google Voice to RingCentral for call waiting and VA coverage.
- **Card on file at booking.** "You always want to collect the credit card. You don't want to chase your money."
- **The job workflow:** call → phone quote → card collected → scheduled **by ZIP code against existing routes** → cleaner gets a push notification and can accept or reject → **in-app checklist that cannot be skipped** (the job won't close until every room is checked) → completion notification → card charged → review request sent → **follow-up call the next day asking how it went and pitching recurring service.**
- **Outbound lead gen:** VAs scrape Zillow for realtors, property managers and real estate agents in the county, build a list, and call and email for appointments. **Realtor work is preferred because "these homes are empty, they're just staged — no grease, the bathrooms are fairly clean, so the job is simpler."**
- **Quality service guarantee** as an objection-handler: "My biggest objection on the phone is *maybe it's too much, not in my budget.* That's when I say — **you can choose another cleaning company, but if everything goes wrong then you're going to have to call me and I'm going to have to redo it.**"
- **Upsell from the existing base:** added carpet cleaning because recurring customers kept asking. Started with a **portable machine to test** before committing to a truck mount.
- **What customers actually buy:** "They're not looking for the cheapest price... **you're not selling them a cleaning, you're giving them their time back.**"

## The grind
- Year one was chaos: scheduling in Google Calendar, invoices in Google Sheets, "everything inside my head." He is explicit: **"process → growth. No process → disaster."**
- Software stack: **Jobber** (jobs, invoices, routes), **Slack** (internal comms, with a channel per scenario — cancellations, new charges), **RingCentral** (VAs in the Philippines answering phones), **ClickUp** (goals and department tasks), **Booking Koala** (booking), **WordPress**.
- **His method for choosing new systems: ask the staff.** "I cannot test everything, so I ask my employees — tell me what we can improve. Same with my customer service VA — what software do we need to make your life easier." And: **run the new system alongside the old one for a month (most offer 14-day trials) before switching.**

## Cash flow traps
- **The workers' compensation story is the most useful warning here.** An employee was cleaning an old sash window; nothing was holding it up; **it fell and fractured her hand.** She couldn't work. **Washington's industrial insurance rate rose materially, and it takes three accident-free years to reset.** He notes it moved workers' comp from a minor line to nearly the size of his largest expense after labour. *(This is why his net margin is 15% and not 20%, on his own account.)*
- **December is a soft month** — enough to miss an annual target.
- Growth without documented process: "I had everything inside my head. It was terrible."

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not covered — he's growing. But he says the plan is to **"run it for another four or five years and really sell it."**

## What sellers hide — and one seller getting valuation wrong on camera
**The single most instructive moment in this video, for a buyer:**

> **Chris:** "Right now with the $1.4–1.5 million, I can easily ask two times that in revenue — so that'll be three to four million for the business."
> **Host:** "I think you want a little too much, because **it's always a multiple of the net.**"
> **Chris:** "So the net? ...I thought it was more of the gross."

**A successful, seven-figure, ten-year operator did not know his business is valued on earnings rather than revenue.** At 15% net on $1.4M, that's roughly **$210K of profit** — at 3× SDE, around **$600–700K**, not $3–4M. **He is off by a factor of five.**

For Jay, this is the practical lesson: **many sellers of small service businesses anchor on a revenue multiple they heard somewhere.** Expect it, don't take it as bad faith, and be prepared to walk the seller through the arithmetic — or to walk away. It also explains why so many small businesses sit on the market unsold.

**Other diligence questions this video generates:**
- **Ask for the workers' comp / WSIB experience rating and claims history, not just the current premium.** A single claim can move the rate for three years, and the buyer inherits the experience rating. This is a real, quantifiable liability that rarely appears in a CIM.
- **Ask what marketing spend produces the revenue, by channel, with cost per booked job.** At 10% of revenue with $5K/month in one channel, this is the largest controllable expense after labour — and the most likely to have been cut before a sale.
- **Ask for the review count and rating.** His own 100-review inflection point is a real asset that transfers with the business (unless it's a franchise — see `extracts/UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future.md`).
- **Ask about the 30-day and 90-day survival rate of new hires**, not just headcount. His entire constraint is that people quit after two days.
- **Ask whether processes are documented.** His own best lesson: *"Document everything — especially videos. When you want to sell it, that's where the value comes in. **This is the standard operating procedure of your company: here you go.** It's more attractive for people to buy."* A business run out of the owner's head is worth less, and he knows it.
- **Ask how many jobs are turned away for lack of staff.** If demand exceeds capacity, the growth constraint is recruiting — which means a buyer's plan must be a *hiring* plan, not a marketing plan.

## Verdict
**Good numbers, real constraints, and one accidental masterclass in seller psychology.**

Three things to carry into a Canadian residential-cleaning acquisition:

1. **The margin benchmark: ~15% net, with 20% as an aspiration**, on a $1.4M book with 22 employees. That is the reality check against any seller claiming 30%+ on a labour-intensive residential business — and it aligns with the 15–25% figure from `extracts/UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future.md` and the 20–30% commercial figure from `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Scaling-Your-Cleaning-Business-Boosting-Profits-Efficiency-in-Janitorial-Services-Ep.-149.md`.
2. **Marketing at 10% of revenue, concentrated in Google Local Services Ads.** This is now the third independent operator in the corpus to name LSA as the highest-intent channel. **Verify Google LSA availability and Google Guarantee eligibility for the target Canadian city and category before assuming this transfers** — coverage differs by country.
3. **The workers' comp experience-rating trap.** One accident, three years of elevated premiums, and it visibly cost him five points of net margin. **In Canada the equivalent is the WSIB/WCB experience rating (and NEER/CAD-7-style adjustments), which absolutely follows the business.** Get the claims history and the rate schedule in diligence — this is one of the few liabilities you can price exactly.

And the meta-lesson: **the seller here believed his $1.4M business was worth $3–4M.** Being able to have that conversation calmly — anchored on SDE, add-backs and a defensible multiple — is going to be a recurring requirement in a market of owner-operators who have never sold anything before.

**US (Washington State) — labour costs, workers' comp mechanics and Google LSA coverage all need Canadian verification.** WA's high minimum wage makes its $20–22/hour cleaner pay a reasonable proxy for Ontario/BC once payroll burden is added (see the Canadian burden-rate note in `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Price-Commercial-Cleaning-RIGHT-Cleaning-Bid-Calculator.md`).
