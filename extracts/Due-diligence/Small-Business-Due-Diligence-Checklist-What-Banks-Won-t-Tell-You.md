## Key numbers
- One overlooked document nearly cost a client **$200,000**.
- A second client who ignored his own doubts lost **~$250,000** and had to inject "hundreds of thousands" within a year to make payroll, on a business whose revenues turned out to be inflated.
- Both cautionary figures are anecdotal and unaudited — treat them as illustrative, not as base rates.

## What to look for
**Financial verification**
- **Tax returns vs financial statements are different documents with different purposes.** Returns are built to minimize tax; statements are built to look healthy. Get both, for 3+ years, and reconcile them line by line.
- The classic tell: **losses on the tax return, profits on the financial statements.** Someone is lying to someone.
- Chase **"miscellaneous expenses"** that are consistently high and conveniently offset dips in profitability. In his example, the invoices concealed personal travel booked as business development and payments to family members for services never rendered. (Note: those are *add-backs* a seller will want credited to SDE — verify each one is genuinely non-recurring and not a real cost of operating.)
- **The "Friday afternoon test"** — stress-test cash flow at the moment payroll and supplier payments land, not at month-end when the balance looks tidy.

**Legal and compliance**
- **Employee vs independent contractor classification.** Misclassification carries penalties and back-assessments. (This is the single biggest landmine in cleaning and landscaping — see `extracts/Cleaning-operators/`.)
- **IP ownership** — logos, customer lists, proprietary methods actually owned by the corporation, not by the departing owner personally.
- **Litigation** — past, present, and threatened: customer disputes, contract breaches, regulatory actions, employment claims.
- **Permits and licences: does a change of control void them?** He calls this the detail most sellers forget and many advisors miss.

**Operations**
- **Customer concentration.** Name the top customers and their revenue share. One dominant customer is a major red flag.
- **Vendor contracts under change of control** — same question as permits.
- **The "parking lot test"** — visit early morning, late evening, weekends. Busy lot = live business; empty lot = trouble.
- **Employee interviews** — consult an employment lawyer first, then ask open-ended questions about role, culture, and outlook, and listen for gaps between what staff say and what the seller said.

**Market position**
- Social media / review-site reputation audit: recurring complaints and recurring praise.
- Competitive secret shopping.
- Google autocomplete on industry keywords as a cheap read on live customer sentiment.

**Deal breakers**
- The **"too good to be true" pattern**: suspiciously consistent profits, margins or growth that defy industry norms.
- **Owner behaviour**: overly eager to sell, pushing a fast close, evasive or inconsistent answers.
- **Documentation gaps** — inability or unwillingness to produce core documents, or promises to send them "later."

## Canadian-specific
- **US — verify for Canada.** He says "IRS"; read **CRA**. Canadian analogues to add to the checklist:
  - **CRA arrears and liens** — payroll source deductions and GST/HST are held in trust; unremitted amounts can follow the business and, in some cases, directors personally. Get a CRA clearance/statement of account.
  - **WSIB (ON) / WCB (other provinces)** clearance certificate — unpaid premiums can attach to a purchaser.
  - **Worker classification** is tested under the CRA/provincial common-law tests, not the IRS 20-factor test — same risk, different rulebook.
  - **Provincial licensing** — pest control applicators, WHMIS, pesticide licences (Ontario/Quebec cosmetic-pesticide restrictions), snow-contractor insurance requirements. Confirm each transfers or is re-issuable to the new entity.
  - **Employment standards on transfer**: in most Canadian jurisdictions, employees carry their **accrued service** to the buyer in a share sale, and often in an asset sale too. Termination/severance exposure is inherited. Price it.
- The Canadian version of "the banks won't tell you": the bank underwrites *its* collateral position, not *your* return. CSBFP lends against assets, so nobody at the bank is diligencing the customer concentration that will actually kill you.

## Quotable lines
1. "Tax returns are prepared for the IRS, designed to minimize tax liability. Financial statements provide a more comprehensive view... but they can also be manipulated."
2. "Will a change of control cause a loss of permits or licenses?"
3. "It's better to miss out on a good deal than to get stuck with a bad one."

## Verdict
Thin on numbers, strong on **checklist structure** — this is the best single source in the corpus for the *shape* of a diligence plan, and three of its items (change-of-control on permits, employee classification, tax-return-vs-statement reconciliation) are high-value and rarely mentioned elsewhere. The "parking lot test" and "Google autocomplete" tricks are cheap and genuinely worth doing. Discount the storytelling and the loss figures. Use the four headings — Financial / Legal / Operations / Market — as the skeleton of the `KNOWLEDGE.md` due-diligence checklist, with the Canadian additions above bolted on.
