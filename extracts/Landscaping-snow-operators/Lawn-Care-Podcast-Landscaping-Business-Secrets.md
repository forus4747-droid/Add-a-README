## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** | **$15.5M** (New Hampshire, snow belt) |
| **Employees** | **100+**, with **700 assets** and **5 in-house mechanics** |
| **Employee retention** | **95%** — the host calls this "absolutely unheard of in this industry" |
| Hiring pipeline | **A 30+ person waiting list** of approved, hired candidates with no current opening |
| **Non-billable time cost** | **~$3,000/day** wasted if crews stand around 5 minutes morning and evening across 50–75 people |
| **Mowing productivity decay** | Owners cutting for themselves: **24 lawns/day.** Hired crews: **18 → 16 → 14/day.** "If you don't watch your pricing, your profitability disappears — and we saw that happen firsthand." |
| Gross margin | Runs the business on **gross profit as a percentage of revenue**, daily. Names **30% GP as the level at which "the rest of the company is struggling."** |

**The productivity-decay number is the most important single figure here for a buyer.** A seller who built their pricing when *they* were cutting 24 lawns a day, and who now runs crews doing 14, has a 40% labour-cost increase that may never have been repriced. **Check whether the price list predates the crews.**

## Staffing reality — the deepest treatment of retention in the corpus
**The Bleed Blue Truck programme.** At **10 years of service**, any employee who doesn't already have a company vehicle — i.e. crew members and crew leaders who never went into management or sales — **gets a new company truck for personal use**, plus a **$250 fuel card**, with registration, inspection and maintenance covered. Custom plates: BLEED BLUE 1, BLEED BLUE 2.

Fairburn's reasoning: "We could run like a normal landscape company where the managers earn more — field managers, sales staff — and the crew leader and crew member get left behind. **I decided that wasn't going to happen here.**" The effect: "All I hear walking around now is *I only have five years left, I only have seven years left.*"

**Financial education as a retention tool — the most original idea in the operator set.**
- Started with the **North Point Cares fund**: a pool funded by the two owners and staff peers, with **a panel of employees deciding who gets loans and short-term advances.**
- That panel came back and said: *"It's been great to help these people — but how do you teach a man to fish?"*
- They rolled out **Dave Ramsey's SmartDollar** company-wide, supported by monthly meetings of 20–30 people discussing wins and struggles.
- The story that lands: a young fleet mechanic entered his budget and savings into the projection tool. **"$5.6 million showed up on the screen. He said to me — *is that a comma at the end? Why is there three zeros?*"** Fairburn: "Never in his life has he ever equated millions to himself. And now he has."
- His framing: **"My plan is to make landscape millionaires."** And the retention logic is explicit: *"If you sit down a 21-year-old and explain compound interest, they're not going to leave because the company down the road gave them a better health benefit."*
- The counter-example the host offers: a foreman who quit **over a single health benefit.** "If there's no vision, guys are going to jump ship pretty quickly."

**Onboarding:** application → recruiter call → placement matched to skill set (maintenance, design-build, irrigation, turf care) → **"North Point University"** training modules → dispatch to a crew → **recruiter follows up again** to confirm they feel confident, safe and equipped. Crew leaders and foremen are briefed on how overwhelming a large operation is for a new hire.

**Recruiting channels:** local Facebook ads, and **QR codes on jackets, snow plows and trucks** that link straight to the application page. **A full-time in-house recruiter** who meets candidates where it's convenient for them.

## Winning work
- **Card on file, mandatory.** They moved off cash and cheque early, via Service Autopilot, and were among the first in their market with a full CRM. "**We really appreciated the positive cash flow of having credit cards on file** — and that was a big, scary step at the time."
- **The ideal-customer definition follows from it:** "Our ideal client became somebody willing to have a card on file, willing to have automated services. **We were showing up annually to complete a spring cleanup without even calling people.**"
- **The service model: opt-out, not opt-in.** "We changed our mentality from waiting for approvals to do work — **the moment you sign up, you're getting this full service until you say [stop]. We're going to come the following season until you tell us not to.** We'll send you price increases and we'll communicate."
- **He identified the ideal customer "about five years too late."** Before that: *"say yes to everything, work for everyone... the customer was running the show."*
- **They exited residential maintenance 3–4 years ago for commercial** — reluctantly ("the clients had grown into family almost") — because **commercial lets you control labour better.** They kept residential for turf care and irrigation.

## The grind — the non-billable time system
This is the most concrete operational content in the extract, and it is directly stealable:
- **Fuelling moved to nights only. No morning gas station stops.** "The intention is to get on the property and stay there and be billable."
- **The gate system:** crew leaders and foremen **punch in behind the gate** and pull the truck up (pre-day prep having been done the night before). **Crew members arrive 15 minutes later and wait outside the gate, off the clock.** They punch in when they board the truck.
  - The incentive is deliberate: "**That gives the crew accountability, because the crew leader isn't wasting any time — he's got two guys who aren't on the clock yet waiting for him.** It's going to be an uncomfortable conversation if he's been moseying around while they're not getting paid."
- **Dispatch at 7:00; most trucks out by 7:07.** On the day of the interview, half were gone before 7:00 on the season's second day.
- His justification: "Landscaping is interesting — a lot of other contractors are billable 100% of the time because staff drive direct to a job site. **We're paying them travel time.** We don't look at it like we're reducing their income; we're getting them onto a profitable place faster. **And in doing so we retain the profitability that allows us to have better benefits, give away trucks at 10 years, do profit sharing, have great company events.**"
- His verdict: **"Non-billable time is where a lot of the net profit in the landscape industry disappears."** And: "If you're not profitable, it's probably the easiest thing you can make an adjustment for."

*(Worth flagging: the gate system requires care in any jurisdiction with rules about when time is compensable. See the Canadian note below.)*

## Cash flow traps
- **Divisionalised accounting is non-negotiable.** They classify each service line separately on the books — turf care, landscape, irrigation, snow — "**because you could run a business where half of it's losing you money and the other half's making great money, and you need to know that so you can make adjustments.**"
- **Job costing.** Moved from Service Autopilot to **Aspire** three years ago — "landscape accounting software on steroids... **Aspire really cares about your profitability and shows you very aggregate numbers of how to get there.**"
- **Revenue is the vanity number.** "$15.5 million is impressive... **but it doesn't matter if you're only working at a 30% gross profit margin and the rest of the company is struggling.** And if the company is struggling, the guys are struggling, your equipment's struggling — **and especially it rolls up to the owner: stress load, desire, willingness, burnout.**"
- **Budget before the season, monitor monthly.** "There's nothing worse than getting to the end of the year, working your tail off, sweating in the heat, freezing in the cold — **for the accountant to tell you: hey, you lost money.** You should know if you're making or losing every month along the way."
- **Watch the small leaks:** non-billable labour, un-billed materials, inadequate markups. "Ask the questions of your accountants... understand what overhead is."

## Why owners quit/sell
- Named directly, and it links profitability to burnout: **low gross margin → struggling company → struggling crews and equipment → "it rolls up to the owner: stress load, desire, willingness, burnout to continue like that."** That is the causal chain a buyer should look for in a tired seller — the exhaustion is usually downstream of a margin problem, not of the work itself.

## What sellers hide
- **Ask when the price list was set, and what the crew productivity was then.** The 24 → 14 lawns-per-day decay is the classic silent margin killer. **A seller who priced as an owner-operator and now runs crews may be losing money on their oldest accounts.** This is checkable: ask for route times per property, then compare to the billed price.
- **Ask for gross margin by division, not company-wide.** He is emphatic that a business can hide a loss-making division behind a profitable one. **Demand divisionalised P&Ls — maintenance, enhancement/construction, irrigation, turf care, snow — separately.**
- **Ask for the non-billable time percentage.** If the seller has never measured it, assume it's large. At his scale it was $3,000/day; proportionally on a $1M business it is still real money, and it is **the cheapest post-close margin improvement available.**
- **Ask what proportion of customers are on card-on-file auto-renew versus invoiced annually.** An opt-out service model with cards on file is a materially better asset than an opt-in book requiring re-approval each spring — better cash conversion, better retention, less selling.
- **Ask for the residential/commercial split and why.** He left residential maintenance specifically because **commercial allows better labour control.** A residential-heavy book in a Canadian market has more scheduling variance, more relationship dependency on the owner, and worse route density.
- **Ask about the benefits package and what happens without it.** A business with above-market pay and real benefits has a retention asset you're buying. One without has a turnover problem you're inheriting — and the host's story of a foreman quitting over a single health benefit is the warning.
- **Check the seasonality plan.** He runs "hub and spoke" deliberately to smooth New England seasonality — landscape into snow and back, with irrigation picking up when maintenance slows in drought. **A Canadian target without a real winter revenue line is a seven-month business carrying twelve months of overhead.**

## Verdict
**The best operator video in the corpus on landscaping at scale**, and the single richest source anywhere here on **retention** — which is the binding constraint in every green-industry business.

Four things to carry forward:

1. **The productivity-decay trap (24 → 14 lawns/day).** This is the specific mechanism by which a founder-priced landscaping book becomes unprofitable under hired crews. It is the first thing to test in diligence on any Canadian maintenance route, and it explains a great many "the owner was making $200K and I can't" stories.
2. **Non-billable time as the cheapest margin fix.** Night fuelling, pre-day prep, and a disciplined dispatch time are free to implement and worth real percentage points. Whether or not the gate system is appropriate, **measuring the gap between clock-in and first billable minute is the exercise.**
3. **Divisionalised gross margin, monitored monthly against a budget.** He is unusually clear that revenue is meaningless and that the owner's burnout is downstream of the margin. For a buyer, this is both a diligence demand (give me P&Ls by division) and a post-close operating discipline.
4. **Retention is buildable and it is a moat.** 95% retention and a 30-person waiting list, in an industry where everyone else is permanently short-staffed, came from above-market pay, a real training pipeline, a ten-year truck programme, and — most unusually — **teaching crews personal finance.** In a business where labour is 50%+ of cost and turnover is the default, that is worth more than any piece of equipment.

**US (New Hampshire) — closely comparable to Canadian conditions**, which makes it more transferable than most of the corpus: a genuine snow belt, a hub-and-spoke seasonal model, and commercial maintenance as the core. Three Canadian checks: (1) **the off-the-clock gate system needs legal review** — provincial employment standards on when time becomes compensable, and on travel time, vary and are enforced; **verify with a Canadian employment lawyer before copying it.** (2) **The Ramsey SmartDollar content is US-specific** (401k, US tax treatment) — the *idea* transfers, the *program* would need a Canadian equivalent (RRSP/TFSA-based). (3) **Company vehicles for personal use are a taxable benefit in Canada** (standby charge and operating benefit under the ITA) — the Bleed Blue truck would create a T4 benefit for the employee, so structure and communicate it accordingly.
