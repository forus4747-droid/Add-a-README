## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
The most number-dense operator video in the corpus. He prices commercial snow in **four quadrants**, built on a **45-inch annual snowfall** assumption (metro Detroit) and a **3-inch average storm → 15 plow events per season.**

**Full worked seasonal bid, one commercial site:**

| Quadrant | Rate | Events | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Plow** | **$245** base scrape (1.5–3 in tier) | 15 pushes | **$3,675** |
| **2. Sidewalks** | **$45** per shovel (1.5–3 in tier) | 15 visits | **$675** |
| **3. Bulk salt** | **$240/ton applied** | 30 runs (2× plow events) | **$7,200** |
| **4. Ice melt** | **$30/bag applied** | 30 bags | **$900** |
| Plow + walks | | | $4,350 |
| Salt + ice melt | | | $8,100 |
| **Seasonal total** | | | **$12,450** |

**Note what that table says: salt and ice melt are $8,100 of a $12,450 bid — 65% of the revenue.** "Obviously you make more money in your salt than you do your snow."

**Depth tier multiplier: 1.5×.** Tiers are 1.5–3 in / 3.1–6 / 6.1–10 / 10.1+ (per inch above that). Plow price ladder: **$245 → $365 → $550 → $82.50 per inch.**

**Salt pricing discipline — his industry callout:**
- Bulk salt costs **$80–100/ton**; charge **2–2.5×** = **$180–260/ton applied.** He's at **$240**.
- "Please stop putting bulk salt down at $100, $125, $150 per ton applied." Industry floor should be **$180–200**, preferably **$240–300**.
- Ice melt: stop at $12–15/bag; **$22–25 minimum, $30 preferred.** One bag covers **~1,000 sq ft** — four or five entry doors plus 50–100 linear feet of storefront.
- **Padding built into the salt bid:** a 1-acre lot actually takes **800–1,000 lb** of salt; he **bids it at 2,000 lb**. That's deliberate margin, and he says so.
- **Salt run multiplier: 2× plow events minimum**, prefers 2.5–2.75×. Bid 30 runs to win, 37.5 to be paid properly.

**Where the $245 comes from — the best cost-accounting insight in the operator set:**
- Summer lawn/landscape rate is **$80 per man-hour** using a *multiple* overhead recovery system.
- Winter uses a **single overhead recovery system** over a **4-month window** instead of a full season of thousands of man-hours. Same overhead, a fraction of the hours to absorb it → **the rate roughly triples to $245/hr.**
- **Production rate: a 9-ft 2-in Boss plow covers ~1 to 1.25 acres per hour.**

**Scaling to other markets:** take your local annual snowfall ÷ 45 and scale every number, or rebuild event count as snowfall ÷ 3.

## Staffing reality
- Thin, but the cost side of a flat-rate month is given: **$200–600 of labour, fuel and salt** in a light month (November, March) against a $2,490 billing.

## Winning work
- **Snow conversations start in early July.** "We've been having conversations to get our snow work lined up since early part of July."
- **Two bid postures, stated openly:** bid 30 salt runs to be competitive ("sharpen the pencil"), bid 37.5 to be at retail — "bid it to not get it, or bid it — if you get it, great, because you got the best price."
- Contracts sold at $99 as a product; he notes buyers should have a lawyer adapt them "for your state, province... Canada, America, all that mess."
- Every site is different: plow-only, plow-and-walks with salt on demand, full service. He names churches that "only want salt on Wednesdays and Sundays."

## Cash flow traps — the core of the video
- **The December/January invoice shock.** "Companies have real budgets, they don't want $5,000 invoices in December and January. I wouldn't either."
- **The fix: five equal flat-rate monthly billings, November through March.** $12,450 ÷ 5 = **$2,490/month**. November and March cost him only $200–600 to service, which funds payroll through the shoulder months. "That's called making sure that we are guaranteed income."
- **Current mix: ~40% flat rate, 60% per push**, and he is deliberately migrating clients toward flat rate. "I personally like to have a hybrid system — that way if it snows we get paid good, if it doesn't snow obviously we're still getting some money coming into the checking account which helps pay for payroll and the company overhead."
- **The 45-inch cap clause.** Above the contracted snowfall threshold the contract **reverts to per-push pricing** — his protection against a Minnesota-style 80-inch year. Without this clause a seasonal contract is an uncapped weather short.
- Payment options offered: 50% up front / 50% at 2 months, or the five monthly installments.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not covered directly. His industry-wide complaint is the setup for it: "we're all giving it away still for pennies on the dollar compared to what I think we're all worth." Chronically underpriced snow work is what makes winter a burden rather than a profit centre.

## What sellers hide
- **Get the revenue split by quadrant.** If salt and ice melt are 65% of a properly-priced snow bid, a seller whose snow revenue is mostly *plowing* is either not selling salt or selling it at $125/ton. Both are fixable — and both mean the business is worth less than the revenue suggests until you fix them.
- **Ask what snowfall assumption every seasonal contract was priced on, and whether it has a cap clause.** Uncapped seasonal contracts in a heavy year are how snow companies lose money on their best customers.
- **Ask for the applied price per ton of salt and per bag of ice melt.** These are single numbers that instantly tell you whether the book is priced at industry standard or given away. At $240/ton vs $125/ton on 30 runs, that is a **$3,450 difference on one site** — pure margin.
- **Ask for salt tonnage actually applied vs billed.** He bids 2,000 lb on a 1-acre lot that takes 800–1,000. That padding is real margin — and it disappears the moment a customer audits it or a new owner services the site honestly.
- **Ask for the flat-rate vs per-push mix.** Per-push-heavy books are weather bets; flat-rate books are annuities. Same warning as `extracts/Landscaping-snow-operators/How-to-run-a-Snowplow-Business-Red-flags-pricing-systems-and-what-not-to-do.md`.
- **Ask how winter overhead is recovered.** A seller running a combined lawn-and-snow business who prices winter work at the *summer* man-hour rate is under-recovering overhead by roughly 3×. Their "profitable" snow division may be subsidised by the lawn division.

## Verdict
**The single most useful operator video in the snow/landscaping set** — a complete, transparent, line-item seasonal bid you can rebuild for any market by scaling on local snowfall. Pair it with the Dirt Monkey video for contract structure and receivables risk; between them you have the whole snow business.

Three things to carry into a Canadian snow acquisition: **salt is the profit centre, not plowing**; **the single-overhead-recovery argument for a ~3× winter rate**; and **five equal monthly installments November–March with a snowfall cap clause** as the target contract structure.

**US (Michigan) — mostly transferable, three Canadian checks.** (1) Rebuild the event count from Environment Canada snowfall normals — 45 in is close to Toronto (~48 in / 122 cm) but far below Ottawa, Montreal, Quebec City or snowbelt Ontario, so the plow-event multiplier changes materially. (2) **Salt supply is contracted pre-season in Canada** and shortages in heavy years are real; verify the seller's supply agreement and price exposure. (3) **Slip-and-fall liability**: Ontario's *Occupiers' Liability Act* 60-day notice rule and provincial insurance requirements have no US analogue here — verify coverage, and note that inadequate salting is the liability exposure, which is exactly the line item a seller under-prices.
