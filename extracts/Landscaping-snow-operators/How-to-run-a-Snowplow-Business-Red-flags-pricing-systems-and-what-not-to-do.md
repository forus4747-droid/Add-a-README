## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
**The three pricing structures, and what each does to the operator:**

| Structure | How it works | Who carries the weather risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Hourly** | Bill for time on site | **Contractor.** "Hourly for you guys is great when you have a great year, but if you don't get any snow, you could be looking at having some real difficult times paying your bills." |
| **Per event** | Lump sum per snowfall, tiered by depth | Contractor (no snow = no revenue), but the depth tiers protect margin |
| **Seasonal / monthly** | One annual number, divided across the season months | **Customer** — and this is the one that "keeps you fat and healthy all year long" |

**Per-event depth tiering — the pricing detail worth stealing:**
- Price the property on a **2–3 inch event** as the base.
- **3–6 in:** add **10–20%.**
- **6–9 in:** raise again. **9–12 in:** raise again.
- **Over 12 in:** switches to **time and materials.** "I'm completely covered under any and all circumstances."
- Never quote one flat per-push price for any depth: "it's going to take you three times longer to plow a monster snowfall as it is a minor snowfall."
- **Attach the weather report to the invoice** showing inches fallen. Kills the argument before it starts. "Customers seem to like that."

**Seasonal contract math:**
- Minnesota averages **12–15 snow events**. He bids on **17–19 events** — "that way, I'm protected. And that is literally how we do it inside the industry."
- Season defined as **November through March.** Snow in October or April is **billed on top as bonus revenue.** (Last year: 19 inches in April.)
- **Ice control is a separate and larger business.** "In Minnesota, commercial ice control can be almost double what you would experience with snow plowing and snow removal."

**He runs all three structures simultaneously** — hourly, per event, and seasonal — deliberately: "that way I have all of my bases covered no matter what the season brings my way."

## Staffing reality
- Thin here, but one telling detail: the boneyard is nearly empty because "most of our stuff is out on job sites." The snow business runs on equipment that has to be somewhere.
- Plows go **on early**, months before the season, "to make sure it's not broken... we get all the bugs out of it, that way when the season starts everything is just flowing." Last year the plow stayed mounted until **early June**.

## Winning work
- Bid and estimate **in August**; he considers himself behind doing it in September.
- Customers dislike hourly too — it gives them nothing to budget against. "You may get a little bit of pushback."
- Depth-tiered per-event pricing is sold as a benefit to the *customer* (predictable, auditable against the weather report), which is what makes it winnable.

## The grind
- Winter operating conditions are the constraint on everything: "when it's 15 below and the wind's blowing at 40 mph, you need that piece of equipment to operate. And you need your operator inside of it to be comfortable."
- He buys equipment **specced for winter first**, then strips it down for summer use — "I purchase it with the winter in mind and then peel everything off to use it in the summer."

## Cash flow traps — the most important section
- **The receivable that melts.** His flagship warning: "Make sure you invoice and expect to be paid immediately upon completion... never extend credit further than [a month]. If you extend that credit out and they get a big bill and then all of the snow melts, there's a lot less incentive for the customer to pay you."
- **The story behind it:** a commercial site with a **$10–12,000 end-of-season bill**. The snow melted, **the association dispersed, the complex was sold**, and "Mr. Snowplower was left with no money in his pocket."
- **The under-minimum refund clause.** He holds a contract where **plowing fewer than 10 times means refunding money** — but notes the customer has been paying monthly whether it snowed or not. "This works both ways, good and bad."
- **The over-events clause rarely triggers.** You can write a bonus clause for exceeding the event count, "but a lot of times that clause won't kick in because you just don't get enough snow to activate it." Asymmetric: the refund clause bites more often than the bonus pays.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not addressed directly. But the structure of the risk is the answer: an operator on hourly or per-event pricing with a light winter has no revenue and full fixed costs — the same trap named in `extracts/Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`.

## What sellers hide
- **Ask for the mix across all three structures**, in dollars. A book that is mostly hourly and per-event is a weather bet dressed up as a business; a book that is mostly seasonal contracts is the annuity. This is the single most important question to ask a snow business seller.
- **Ask for snow-event counts per season for 5+ years, alongside revenue.** A seller marketing off a heavy-snow year is selling you the top of a weather cycle. Normalise revenue to the local average event count — and check what *they* assumed when bidding.
- **Read every contract for the under-minimum refund clause.** That's a contingent liability the seller will not volunteer, and it lands on you in a light winter.
- **Aged receivables, specifically the season-end balances.** His $10–12K story is the industry's characteristic bad debt: bills come due exactly when the customer's motivation to pay disappears. Get the collections history, not just the A/R total.
- **Ask whether the season is defined and whether shoulder-month snow is billable.** An operator plowing April at no charge has given away real revenue and will not have it in the numbers.
- **Ask about ice control separately.** If it's "almost double" snow revenue and this business doesn't do it, that's either upside or a licensing/equipment gap. If it does, the salt cost and liability exposure need their own diligence.

## Verdict
The best **contract-structure** video in the snow set, and the only one that treats snow as a *credit and weather-risk* problem rather than a pricing problem. Three things to carry into any Canadian snow acquisition: **bid the seasonal contract above the average event count**, **tier per-event pricing by depth with T&M above 12 inches**, and **collect immediately or you will not collect at all**.

**US (Minnesota) — largely transferable to Canada, with checks.** Minnesota's 12–15 event average is a reasonable proxy for southern Ontario or the Prairies, but the number must be rebuilt from local Environment Canada data for the specific market — Atlantic Canada, snowbelt Ontario and Quebec run far higher. Two Canadian-specific additions he doesn't cover: **slip-and-fall liability and insurance** (Ontario's *Occupiers' Liability Act* amendments impose a **60-day written notice** requirement for snow/ice injury claims — verify current provincial rules), and **salt supply contracts**, which in Canada are commonly locked in pre-season and are a real cost exposure in a heavy year. Note also the video is padded with equipment footage and family banter; the substance is roughly six minutes of a 22-minute runtime.
