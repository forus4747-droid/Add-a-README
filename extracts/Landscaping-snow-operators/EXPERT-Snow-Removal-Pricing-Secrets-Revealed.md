## The costing methodology — the genuinely valuable part
**Labour rate build:** base wage → add overhead recovery (the costs you cannot bill directly: shop rent, lease payments, cell phones, HR, secretaries) → **break-even rate per hour** → add profit % → **billing rate**.

**Equipment rate build — the input list is the best in the corpus** and is directly reusable as a diligence template. For each machine (his example: plow truck + salter):
- Forecastable **billable hours per year**
- Hours in a typical **snow event day**
- **Replacement cost** of the unit
- Current **financing conditions**
- Expected **life expectancy**
- **Resale value** at end of life
- Annual **maintenance and repair** cost
- **Insurance** cost to put it on the road
- Annual **licensing** fees
- **Gallons of fuel per day** and average fuel cost

That produces a **cost per hour and cost per day**, on which the overhead markup and profit percentage are stacked to give the billable rate.

**Site measurement → production rate → hours:** measure the lot by dropping pins in the CRM's site-measurement tool, store the measurements against the customer record (reusable for summer lawn work on the same site), then run the area through a production rate to get hours.

**Bid presentation:** the same estimate can be flipped between **per-push/per-visit** and **flat-rate seasonal with five equal payments**, with line items shown or hidden depending on what the client wants to see. Steve: "every company does it differently... we can help customers who want flat-rate seasonal, per push, or mixed markets who do both."

## What sellers hide
- **Ask for the equipment cost model, machine by machine, using the ten inputs above.** Most snow operators have never built one. If a seller cannot tell you the **cost per hour of their plow truck including replacement reserve, maintenance, insurance, licensing and fuel**, they do not know whether their contracts are profitable — and neither will you until you rebuild it.
- **Replacement reserve is the number nobody funds.** A snow business showing 50% "profit" while running trucks and plows to failure is distributing depreciation as earnings. **Ask the age, hours and expected remaining life of every unit**, then subtract the annualised replacement cost from SDE. This is the single largest adjustment in most equipment-based service deals.
- **Padded salt tonnage is real margin that a new owner may not be able to keep.** He says so plainly. If the seller bids 2,000 lb per acre and applies 800–1,000, that spread is in the P&L — and it disappears the moment a client audits it, a new operator services the site honestly, or salt prices spike.
- **Ask for revenue per man-hour by contract**, not in aggregate. That single ratio ($951/hr on his best estimate) exposes which accounts carry the business.
- **Ask whether summer and winter overhead are allocated separately.** A combined lawn-and-snow business that spreads overhead across the full year is under-recovering in winter (see the single-vs-multiple overhead recovery argument in `extracts/Landscaping-snow-operators/How-WE-Price-SNOW-REMOVAL-100-TRANSPARENT-PRICING.md`). Its snow division may be subsidised by lawn.
- **Ask for the site measurements.** If the seller has square footage and production rates on file for every account, the book is transferable. If pricing lives in the owner's head, you are buying an estimate you cannot reproduce.

## Verdict
**A software demo wrapped around a genuinely excellent costing methodology.** Roughly half the runtime is LMN promotion with affiliate links, and the specific numbers ($11,414.85 at 52.5% net) are one operator's single estimate, not an industry benchmark. Read past that.

Two things make it worth its place in the corpus. First, it is the **only source here that discloses cost, break-even and price on the same snow bid**, which lets you see that a well-priced commercial snow contract can carry **~52% net profit** — and confirms the 50% figure the "big guys" target. Second, the **ten-input equipment cost model** is the most directly reusable diligence tool in the operator half of this knowledge base, and it generalises beyond snow to any equipment-heavy service business — landscaping, pest control trucks, cleaning vans.

The most important buy-side application: **use that model to find the depreciation gap.** A seller running old equipment at high stated margins is not more efficient; they are deferring a capital bill that arrives on your watch, often in the first two winters. Rebuild their cost per hour with a real replacement reserve and see what the margin actually is.

**US (Michigan) — the methodology is jurisdiction-neutral.** LMN is, incidentally, a Canadian company (Ontario), and its production rates and budgeting model are widely used by Canadian landscape contractors, so the tooling transfers directly. The 45-inch snowfall assumption does not — rebuild event counts from Environment Canada normals for the target market.
