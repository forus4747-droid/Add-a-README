## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- **Margin targets:** "industry average is **50% gross**… if I do **40% net**, I'm typically running a 50% gross." A peer in Connecticut reports **60–70% net on snow** in his market. Cross-check offered on air: **"You've got a $3 million company, 50% gross is going to net you about 20%"** — i.e. net collapses as overhead grows.
- **Market rate can far exceed cost-plus:** "your numbers are going to say you can do it for $171, but you're going to do it for **$300 a ton applied** because that's market rate."
- **Lot staking: $4.50 per stake**, or "$200 for 200 stakes — a dollar a stake plus time."
- Snow operator labour ≈ **$35/hour** loaded (separate cost code from landscape labour).

**The tiered seasonal package — his whole sales model:**
- Everything is sold as a **seasonal partnership**, not per-push, "divided over five equal monthly payments."
- Three tiers, presented as a buffet: **10 plows + à la carte salt** (client takes market-rate salt risk) · **10 plows + 25 salts** (locked-in salt price) · **10 plows + 35 salts** (full protection). "I pre-buy, you pre-buy."
- **A ceiling: after 40 inches the contract reverts to per-push and per-ton.** That is the operator's protection against a catastrophic winter.
- He is deliberately abandoning depth-tier multipliers (1.5–3", 3.5–6", 6–9", 9"+ at a 1.5x multiplier between tiers): "when it's snowing five inches, we're there every three inches anyway." Ten visits is ten visits regardless of how you label the depths.
- Four separately-priced quadrants: **plowing, sidewalks, bulk salt, ice melt** — plus lot staking. "Not every client's the same. Some don't have sidewalks, some don't care about the ice."

**Three sales strategies he names explicitly:**
1. **Cut people's legs out** — price to a 10% true net to buy market share and snipe sites.
2. **Know your numbers** — industry-average 50% gross, net varying with overhead.
3. **Market rate** — charge what the market bears regardless of your cost.

## Cash flow traps and seasonality
- **Pre-season pricing as a forcing device:** "All these contracts are good through September 30th. After October 1st all these numbers go up **20 to 30%**." Stated purpose: "lock in the routes. **I don't want to know if my bills are paid in November — I want to know my bills are paid in August.**"
- The perennial fight is "end of October, getting them back to us."
- Contracts for the winter are being written in **84°F August heat** — the snow sales season runs months ahead of the revenue.
- **Never assume delivery:** he sends the proposal as a PDF by email *and* through the software portal, with an explicit "no verbal agreement" line, because "half of the proposals you send that you've been waiting on for three months — they didn't even get it, and now you're three months late and they're wondering if you're serious."

## The grind
Sales call, then a baby appointment, then a three-year-old and a four-year-old — "it just never ends, brother." Quotes, bids, estimates, AP and AR fill Mondays and Fridays.

## What sellers hide / diligence value
- **How you sell it and how you book it are different things.** He sells a flat seasonal price, then flips the job back to per-visit in the software so crews clock every plow, salt and ice-melt visit. Without that, "you have a site log that you visited" but no job costing, no inventory tracking, no idea which contracts actually made money.
- **Direct application:** a snow business selling seasonal contracts with no per-visit job costing genuinely does not know its margin by account. If a seller can't produce visits-per-contract and salt tonnage by site, treat the reported margin as an estimate, not a fact.
- Software honesty note: adopting LMN takes **"50 to 100 hours over the next 3 to 6 months to learn."** A target running on it properly has real data; one that just bought it does not.

## Canadian-specific
US operator (Michigan) with a Connecticut guest. **US — verify for Canada**, but the snow-market structure is directly comparable to Ontario and Quebec. The 40-inch reversion ceiling, five-equal-payment seasonal billing and pre-season price escalation are all standard practice in Canadian snow contracting and are exactly the terms to look for when reading a Canadian target's contract book. Seasonal prepay is also the single best cash-flow feature available in the trade — verify it is actually collected in advance and not billed in arrears.
