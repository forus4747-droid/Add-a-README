## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- **Billing range: $30–$100/hr.** Small (1×/wk, 2 hrs/visit) → **$60–100/hr** to make the drive worth it. Large (M–F, 8 hrs/visit) → **$30–34/hr**.
- **Production rate: 1,000–2,500 sq ft per labour hour** — notably *slower* than the 3,000–4,000 quoted elsewhere in this corpus. Dense cubicles + high traffic = 1,000; big open floors = 2,500.
- **Add a 20–30% time cushion** to the estimate (×1.2) before pricing.
- **Worked example:** building measured on Google Maps at 195 ft × 49 ft ≈ 9,500 sq ft → at 1,500 sq ft/labour-hr = 6.5 hrs → ×1.2 = **7.5–8 hrs** → at **$35/hr = $267/visit** → 3×/wk = **$800/wk** → **$3,200 per 4 weeks** → **13 invoices/yr = $41,700/yr** → **5-year contract ≈ $288,000.**
- **Per-visit price card:** 1 hr = **$80–100**; 2 hrs = **$115**; 3 hrs = **$150**; 4 hrs = **$170**; 5 hrs = **$200**; 6+ hrs = **$35/hr**.
- **Rate-of-thumb: local minimum wage × 3.** ($15 minimum → target $45/hr average.)
- **Labour is 95% of cost.** Supplies: **<$500 startup per contract** (mostly the vacuum), then **1–2% of contract value** ongoing. Floor solution / disinfectant $10–20/gallon.
- **Target margin: 30–50%.**
- Real accounts named: a **Dave & Buster's at ~$13,000/month**, 6 days/wk, 3 people, contract says 5 hours, crew finishing in ~4. Another operator averaging **$39/hr**. A client scaling **$20,000/mo → $42,000 (Dec), $38,000 (Jan), $41,000 (Feb)**.

## Staffing reality
- He uses **subcontractors** deliberately: "you can get the job done a lot cheaper if you're hiring payroll workers, but they're going to require much more management." Subs get paid a fixed rate with no deductions so they take home more and stay.
- **Retention lever: hire the sub who lives near the contract.** "Make sure they live near the contract — that helps a lot with retention."
- The first clean of any building **always takes double the time.**
- Named failure mode: "having employees screw you over because you didn't do the right screening process."

## Winning work
- Bid **by the hour, not by square foot** — "the same 10,000 square foot contract can take double the time in one office compared to another."
- Measure the building yourself with the **Google Maps measuring tool** before the walkthrough; ask the client for their square footage too.
- Take walkthrough notes **section by section** with a time estimate per section.
- **Always send two proposals**: the requested frequency, and a higher frequency at a small discount. "If it's one time a week, why not two times a week?"
- **Round down psychologically**: $3,000 → $2,995; $3,500 → $3,495.
- "Contracts are not won by being the cheapest one, they're won by being the best one."
- Named failure mode: doing walkthroughs and losing to someone else "because you didn't follow a sales process."

## Cash flow traps — the best section of this video
- **Corporate clients expect net 30.** You can sometimes negotiate net 15 or net 10; small contracts occasionally pay up front.
- **The float trick:** invoice the client *immediately* (not month-end), and pay the subcontractor **10 days after** receiving their month-end invoice. "The goal is to get paid first and then we pay the subcontractor, so we're always cash flow positive."
- **Invoice every 4 weeks, not monthly** → 13 invoices/year instead of 12. He claims to have "lost multi-six figures by charging every month." A monthly invoice must be weekly × **4.33**, not × 4 — invoicing at ×4 monthly is a silent **7.6% revenue leak**.
- Losing contracts "because you didn't have any retention practices."

## What sellers hide
- **The 4 vs 4.33 error.** Ask how the seller invoices. A book billed monthly at weekly×4 is under-collecting ~7.6% — that's found money for you, but it also means their historic revenue understates the contracts' true value, and their per-hour rates are worse than they look.
- **Contract hours vs actual hours.** The Dave & Buster's example — contract says 5 hours, crew does 4 — cuts both ways: it's margin today, and it's a scope-cut complaint waiting to happen. Ask for actual clocked hours vs contracted hours per account.
- **Subcontractor dependency.** If the book runs on subs, ask: are they classified correctly, do they have their own insurance and WSIB clearance, and are any of them one client relationship away from taking the account directly? A sub who lives near the contract and services it alone is the person most able to steal it.
- **Net-30 receivables are the whole working-capital negotiation.** A janitorial book with corporate clients carries roughly a month of revenue in A/R. Get the aged receivable listing and set the working-capital peg on it.

## Verdict
Strong on **cash-flow mechanics and contract value**, weaker on cost discipline — his 1,000–2,500 sq ft/hr production rate is materially slower than the 3,000–4,000 used by two other operators in this corpus, which means his hourly prices are not directly comparable. Reconcile the two before trusting any janitorial bid. The durable takeaways for Jay: **one mid-size contract is a ~$288K five-year asset** (this is why recurring janitorial books trade on multiples of contract revenue), **the 13-invoice year**, and **the get-paid-before-you-pay float**.
