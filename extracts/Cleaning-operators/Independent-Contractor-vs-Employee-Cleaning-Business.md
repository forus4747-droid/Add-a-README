## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- **Year 1: $0 → over $300,000 in sales**, from two sisters cleaning solo to **five or six employees** by month twelve.
- Current: **"multiple six figures, very close to $500,000."**
- **Current total overhead: under $2,500/month**, including a full-time VA, ad platforms, and software — after going fully subcontractor.
- **The wage conversion: W2 cleaners at $17/hour became 1099 subcontractors at $35/hour** — but they now buy their own supplies and get themselves to jobs.
- Overhead she eliminated: multiple **storage units at ~$150/month each** across NJ and NY, vehicles (purchase, insurance, gas and toll reimbursement), overtime, sick days, benefits, supplies, laundering rags, driving unlicensed staff to jobs.
- **Supply inflation, specific:** Easy-Off oven cleaner from under $3 to **~$7 a can** by 2024. "All of the residential cleaning supplies... I can say with conviction that they have doubled in price."
- **The finding that started everything:** looking back over four years of numbers, "even before my sister left the business I wasn't making as much money, bringing home as much money, as I was in year one and year two." Revenue grew; take-home shrank.

## Staffing reality
- **The classification switch was a cost decision, not a legal one.** W2 brought overtime, sick days, benefits, vehicles, insurance, gas and toll reimbursement — "my expenses were growing as fast as my business was growing."
- **She ran a 7–8 month transition**, not an overnight switch: told staff early, helped them register businesses, get insurance policies naming her as additional insured, get driver's licences and pass road tests, and offered to help them build their own websites and marketing.
- **The interim fix after her sister left:** she gave her best cleaners **+$3/hour** to absorb the operations work — lead training, washing rags, restocking supplies, quality checks.
- **The control substitutes she built** (because subcontractors can't be directed like employees): partner only with cleaning businesses she knows and trusts; **no re-cleans and no monetary refunds**, published on the website; instant quoting and booking; client walkthrough while the cleaner is still on site; photo and video documentation; ZenMaid checklists; **subs are not paid until the 24-hour complaint window closes**, and a return visit is unpaid.
- End state: **"I am 100% hands off with my cleaning business. I don't even answer the phone anymore"** — a VA does admin, she runs ads.

## Winning work
- The price increase was forced and overdue: "I hadn't raised my prices in years." She did fresh market research on competitors, raised prices, told legacy clients, **and lost some.** "That wasn't an issue for me — I know how to find more clientele."
- The principle behind it, stated bluntly: **"You shouldn't be in business just to give your technicians work... I am in business to make money, I am not in business to just pay people."**

## The grind
- She had **not been paying attention to the numbers** during fast growth. No QuickBooks, no professional accountant, taxes filed but books never examined. "All I saw was I was making a lot of money, but at the end of the year, how much of that was I actually profiting?"

## Cash flow traps
- **Revenue growth outrunning margin.** The single most transferable lesson: years 3 and 4 were bigger and paid her less than years 1 and 2. Every added employee added a vehicle, insurance, fuel, tolls, overtime and a storage unit.
- **"You can have a million dollar business and be negative at the end of the year."** Her refrain — *know your numbers* — is the whole video.
- **Not raising prices through an inflationary period** while supply, fuel and toll costs roughly doubled.

## Why owners quit/sell — the most honest passage in the corpus
- Her **sister and 50/50 partner left the business abruptly**, taking the entire operations function with her: hiring, training, supplies, restocking, inspections. There was no succession plan for a partner.
- The fallout was personal, not just operational: "it really did take a toll on my mental health, on my personal well-being... I fell into this really deep depression and I almost completely gave up on my cleaning business. I hated it."
- **She self-sabotaged for eight or nine months** — not marketing, "staying in bed," "almost unraveling my business that we built together."
- What saved it was people, not systems: mentors and a peer community who told her not to sell and not to let it go under.
- **This is the shape of a real seller.** An owner whose co-founder walked out, whose margins have been quietly eroding for two years, who hasn't raised prices, and who has spent most of a year disengaged — that business will come to market cheap and look worse than it is. It is also exactly the business where the numbers on the P&L understate what a disciplined operator could earn.

## What sellers hide — and the biggest Canadian landmine in this corpus
- **A cleaning business running on "subcontractors" is carrying an unquantified reclassification liability.** Everything she describes as a control system — mandated checklists, photo documentation, a published no-refund policy, withholding payment for 24 hours, unpaid return visits — is evidence of *control*, which is precisely what tax authorities test for. She converted her own former employees, doing the same work, to contractors. That fact pattern is the textbook reclassification case.
- **In Canada this is not a $17-vs-$35 arithmetic question, it is a CRA exposure question.** The CRA and provincial authorities apply a control/integration/tools/chance-of-profit test, and a cleaner who services the company's clients, on the company's schedule, to the company's checklist, is very likely an employee regardless of what the contract says. Reclassification brings back **unremitted CPP and EI (both portions), interest and penalties, plus WSIB/WCB premiums and vacation/stat pay** — and those amounts can attach to the business and to directors. **This liability follows a share purchase and can follow an asset purchase.**
- **Diligence actions:** get a CRA statement of account and a payroll compliance review; get each subcontractor's business number, GST/HST registration, WSIB clearance certificate and their own insurance; check whether any sub works exclusively for the target; request any CRA ruling (CPT1) obtained. If the seller has never sought a ruling, price the exposure.
- **Ask for take-home, not revenue.** Her own test: "people will tell you all the time 'oh I own a million dollar cleaning business, I'm doing $750,000, I'm doing $10 million.' That's awesome — but how much are you netting?"
- **Ask when prices were last raised, and by how much.** A book that hasn't been repriced since 2021 has a hidden margin recovery in it — and a hidden churn event when you take it.
- **Ask what happens if the operations person leaves.** Her whole crisis was one person walking out. In a small service business, find out who actually runs the field, and whether they're staying.
- **A sub-$2,500/month overhead figure is only credible alongside the classification risk.** Do not model an asset-light cleaning company at her overhead until you've priced the payroll liability she has moved off the balance sheet.

## Verdict
**Two videos in one, and both matter.** The operational half is the clearest account anywhere in the corpus of *how a growing cleaning business gets less profitable every year* — vehicles, storage, overtime, benefits and reimbursements scaling faster than revenue, with no bookkeeping discipline to catch it. The human half is the best primary-source description of **why a service business ends up for sale**: a partner leaves, the owner's mental health collapses, the business drifts for nearly a year, and the numbers look terrible by the time anyone opens the books.

For Jay, the diligence takeaway is sharp and specific: **when a Canadian cleaning company's margins look impossibly good, check the payroll line first.** The most common explanation is not superior operations — it is that the workforce has been reclassified as contractors, and the liability has not gone away, it has only gone unrecorded. Price it, indemnify it, and hold back against it.

**US (NJ/NY) — the tax mechanics do NOT transfer to Canada.** "W2" and "1099" have no Canadian equivalent as forms; the concepts map to **employee (T4) vs independent contractor (T4A / invoicing)**, and the Canadian test is stricter in practice than the US one. Verify with a Canadian employment lawyer and a CPA before relying on any part of the restructuring described here.
