## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
Full worked model for a **10,000 sq ft office, 5×/week**:

| Line | Figure |
|---|---|
| Production rate (standard maintenance clean) | **3,000 sq ft/cleaner-hour** (range 2,500 slow – 4,000 fast) |
| Cleaner wage | **$20/hr** |
| Labour cost per sq ft | $20 ÷ 3,000 = **$0.0067** |
| Overhead per sq ft | **$0.0040** |
| **Total cost per sq ft** | **$0.0107** |
| Price at 50% markup | $0.0107 × 1.5 = **$0.016/sq ft per session** |
| Price per session | $0.016 × 10,000 = **$160** |
| Hours on site | 10,000 ÷ 3,000 = **3.33 hrs** |
| Labour per session | 3.33 × $20 = **$66.60** |
| Overhead per session | **$40** |
| Total cost per session | **$106.60** |
| **Profit per session** | **$53.40** (33% of price) |
| Monthly revenue (5×/wk × 52 ÷ 12) | **$3,466** |
| Monthly cost | $1,443 labour + $866 overhead = **$2,309** |
| **Monthly profit** | **$1,157** |

Note the gap between the *stated* "50% profit margin" and the actual **33% of revenue** — the 50% is a markup on cost, not a margin on price. Anyone quoting "50% margin" in this industry may mean either. **Always ask which.**

## Staffing reality
- Production rate *is* the staffing model. A cleaner who does 2,500 sq ft/hr instead of 3,000 costs you 20% more labour on the same contract — at $0.0067/sq ft that's the difference between profit and break-even.
- He frames faster cleaners with better equipment as a choice: bank the efficiency as profit, or pass it to the cleaner as higher pay. "The happier they are the happier your customer."
- The failure loop he names is precise: underprice → can't pay the cleaner properly → cleaner does poor work → client complains → owner spends energy backwards instead of selling.

## Winning work
- Being cheapest is not just unprofitable, it's **suspicious to the buyer** — "if you are too cheap they likely won't take you because it looks suspicious."
- Be transparent about how the price is built; it builds trust.
- If quoting hourly, use an **NTE (not-to-exceed)** cap: "it normally takes 3 hours 33 minutes, but I know I will not go over 4.5 hours." Clients hate open cheques.

## The grind
- Underpricing means the owner cleans the building himself: "now you become a glorified cleaner, not a business owner."

## Cash flow traps
- **Overhead omitted from the bid.** "If not properly done this will poke a hole in your ship and you will start bleeding." His overhead list: advertising, owner salary, insurance, office, vacation, admin, printing, Wi-Fi, computer, tools. At $0.004/sq ft it's **37% of total cost** — more than a third of the bid — and it's the line amateurs leave out entirely.
- Specialty work (deep clean, disinfecting) has a **lower** production rate; pricing it at the maintenance rate guarantees a loss.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not addressed directly, but the described spiral — underbid, self-perform, firefight complaints, no time to sell — is exactly the exhaustion path described in `extracts/Operator-burnout/`.

## What sellers hide
- A seller quoting revenue per contract tells you nothing. **Ask for square footage, frequency, and hours actually worked per visit** on every account. Then compute their implied production rate. If it's above 4,000 sq ft/hr, either the crew is being run into the ground or the scope isn't being delivered — and the complaint will arrive on your watch.
- A seller who has never allocated overhead per account cannot tell you which of their contracts are profitable. Rebuild every account from this formula before you believe a portfolio-level margin.

## Verdict
The cleanest arithmetic in the corpus for **janitorial unit economics**, and the source to use for the OPERATORS.md cleaning table. Two numbers to memorise: **3,000 sq ft per cleaner-hour** and **overhead ≈ 37% of total cost**. Caveat the wage — $20/hr is a US mid-market figure; in Canada budget provincial minimum plus payroll burden (CPP/EI/WSIB/vacation pay), which typically lands $18–24/hr all-in in Ontario. Use this model as the audit tool for any janitorial business Jay looks at: recompute every contract, and the seller's "profitable book" will usually shrink.
