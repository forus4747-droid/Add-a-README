## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
**Margin benchmarks — the clearest statement in the janitorial set:**
- **Janitorial recurring accounts: 20–30% target.** "We try to stay as low as 20, upwards of 30% on janitorial accounts."
- **Floor care and tag work: higher.** "That's where you're going to make some extra cake on top of that."
- **Smaller accounts carry higher margin percentages; bigger accounts carry lower** — the same inverse relationship as `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Price-Commercial-Cleaning-RIGHT-Cleaning-Bid-Calculator.md`.
- **Market wage drives price:** "Washington State's one of the highest minimum wages in the nation, so we have to pay more to get the right people on board — so our prices are going to be higher. **There's no exact science to it.**"
- **A brand-new operator can justifiably take 10–12% margin work** to get cash flowing: "as long as you're making money on it, I think it's a win... get that money saved up so you can get yourself an employee, then start working on bigger accounts for higher margins."

**The account-quality threshold — the most useful single number here:**
- Early years: one-time-a-week accounts, one hour a night, **~$600/month**. His verdict: **"the profit is not going to keep the lights on."**
- **Today: nothing under three days a week — preferring five, six or seven — with a $1,650/month minimum.** "We know that there's going to be X amount of profit on there that's going to pay for other things."
- **The transition happened around the two-year mark.**

**The niche that changed the business:**
- Got a data centre contract by luck at ~18 months. **Nine years later: from 1 → 5 → 7 → nearly 50 data centres in a single cluster.**
- Scale difference: a normal account is **1–2 hours/day**; data centres are **8, 12, or 24 hours a day.** "It's basic janitorial for the most part until you get into the server areas. There's nothing new to learn — it's just on a bigger scale." The learning curve on the back end (compliance, access, documentation) was "huge."
- **Two data centres won through LinkedIn: "seven figures between the two of them."**

## Staffing reality — the strongest section, and it contains a real cautionary tale
**Employee of the Month backfired badly.**
> "We started out with employee of the month. The first couple were great, and then **they started quitting** — the employee of the month. We got to the fourth employee of the month that quit. I finally got in touch with her: 'You're a rockstar, what's going on?' **It's just the tension that's created with the staff** — this person thought they should have been employee of the month... there was resentment from the staff towards this person. Rather than confront it, they just left and went elsewhere."

He then reasoned through the alternatives: birthdays were out (some religious groups don't celebrate them, creating a new exclusion). He landed on **work anniversaries** — **"nobody can be upset about an anniversary, because that person put that time in."**

**That recognition programme, posted publicly on LinkedIn, won him the business.** A data centre manager who had refused him for eight years reached out:
> "**Love the culture and the way you take care of your employees.** I think it's time we sit down and have a chat." He walked Eaton through the scope **and told him what the incumbent was charging.** "We love the staff, we love them like family — **we just don't like the company that they work for.** We know you're going to be a little bit more expensive, but we know you're going to take care of them."

The existing cleaners transitioned to Eaton's payroll the next day. The reason they were willing: **"this company never checked in with the employees... 'Hey, we've been out of gloves for three months, what's the deal?' 'Oh, we'll get it to you' — and they never do."**

The manager then referred the data centre across the road — same incumbent, same problems. Two contracts, seven figures, from posting work anniversaries.

**Team evolution:** started as husband and wife doing anything — one-time-a-week accounts, residential, final construction cleaning ("jumping in and out of houses because they were laying concrete"). First manager was **an existing employee, 23–24 years old, shadowed intensively then relocated 300 miles** to run the data centre cluster. "I don't have to micromanage him. I just check in from time to time."

**Leadership development:** a monthly peer group of local entrepreneurs (including new business owners), plus a **life/business coach hired last year** whom he also brings in to work with staff. He's candid that he didn't believe in it at first, and that a famously blunt member of his peer group was visibly changed by it.

## Winning work
- **Specialise.** The early lesson: "there's times my wife and I were jumping in and out of houses... it was like, what are we doing? **We just need to focus on one thing** — and it's commercial." His reasoning: residential means the homeowner is present; commercial is "more laid back, you just do your thing."
- **Bid with a calculator, always.** His anecdote: an operator brought him a price for their first large account. How did you get it? *"This other person I talked to, they've been in the industry three years, said do this and times it by two."* Eaton: do you know your tax rate in there? Your overhead? Your profit? **"They had no clue."**
- **LinkedIn is free and underused.** "Social media is huge and it's free and people forget about that. **But it's the message that you put out there — it's the story that you tell.**"
- Universities now approach him directly — "the president, the vice president, the athletic director all on this Zoom" — to decide between in-house and outsourced.

## The grind
- Eleven years to get from a two-person operation taking any work to a business with account minimums and a manager running a 50-site cluster.
- Honest about luck: **"they say the harder you work the luckier you get. We were busting our butts and all of a sudden I get a call for a data centre."** He is explicit that the alternative path is "two, three, four years of really hustling until you can get those right accounts."

## Cash flow traps
- **Accounts that lose money invisibly.** The hosts' observation: *"I just found out, after servicing this account for 18 months, that we've been losing $200 a month on it. It just got lost in the client list."*
- **Low-frequency accounts consume management attention out of proportion to revenue** — the reason for the three-day/$1,650 floor.
- Bidding without knowing payroll burden, tax, insurance and overhead percentages — "they almost throw out a cheap price, and then they realise they're not making any money."

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not directly addressed. But the incumbent contractor in his story is the picture of a business about to lose everything: neglected staff, unanswered supply requests, no client check-ins, and a client actively shopping for a replacement it will pay *more* for.

## What sellers hide — this episode is unusually rich here
- **Ask for the account list with frequency, monthly value and margin — sorted ascending.** Eaton's own threshold (3+ days/week, $1,650/month minimum) is a usable benchmark. **A book full of one-day-a-week $600 accounts is revenue that does not carry overhead**, and it will consume the new owner's management time. Those accounts may need to be shed, which means planned revenue decline post-close.
- **Ask which accounts they have never margin-tested.** The "$200/month loss for 18 months" scenario is normal, not exceptional. If the seller cannot produce per-account profitability, assume some of the book is negative.
- **Ask about supplies and equipment spend per account, and check it against the crews.** "Out of gloves for three months" is what a starved account looks like from the inside — and it is a leading indicator of a contract about to be lost. **Walk sites and ask the cleaners what they're short of.**
- **Ask when the client last heard from the owner.** The winning pitch here was not price — the incumbent was *cheaper*. It was that the client believed the staff were being mistreated. **Client relationship depth is the asset; a seller who hasn't visited a site in a year has already lost it, they just don't know yet.**
- **Ask whether any recognition, bonus or incentive scheme exists and how it has gone.** Eaton lost four of his best cleaners to a well-intentioned Employee of the Month. In an industry where a book is only worth what its crews will keep delivering, an incentive scheme that breeds resentment is a real, findable liability.
- **Ask what happens if the field manager leaves.** One 24-year-old relocated 300 miles runs nearly 50 sites. That is the whole data centre business in one person. Identify the equivalent person in any target and find out whether they are staying.
- **A concentrated niche cuts both ways.** Fifty data centres in one cluster is a moat and a concentration risk simultaneously — the same tension as the multi-service business in `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`. Ask who the ultimate client is behind the sites; fifty contracts with one operator is one contract.

## Verdict
**The best janitorial *scaling* video in the corpus** — the pricing videos in this folder tell you how to bid one account; this one tells you which accounts are worth having and what a book looks like after eleven years of discipline. It's long (57 minutes) and roughly a third is football talk and four separate sponsor reads, but the substance is strong.

Three things to carry into a Canadian janitorial acquisition:

1. **The account-quality screen.** *Minimum three days a week, minimum ~$1,650/month, 20–30% margin.* Run any target's book through it. What fails the screen is either a repricing opportunity or revenue you'll deliberately shed — either way it changes the valuation.
2. **Contracts are won and lost on how the *cleaners* are treated, not on price.** This is the single most transferable insight for a buyer: the incumbent in his story was cheaper and still lost two seven-figure sites. In diligence, **talk to the crews and ask what they're short of** — and after closing, understand that supplies, uniforms and responsiveness to staff *are* the client-retention strategy.
3. **Recognition programmes can destroy retention.** Individual awards created resentment that cost four top performers; tenure-based recognition (which nobody can dispute) worked and became a marketing asset. Cheap to implement, and directly relevant to the turnover problem every operator in this corpus describes.

**US (Washington State) — nothing jurisdiction-dependent except wages.** His high-minimum-wage argument transfers directly to Ontario and BC, where minimum wage plus payroll burden sets the floor on janitorial pricing (see the Canadian burden-rate note in `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Price-Commercial-Cleaning-RIGHT-Cleaning-Bid-Calculator.md`). One Canadian addition worth checking on any commercial janitorial book: in several provinces, **building-services contract workers have specific successor-rights protections on a change of contractor**, meaning incoming contractors may be required to offer employment to the incumbent's staff and recognise their service. That would turn the "staff transitioned next day" story from a courtesy into an obligation — verify provincially before assuming you can re-staff a site.
