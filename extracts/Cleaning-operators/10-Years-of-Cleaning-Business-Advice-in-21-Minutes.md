## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- **Residential recurring client: ~2-year average retention, ~$3,000 lifetime value — and he is explicit that this is a GROSS PROFIT number**, i.e. after paying cleaners, not revenue. That distinction makes this one of the few genuinely comparable LTV figures in the corpus.
- Recurring weekly/biweekly service: **~$150–200 per clean.**
- One-off move-in/move-out job: **$500–600.**
- The comparison he wants you to make: a $600 one-off is smaller than $3,000 of accumulated gross profit — "the value of that recurring client blasts out any of these one-off jobs."
- **Recruitment funnel: ~100 applications → 1 hire.**
- Margin reality check: "we're certainly not a high-margin technology business... you can make a really good income and a really good margin in the cleaning industry, but you need to do a few things right."

## Staffing reality — the strongest section
- **"Ultimately this is a people management business"** — and he says he did not appreciate how hard that side would be.
- **100:1 application-to-hire ratio, and you are always recruiting.** "We would always be recruiting, we would always be running interviews."
- **Post-offer melt is severe and is the number most people miss:** candidates who accept the offer and never show for the first job; candidates who work two or three jobs then start turning up late, develop an attitude, or refuse feedback. "It was incredible and crazy to me how many of those would actually fall out of the process."
- **The hiring lesson he reversed:** he started recruiting from five-star hotels for the experience — "we wanted to bring the five-star hotel experience into the home." It failed. One early hire was technically excellent but dismissed client feedback and became defensive; "on paper she was a fantastic cleaner but the softer skills were hard."
- **He shifted the emphasis to soft skills** — attitude, punctuality, response to feedback — over hard experience. "Someone who didn't have the experience but had a fantastic attitude and wanted to get better and would take on client feedback... was actually a far better fit."

## Winning work
- **Specialize; do not be a generalist.** This is his single most emphasised point. House cleaning *or* office cleaning *or* windows *or* carpets — never several.
- **The reason is pricing power, stated precisely:** if company A and company B both offer everything, "there is no pricing power between those two companies. The market will set the price." Specialists have fewer true comparables and can price above the market rate.
- **Why the price matters:** the margin above cleaner wages is what funds marketing, recruitment, an office manager, and the owner's own pay. "If your pricing is set too low to begin with there is just not enough profit to do these types of things — you'll never get the business up and running."
- Specialization compounds: easier marketing, easier recruitment, sharper website, clearer job description for staff.
- **The website is the storefront.** He contrasts the tens of thousands spent refurbishing a restaurant with the few hundred people spend on a website. Track conversion with Google Analytics; A/B test a **simple lead-capture form vs a full online booking form with a credit card**.
- **Marketing attribution is the discipline, not the channel.** Try everything — Google Ads, Facebook, flyers, door hangers, newspaper — but **track by channel or you are guessing.** He admits he used to put money into flyers and Yelp and judge by whether the week felt busy.
- **The attribution trick: channel-specific coupon codes.** `FLYER20` for $20 off first clean, `FACEBOOK20` on the Facebook ad. Doubles as an offer and as tracking.
- **His answer after testing everything: Google.** Google Ads + Google Local Services Ads + SEO. The logic is intent — "you would go to Google 'house cleaners near me'. You wouldn't be browsing through Facebook, you wouldn't be flicking through videos on TikTok, you wouldn't be driving past a yard sign."

## The grind
- The hard part is not cleaning, it is the permanent recruiting treadmill at 100:1 with high post-offer attrition, plus the pull to accept any revenue in the early days. "At the beginning when you're trying to get revenue in... it is hard to turn down work from other areas."

## Cash flow traps
- **One-off jobs consume management time out of all proportion to their revenue.** A three-bedroom house across town takes about the same time as a three-bedroom house here; two post-construction jobs can differ wildly in scope. "There were just far more variables that could go wrong."
- **Recurring clients are nearly management-free after the first two or three cleans.** That is the real scalability argument, and it's a labour-cost argument, not a revenue one.
- Underpricing at the start is structurally self-limiting: no margin → no marketing/recruitment spend → no growth.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not addressed directly. The implied failure mode: a generalist with no pricing power, a permanent hiring crisis, and a book of one-off jobs that each need managing personally. That owner has bought themselves a job with no exit.

## What sellers hide
- **"LTV" needs a unit.** He quotes $3,000 as *gross profit* over two years. Most sellers quoting an LTV mean *revenue*. On his own numbers, $150/week × 104 weeks is ~$15,600 of revenue producing ~$3,000 of gross profit — a **~19% gross margin per client.** Always ask: revenue or gross profit, and over what retention period.
- **Ask for the generalist/specialist split.** A cleaning business doing residential *and* commercial *and* carpets *and* post-construction has no pricing power in any of them and is competing on price in four markets at once. Its margins will not improve without shrinking its revenue.
- **Ask for the recurring vs one-off revenue split, and the management hours behind each.** A book that is 40% one-off looks like more revenue and is materially more work — and it does not renew. Same warning as `extracts/Cleaning-operators/STOP-Guessing-Prices-Exact-Formula-to-Price-Your-Office-Cleaning-Services.md`.
- **Ask for the recruitment funnel numbers**: applications, interviews, offers, no-shows, 30-day survival. If the seller can't produce them, they don't have a hiring system — they have the owner personally persuading people to show up, and that walks out the door at closing. At 100:1, a business that needs three hires a quarter needs 300 applications a quarter flowing in.
- **Ask what share of leads the seller can attribute to a channel.** If the answer is "word of mouth and referrals," the pipeline is the owner's personal reputation, which is not transferable. If it's Google Ads with tracked cost per booked client, it is transferable and you can model it.
- **Average customer retention is the number that sets the price.** Two years is his figure for residential. Get the actual cohort retention from the seller's scheduling software, not their recollection.

## Verdict
The most complete **business-model** view of residential cleaning in the corpus, and the best counterweight to the commercial-cleaning pricing videos that dominate the rest of this channel folder. Three things to carry into diligence: **the ~$3,000 gross-profit LTV over ~2 years** as the residential benchmark, **the 100:1 hiring funnel with heavy post-offer melt** as the true staffing cost, and **specialization as the only source of pricing power in a commodity trade.**

That last point is the acquisition thesis in disguise: **a generalist cleaning company with weak margins is a turnaround, not a bargain.** If Jay buys one, the fix is to shed service lines — which means deliberately shrinking revenue before margins recover. Model that dip, and make sure the debt service survives it.

**Geography is unstated** (the phrasing reads British or international; the currency is dollars) but nothing here is jurisdiction-dependent except the marketing channels, which apply unchanged in Canada. The one Canadian addition: **Google Local Services Ads availability and the Google Guarantee vary by country and service category** — verify coverage for the target city before assuming his channel strategy transfers.
