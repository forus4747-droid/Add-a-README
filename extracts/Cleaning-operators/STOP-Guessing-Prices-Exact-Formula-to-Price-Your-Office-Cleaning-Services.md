## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
Operator grew **Mastery Cleaning to over $6M revenue since 2017**.

- **Billing range: $35–$100/hr**, driven by frequency and hours per visit. Never below $35/hr — "I pay my cleaners very good."
- **Production rate: 1,000–3,000 sq ft per labour hour.** Deep clean drops to **1,000 sq ft/hr**; normal cleaning ≈ **1,500 sq ft/hr**.
- **Small contract** (2 hrs, 1×/wk): **$60–70/hr → $500–700/month.**
- **Large contract** (8 hrs, M–F): **$35/hr → $5,000–6,000/month.**
- **Worked example:** 3 hrs/visit × 3×/wk at $150/visit ($50/hr) = **$450/wk** → invoiced every 4 weeks = **$1,800 × 13 = $23,400/yr**; invoiced monthly = $450 × 4.33 = **$1,948 × 12 = $23,382/yr**. Same annual total, but "$1,800 every four weeks" *reads* smaller than "$1,948 a month."

**Subcontractor pay card — the cost side of the same accounts:**

| Visit length | Sub is paid | Implied rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 hr | $35–50/visit | $35–50/hr (drive premium) |
| 2 hrs | $50–75/visit | $25–37/hr |
| 3 hrs | $80–100/visit | $27–33/hr |
| 4 hrs | ~$100/visit | ~$25/hr |
| 5+ hrs | $22–25/hr | $22–25/hr |

Baseline sub pay is **$20–25/hr**; short visits get a premium purely to cover the drive. **Target margin: 40% minimum.**

- **One-time jobs:** $50/hr minimum, 5-hour minimum. A 15,000 sq ft deep clean prices at **$500–800** depending on filth. Cap the hours in the quote and bill **+$50/labour-hour** beyond the cap.
- **Post-construction for a production builder** (8–10 houses/month): **clean each house twice** — first pass 8–12 hrs, touch-up the following week 6–7 hrs — billed at a flat **$37/hr**, invoice total varying monthly.

## Staffing reality
- Prefers subcontractors: no deductions, so take-home is higher and they stay. Paid **once a month, 10 days after invoice**.
- Sells the client on staffing quality as the differentiator: "we require at least 5 years experience, we have inspectors and a regional manager."
- If you're doing the work yourself you may be forced down to **$25–30/hr** — which is the arithmetic of being an owner-operator rather than a business.

## Winning work
- **The cheapest-quote objection script** (the most reusable thing here): tell the prospect what buying the cheapest quote actually gets them — unreliable minimum-wage workers, a new cleaner every other week, inconsistent quality, facility security risk, and potentially undocumented workers whose injuries could land on the client. Then contrast with your own: experienced cleaners, good pay, inspectors, a regional manager, problems fixed before the client notices.
- **"Most clients are not concerned about the price"** — they want the provider who makes the problem go away. Restaurants and retail are the exceptions: thin margins, price-sensitive.
- **Competitor price-check trick:** call three competitors posing as a prospect with a specific spec ("3 hours per clean, three times a week") and get a phone quote before your own walkthrough.
- Job type drives the rate: medical (OCD-level standards), factories and heavy manufacturing take longer and must price higher.

## The grind
- Post-construction is the hardest work described: 8–12 hour first passes, then a second pass because inspectors and trades keep re-dirtying the house. "The first clean doesn't usually cover everything."

## Cash flow traps
- **The 4-week float, again:** invoice the client, collect, *then* pay the sub 10 days after their month-end invoice. "That allows me to get paid first and then I pay the subcontractors — so I'm always cash flow positive."
- **One-time jobs without an hour cap** are the classic loss-maker; the +$50/labour-hour overage clause is the fix.
- The 4.33 multiplier again — invoicing monthly at ×4 leaks ~7.6%.

## What sellers hide
- **Ask for the sub pay card, not the average.** A seller quoting "we pay $22/hr" is hiding the short-visit accounts where the true rate is $35–50/hr. Those accounts look high-revenue-per-hour and are actually the thin ones.
- **Ask what share of revenue is one-time work** (deep cleans, post-construction, move-outs). It carries premium hourly rates, so it flatters the blended margin — and it does not renew. A book that is 30% one-time work is worth materially less than the same revenue in recurring contracts.
- **Post-construction revenue is builder-cycle revenue.** A single builder client at 8–10 houses/month is customer concentration dressed up as recurring revenue. When housing starts fall, it goes to zero.
- Cross-check the seller's claimed production rate: **1,000 sq ft/hr for a deep clean vs 3,000 for maintenance** means the *same* building at the *same* price is either a good account or a disaster depending on scope. Get the scope of work document for every contract.

## Verdict
The most complete **price-and-cost pair** in the cleaning set — it gives both the billing card and the subcontractor pay card for the same accounts, so you can compute the actual spread: roughly **$35–50/hr billed against $22–25/hr paid on large accounts (≈40% gross), and $60–100/hr billed against $35–50/hr paid on small ones**. That confirms the economies-of-scale-in-reverse pattern from the bid-calculator video. Use the sub pay card as the diligence benchmark. **US-centric; the subcontractor model is the risk item for Canada** — the CRA's control/integration test would likely reclassify a sub who cleans one route on the company's schedule with the company's supplies, and reclassification brings back source deductions, CPP/EI, and penalties. Price that exposure into any Canadian janitorial deal that runs on "subcontractors."
