# Losing a Major Client? How to Rebuild Your Cleaning Company — Mike Campion

## The situation
A listener writes in: he is **losing his largest customer in 60 days — a client representing 70% of both revenue and staffing needs.** The whole episode is the post-mortem and the prevention.

## Unit economics and hard rules
- **Concentration rule: "if any client is more than 15–20% of your total revenue… we start selling the pants off it right now."** 50%, 60%, 70% is treated as an emergency in advance, not after.
- Coaching target: **25% net profit**. When you lose 70% of revenue with the same cost of goods sold percentage, "you're just going to have to cut half your overhead."
- **Commercial sales cycle: 2–3 months minimum for decent accounts; 6–9 months for the good ones.** "You're not selling for now. When — not if — they're unhappy with their cleaner, instead of going out to market they just call you."
- Small easy accounts (a dentist, a chiropractor): **~$500/month, one person, a couple of hours, once a week** — obtainable fast, precisely because they are unsophisticated and the office manager has been doing it herself.
- Recovery timeline scales with size: "if that 70% represents $3,000 a month we can do that in three or four months; if it's $60,000 it might take six or nine months."

## What sellers hide — the concentration trap
The best passage in the corpus on why a big contract is a liability:
- When a big opportunity appears, owners **lower their price and act desperate**, which repels good clients: "if they know it's somewhere between five and seven grand and you come at them at $3,800, they're probably not going to hire you — or if they do, they're just going to use you for a couple of months until they can't stand it any more and fire you."
- The Walmart/Costco pattern: year one you quadruple revenue on thin margins and feel good; **year two "they know they're your biggest vendor and they know that they own your company"** and dictate a 10% cut. You cannot walk because you have hired the people and rented the space.
- "When you take one client that's 30, 50, 80% of your business, **they own your business as much or more than you do — but you're on the hook for all the risk.**"
- The right frame: **20 customers making up the same revenue.** "The likelihood of all 20 leaving in a single month is very low. The likelihood of [the one big client] leaving all at once is almost 100%."

**Direct application to buying:** a listing showing one anchor client at 30%+ of revenue is not a stable business with a great customer — it is a business whose price is being set by someone else. Ask for revenue by client for three years.

## Why one-time work doesn't rescue you — post-construction and move-outs
Asked whether the listener should take post-construction work to bridge the gap, the answer is a detailed no, and it explains a whole category:
- **It is easy to get precisely because nobody good wants it.** "There are only two types: people that quickly figure out post-construction doesn't work, or people that slowly figure out post-construction doesn't work."
- Cash flow: **you front all the labour and materials, there is no credit check, no collateral, a thousand contract clauses excusing non-payment, and if they do pay it's months later.**
- Staffing: **"we need 300 hours of work this week and then no work for six weeks."** Move-in/move-out apartments are the same — 32 units today, two tomorrow, none for a week, then 16. You cannot build a crew or a culture on that.
- Scope risk: another vendor comes in after you and re-dirties the site; the site isn't ready on the day you have staffed for; "what I thought I was bidding was X but it's really XYZ."
- "I've been doing this for a decade and coached thousands of people — I've never met a multi-million-dollar post-construction business. We have tons of clients with multi-million-dollar regular janitorial businesses that have a real asset they can sell."
- His advice to the listener in a cash crisis: **get a part-time job rather than take one-time work** — "you're not building anything and it's super risky."

## The grind / mindset
- "The quality of your business is limited by the quality of your customer." Taking whoever throws money at you is the rookie move.
- Only accept **recurring clients**: the risk of underbidding a one-off is dispersed over $500; the same effort on a recurring client is a potential ten-year, million-dollar relationship.
- On panic: "don't try and solve a six-month problem in a month and then beat yourself up when you fail."
- Repeated warning against goal-less growth: without a revenue target broken down into leads → bids → clients, a big contract acquires emotional power over you, and that is what makes owners underprice.
