## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
Operator built a commercial cleaning company **beyond seven figures in under 5 years** (Asheville, NC).

**True labour cost build-up — the most valuable thing in this video:**

| Line | Figure |
|---|---|
| Market rate for evening cleaners (Asheville) | **$13.00/hr** |
| **+10%** deliberately, to be above market | +$1.30 → **$14.30/hr** |
| **+14.11% payroll liabilities** (payroll taxes, state UI, federal UI, workers' comp) | → **$16.32/hr true cost** |
| Price at 35% gross margin | $16.32 ÷ (1 − 0.35) = **$25.11/hr billed** |
| Supplies | **2–3% of revenue**, absorbed inside the margin |

**Production rate:** 3,000–4,000 sq ft/hr for offices. 4,000 = low density, low traffic. 3,000 and below = more desks, more offices, higher visit frequency per desk, more foot traffic.

**Monthly price formula:** `efficient cleaning hours × profitable hourly rate × cleanings per week × 4.33`

**Margin scales inversely with account size** — his markup chart is by *weekly hours*. Above ~66 weekly hours he drops to 35%. Small accounts carry higher margins because "smaller accounts take almost just as much management to take care of as larger accounts."

## Staffing reality
- The **+10% above market rate is a deliberate policy**: "we want to attract good people — we don't want to play at the bottom of the barrel." In an industry where labour is ~95% of cost, this is the whole strategy expressed as a percentage.
- Sourcing the market rate: Indeed listings, or PayScale (~$100 for a market report).
- **14.11% payroll burden** is the number most first-time buyers of a cleaning company forget entirely.

## Winning work
- Scope a bid on **three** variables, not one: **size, density, traffic.**
  - *Size:* square footage.
  - *Density:* count of offices, exam rooms, bathrooms, toilets — and how often each space actually needs a full clean vs a daily check. A medical facility is full-clean-everywhere-every-day; a large office may need a serious desk clean once a week.
  - *Traffic:* ask the prospect for daily visitor/patient counts; count parking spaces on the walkthrough or from Google Maps. Four-to-six spaces per door across a strip is a completely different job from one building with a big lot.
- **Bid large accounts at large-account margins or you will lose them.** "If you go into a large facility and bid it at a profit margin of a small facility, the odds of you winning it are going to be very low... larger facilities know this."

## Cash flow traps
- Confusing **gross margin with net profit**. He is explicit: the 35% is what's left *after paying cleaners and staff* and must still cover office, owner salary, overhead and marketing.
- Bidding off wage rather than **true** hourly cost: the gap is 25.5% ($13.00 → $16.32). Bid off $13 at "35% margin" and your real margin is roughly 16%.

## What sellers hide
- Ask for the **payroll burden rate** the seller actually pays and reconcile it to the P&L. If their books show labour at wage-only, there are unrecorded liabilities (or contractors who should be employees — see `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Independent-Contractor-vs-Employee-Cleaning-Business.md`).
- Ask for **margin by account, sorted by weekly hours.** If the big accounts carry small-account margins, they were mispriced and are at renewal risk; if the small accounts carry big-account margins, they're losing money on management overhead.
- Ask what the seller pays **relative to market**. A seller at or below market has a turnover problem you'll inherit; a seller at +10% has a labour cost you can't cut without breaking the service.

## Verdict
The single best **cost-side** formula in the corpus, and it pairs exactly with the square-foot model in `Pricing-Commercial-Cleaning-Jobs-by-Sq.-Ft.-Like-a-PRO.md` (which prices from cost) — use both. Two things to carry into diligence: **true hourly cost = market wage + 10% + payroll burden**, and **margin must decline with account size**. **US — verify for Canada:** the 14.11% burden is US payroll taxes + state UI + workers' comp. The Canadian equivalent is CPP + EI (employer portions) + EHT (ON, over threshold) + WSIB/WCB premiums + statutory vacation pay + stat holiday pay, which typically runs **15–20%** and is *higher* than his figure. Rebuild the burden rate for the province before using this formula on a Canadian target.
