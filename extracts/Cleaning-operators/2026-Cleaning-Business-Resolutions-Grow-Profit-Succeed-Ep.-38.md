## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- **Cost-of-living increases average ~3%/year.** Their argument: you can lift profitability on *existing* clients by **9–12% or more** by selling specialty services into contracts you already hold. "That's low-hanging fruit."
- **Window cleaning cited at ~70% margin** — one of the named high-margin specialty lines, alongside carpet and tile.
- **Payroll is the single biggest expense line on the P&L** — "other than maybe health insurance... actually I would even say the biggest expense line you have is payroll."
- Retention math they walk through: losing 2 accounts/month, gaining 3, at ~$500/account. Their verdict on that ratio: **"the ship is sinking"** — a business churning 24 accounts a year to net 12 has a service problem, not a sales problem.

## Staffing reality
- **Build a competency sheet and tie raises to it.** "You can't have a system where 'Joe got a raise because Matt really likes Joe.' Joe got a raise because Joe's been doing all of his competencies." Objective, published criteria for advancement — this is the retention mechanism.
- Rate each employee 1–10 and use the annual review to decide **who to train up and who to replace.**
- Framing: "your job as an employer is to make sure your teams are well equipped and trained to the maximum... that is an investment you must work on."
- Tool named for field reporting: **OrangeQC** — technicians file inspection notes that route to everyone instantly, which doubles as **upsell lead generation** (see below).

## Winning work — the specialty-services upsell engine
This is the most useful operational idea in the episode.
- **Capture every measurable at the initial sale**: floors, carpet, tile, window counts. Otherwise, when a client calls asking for carpet cleaning, you pay someone to drive out and measure — and they measure only the one room. "Now we can't sell to that."
- Their analogy: a call centre verifies your name, address and contact details as a matter of workflow. **Do the same with building measurements.** If a field is blank, the system should flag it, because a blank field is a service you cannot quote.
- **The account-manager gap is real revenue:** "How many companies do I talk to where they say 'yeah we clean next door, we do all their stuff' — and the people next door say 'we clean over here too but we don't do any of the...' *I didn't know you did that.*" One account manager sells everything; another says "they're paying, so they're good."
- **Use the inspection reports to trigger the sale:** "James was at the building and I saw his report — those windows are filthy, but James didn't say anything." Then make the call.
- **Know your most profitable service lines by name and margin**, and chase those specifically.

## The grind
- Their honest framing of why none of this gets done: "this is some boring stuff... you're either confronted by what you don't know, or confronted by having people not know that you don't."
- The owner-education point, unusually direct: "A lot of business owners look at training for my employees, but then you need to look at yourself. **I really don't understand how to measure square footage. I don't understand how to sell to people. I need to get better at that.** If you get better, then your employees are going to get better."

## Cash flow traps
- **Contracts that go unrenewed for years.** They admit to going "a year plus without renewing contracts." The consequence is stated by a client: *"You need to renew me every year so we can have predictable increases, because I have to go to a board and show my increases. You can't give me a 22% increase because you didn't do it for 5 years."* **Deferred price increases become un-passable price increases.**
- **Not knowing which accounts are profitable.** Their annual review process: list every account, flag which are outside the renewal window, and score each on profitability after labour and materials. That list is your call sheet.
- **Uncaptured measurements** = unquotable specialty work = permanently forgone margin.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not directly covered, but the retention arithmetic is the mechanism: an owner losing 24 accounts a year while winning 36 is running hard to stand still. That treadmill, plus unraised prices compounding against inflation, is how a janitorial business becomes exhausting and unsellable.

## What sellers hide — the best diligence idea in this episode
- **"No complaints" is not a green flag.** Their line: *"I'd rather have someone who's complaining than someone who's not complaining, because they're complaining to someone. If they're not talking to you, they're talking to your competition."* When a seller tells you their clients never complain, that is a data-collection failure, not a quality signal. **Ask instead: what complaints did you receive last quarter and what did you do about them?**
- **Measure retention by renewals, not by survivors.** Their point is sharp: "the measurement of a retention goal should come from renewals... how many of those people actually signed a new contract for the next 12 months." A client who hasn't cancelled but also hasn't re-signed in three years is not retained — they are unattached. **Ask for the renewal date and last signature date on every contract in the book.**
- **Ask when each account last had a price increase.** A book that hasn't been repriced in five years carries an unrecoverable increase: clients on board-approved budgets cannot absorb 22% at once, so the margin is permanently lost unless you raise gradually over years — which delays your return.
- **Ask which specialty services each client buys and which they don't.** The gap between "services offered" and "services sold per account" is quantifiable upside — and if the seller can't produce that grid, it also tells you the account management is unsystematised.
- **Ask for churn by account, not the net number.** Three won and two lost nets to +1 and hides a 30%+ annual churn rate. Get gross gains and gross losses separately.
- **Ask whether raises are formula-driven.** A business where compensation is discretionary and relationship-based will see wage demands and departures the moment the owner leaves.

## Verdict
**Middling as content, useful for three specific diligence questions.** Roughly a third of the runtime is membership-platform promotion and New Year's-resolution philosophising, and there are almost no hard numbers. But what survives is genuinely good, and it fills a gap the pricing videos in this folder don't cover: **what happens to a janitorial book over years of neglect.**

Carry forward: (1) **"no complaints" is a red flag**, not a green one; (2) **retention means renewals signed, not clients who haven't left**; and (3) **the deferred-price-increase trap** — unraised contracts don't just cost you the missed increases, they make future increases unpassable because commercial clients budget annually against a board.

The specialty-services upsell mechanic — capture measurements at the initial sale, use field inspection reports as upsell triggers, and track service penetration per account — is a concrete, cheap value-creation lever for any janitorial business Jay buys. On their own figures it's worth **9–12% versus a 3% COLA**, which on a $1M book is $60–90K of found revenue at high margin. That is a real post-close plan, not a platitude.

**Geography unstated, likely US** (health insurance as a major P&L line is a US tell). Nothing here is jurisdiction-dependent; all of it applies unchanged to a Canadian janitorial book.
