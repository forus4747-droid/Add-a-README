## Key numbers
- Capital gains inclusion rate in Canada: **50%** since 2001 (the long-standing baseline).
- Budget 2024 *proposed* **66.67% inclusion above $250,000** of annual gains for individuals, effective June 25 2024; first $250K stays at 50%.
- Worked example: $300K gain, top marginal rate **53.5%** → $80,250 tax at 50% inclusion vs **~$84,700** under the two-tier proposal (~$4,500 more).
- **Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption raised to $1.25M** on qualified small business corporation (QSBC) shares.
- Proposed Canadian Entrepreneurs' Incentive: an extra **$2M lifetime at 33.33% inclusion**, phased in $200K/yr from 2024 to 2034 → theoretical $3.25M of full/partial relief, break-even vs the old regime at roughly **$6.25M** of gain.
- Corporations: no $250K threshold — proposed 66.67% on **all** corporate capital gains. Said to touch ~12.6% of corporations with gains.
- Salary comparison: $70K salary ≈ $17K tax vs $70K capital gain ≈ $4,500 tax (2023) — the whole reason sellers fight for capital-gains treatment.

## What to look for
**Green flags**
- Target is a **QSBC** (Canadian-controlled private corporation, ≥90% active business assets at sale, ≥50% for the 24 months prior). This is what unlocks the LCGE and is exactly why sellers push for a *share* sale.
- A seller who has been tax-planning for years — purified corporation, family trust, multiple LCGE claims across spouses/kids. A prepared seller is usually a prepared business.

**Red flags**
- Seller discovers mid-deal that their corporation is not QSBC-pure (excess cash, rental property, investments sitting in the opco). They will suddenly demand a higher price or restructure — expect months of delay.
- A seller who is entirely indifferent to structure usually has no accountant involved; their numbers deserve more scrutiny, not less.

## Canadian-specific
- This is **Canada-specific and current-as-of-2024 only**. The 66.67% inclusion-rate increase was subsequently deferred and then abandoned by the federal government; the **Canadian Entrepreneurs' Incentive was dropped with it**. **Verify current status with a CPA before relying on any inclusion-rate number here.** The **$1.25M LCGE did survive** and is the one figure to plan around.
- The LCGE is the single biggest reason Canadian sellers insist on **share sales**, which is exactly the opposite of what a buyer wants (see `extracts/Valuation-craft/Structuring-a-Business-Sale-to-Minimize-Taxes-Asset-vs.-Stock-Sales.md` — note that video is US, but the buyer/seller tension is identical).
- Practical buy-side consequence: in Canada, expect to be asked to buy **shares**, not assets. Price the difference. Every dollar of LCGE the seller preserves is a dollar of tax you can claw back in the purchase price — or trade for a bigger VTB.
- Principal residence exemption stays 100% — relevant when a seller's "retirement plan" is really their house plus the business.
- Registered accounts (RRSP/RRIF/TFSA/FHSA/RESP) shelter gains entirely — irrelevant to the deal, relevant to where the seller parks proceeds.

## Verdict
Useful for **one durable fact — the $1.25M LCGE** — and for understanding *why the Canadian seller across the table wants a share deal*. Everything about inclusion rates in this video is stale politics; do not quote the 66.67% figure to anyone. Treat this as background on seller motivation, not as tax advice. The real takeaway for Jay: in Canada the tax tail wags the structure dog, and the LCGE is your strongest lever for negotiating a share purchase down in price or up in VTB.
