# Making $50,000 Per Month And I Still Almost Quit My Business

- **Industry caveat up front:** this is an **e-commerce/craft/creator business**, not a service business. The unit economics are irrelevant to Jay. **The burnout mechanics are not** — and this is the most detailed first-person account of owner burnout in the corpus, which is why it earns its place.

## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- Started **October 2022** with embroidered sweatshirts, moved to printed apparel, then vinyl cups (which went viral).
- **Revenue at peak: $15,000/month floor, with months at $20K, $30K, $40K, $50K, $60K.**
- Dual income streams: product sales plus content creation, sponsorships, and gifted equipment from manufacturers.
- **Working 10-hour days, Monday to Friday plus weekends**, waking at **5:00 am** "for no particular reason."
- **The collapse:** depression identified around **August 2025**; a "slapped-together" fall collection produced under duress; **no winter collection at all** — "you know, as a small business, that is huge, that is where the money is, and I could not force myself."

## Staffing reality
- **There was no staff.** She names the audience precisely: "the business owners who do everything themselves — all the marketing, all the product listing, all the product making, all that stuff." This is the single-point-of-failure structure that every small service business Jay looks at will share.

## The grind — the core of the video
**The escalation treadmill, described exactly:**
> "When I made my first $10 to $15,000 a month I was on top of the world... But then I started this vicious cycle of always having to one-up myself. So now it's time to make $20,000 a month. And when I hit that I was happy for a second until I set the next goal of $30,000. I hit that and I was happy for a second... **So I just was never happy. It was never good enough for me.**"

**The guilt trap that delays the diagnosis:**
> "When this depression snuck up on me, I felt so ungrateful and I couldn't understand why I felt the need to complain when I have everything I want... **I was extremely cruel to myself.** 'Why are you being such a — there's nothing to cry about.'"

**The physical escalation:**
> "In my entire life I have never suffered with anxiety. But during that time I was living in constant stress where it manifested into physical symptoms... **physical chest pain**... it truly started affecting my health to the point where I couldn't even work if I wanted to."

**The line that matters most for a buyer:** *"If you don't surrender, life will make you surrender. Life will force you to stop."*

**What she identifies as the root cause** — and it is a business diagnosis, not just a personal one:
> "I stopped choosing what I truly wanted to do, and instead I **traded it off for products that were going to sell well even though they made me miserable**, and taking on as much work as I can even though I was working myself to the ground just to make more money."

She also names **FOMO-driven capex** — buying machines because other creators had them, not because the business needed them. In a service business this is the truck, the trailer, the equipment upgrade that never earned its keep.

## Cash flow traps
- Not a financial video, but note one thing a buyer would see in the numbers: **a business whose peak season simply didn't happen.** Anyone looking at these financials in 2026 would see a catastrophic Q4 and assume market or competitive decline. The actual cause was the owner's health. **Financial statements do not distinguish between a failing business and an exhausted owner** — and that gap is where a buyer's opportunity lives.

## Why owners quit/sell — this is the whole document
Every element of the classic seller profile is here in the owner's own words:
1. **Money was not the problem.** $50K months and a custom office. "I had everything I could possibly want."
2. **The business consumed the identity.** "My hobby turned into my business, and I forgot to search for other hobbies outside of that."
3. **Denial ran for months** before the crash — journaling started in August, the crash came at the holidays.
4. **The peak season was abandoned**, which is the visible symptom a buyer would notice.
5. **Recovery is slow and incomplete.** "Even months later, I am still not completely 100% over the burnout."
6. **The eventual resolution was to shed the operating business** — no more phone cases, cups, mouse pads, apparel or accessories; pivot to courses, digital files and information products.

That last point is exactly the shape of a seller. Not a failing business — a **profitable business its owner can no longer bear to run.** Compare with `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Independent-Contractor-vs-Employee-Cleaning-Business.md`, where the same collapse follows a partner's departure and the owner self-sabotages for eight or nine months before recovering.

## What sellers hide
- **A sudden revenue drop is not always a market signal.** Ask directly: *"Was there a period in the last three years where you stepped back from the business, and what happened to revenue?"* A drop caused by owner disengagement is recoverable by a fresh operator; a drop caused by competition or losing a major client is not. **They look identical on a P&L.**
- **Ask what the owner has stopped doing.** She stopped marketing, stopped posting, stopped launching, and fulfilled orders only. In a service business the equivalent is: stopped bidding new work, stopped raising prices, stopped replacing lost accounts. Terry Lammers calls this **"the glide path"** (`extracts/David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`). It is the single most common condition of a business that comes to market.
- **Ask about the owner's health and hours, gently but directly.** Someone working 10-hour days plus weekends at 5am has a business with a labour input the financials do not show. When you replace them, you will need to hire that back — often as more than one person.
- **A seller in this state may accept non-price terms that a rational seller wouldn't** — a longer transition, a bigger VTB, an earlier close. They may also change their mind entirely once they've rested. **Don't mistake exhaustion for commitment to sell.**
- **The flip side, and the ethical line:** an exhausted owner is not a mark. Buying well from a burnt-out seller means paying a fair price and giving them a clean, fast exit — which is genuinely what they need. The Divitkos playbook (`extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`) — keep the name, keep the staff, be a real succession plan — is the right posture here, not merely the effective one.

## The prevention checklist she rebuilt — worth keeping for the day Jay owns one
- **Rest is the treatment, and it has to be learned.** "I didn't know how to do nothing. I really didn't."
- **Set actual working hours.** She cut from 10-hour days to a fixed **12:00–5:00 shift**, with hours added back gradually.
- **No weekends.** No work after hours. **No work inside the home** — she identified editing videos on the couch before dinner as a major contributor.
- **Fixed wake time** (8am alarm) with enough sleep that she wakes naturally.
- **Exercise before every shift**, non-negotiable; walking pad and standing desk for the sedentary hours.
- **"If the work doesn't get done during that work day, it waits until the next work day."**
- **Downsize deliberately** — fewer handmade products, fewer machines, because those consumed the most time.
- **Have a hobby that is not the business.** Hers was gaming: "my hobby turned into my business, and I forgot to search for other hobbies outside of that."
- **Journaling**, specifically because "as a business owner we are always in motion — there are a thousand tabs open in our mind."
- **Ask for help.** "Pride will be the death of you."

Compare Divitkos' four pillars — therapist, business coach, peer group (EO/YPO), and a mentor-investor not on your board. Between them, these two videos are the corpus's answer to the question of how not to become the seller.

## Verdict
**Off-thesis industry, on-thesis subject — and it earns its keep twice over.**

**As a diligence lens:** this video explains, in the owner's own voice, why a profitable business shows up on the market with declining recent numbers and a seller who can't quite articulate why they're selling. The peak season that never happened, the marketing that stopped, the product line that was never launched — those all show up in the financials as decline, and a buyer who reads them as *market* decline will walk away from a good business, while a buyer who reads them as *owner* decline will find the opportunity. **The diagnostic question is: what did the owner stop doing, and when?**

**As a warning:** the failure mode described here — escalating targets, no rest, hobby collapsed into business, identity fused with revenue, pride preventing asking for help — is exactly what happens to a first-time acquirer who buys an owner-operated service business and tries to be everything at once. Divitkos said it takes a therapist, a coach, a peer group and a mentor. She learned it by having chest pains at $50K a month. **The point is not that burnout is a risk; it is that burnout is the modal outcome for someone who does everything themselves.** Building the business so the owner is replaceable is not a luxury — it's both the exit strategy and the survival strategy.
