## Key numbers
- Lammers took a family fuel-and-lubricants business from **$750,000/year in sales to over $42 million** in ~20 years, via **11 acquisitions**, then sold in 2010 to a **$6 billion** agronomy co-op. A completed roll-up, start to exit.
- Starting point in 1991: "me, my mom and dad, we had two trucks and it was a good day if they both started."
- **LOI to closing on the exit: six weeks** — for a business assembled from nine different companies.
- Ran a **$4 million line of credit** as an operator; later worked as a commercial lender, so he has seen the deal from all three sides.
- The one redundancy across 11 acquisitions: **one office employee, retained for a further year until retirement**, on a salary of ~$40,000.
- Largest acquisition came with **~10 employees**; most had **one or two**.

## What to look for
**On confidentiality — why it matters, precisely**
- Employees "get their business education from headlines," so *business is for sale* reads as *business is in trouble*. Most live paycheque to paycheque and have no buffer for uncertainty.
- Barnett's sharpest point: **in any industry and any community, competitors already know who the best workers are.** If a top employee starts looking, "a competitor might decide to hire them just to hurt the other company." Leaked news costs you exactly the people you're buying.
- Small-town amplification: "we live in a very rural area... especially today with texting and cell phones." News travels, and reputational damage attaches to *you* before you've started.
- Lammers' practice across 11 deals: **most staff told on the day of closing**; key employees brought in earlier only where integration required it.

**The key-employee dilemma — an unresolved, real problem**
Two weeks before closing on an environmental drilling company, the question surfaced: the seller couple could vacation for two weeks because **two key employees ran the business**, and the buyer (an engineer/MBA with no drilling experience) *needed* them. Options were:
1. Put employment agreements with non-competes in front of them, possibly with payment attached; or
2. Say nothing about paper, announce a profit-sharing plan instead, and let it ride.

They chose (2). The reasoning is worth memorising: **"These are blue-collar workers, they've never signed an agreement. If you throw that piece of paper in front of them, one, you're probably going to delay things because they'll want an attorney to look at it — but what's the chance they say 'the heck with you, I'm not going to work for you' and leave? That could kill your deal."**

There is no clean answer here. But the *timing* insight is: raising this two weeks before closing is far too late. **Identify who the business cannot run without, in week one of diligence.**

**How NOT to tell employees**
Barnett's story: an acquirer sent an IT technician on the Friday before a Monday close to begin network integration — **with 14 sets of usernames and passwords for a company of 18 people.** Everyone did the arithmetic instantly.

**The first 90 days — don't come in with "Blazing Saddles"**
- "No matter how much you tell these new employees everything's going to stay the same, the first thing in their mind is *what am I getting paid, am I going to lose my job.*"
- Hold back on changes. "Go have a barbecue, everybody gets to know each other." Same for newly-acquired customers.
- **Then listen, because the employees have a list.** Barnett: sellers' "enthusiasm, excitement and passion is starting to wane, and the employees often know this — they work in a sleepy tired place and maybe they even bring new ideas forward that the owner doesn't want." After closing, "employees have stepped forward and said here are all the things we've been wanting to do — the new computer systems, the trade shows we've been trying to get into but the owner didn't want to."
- Lammers' term for the seller's final years: **"the owner gets on a glide path. He quits investing in new equipment, he quits investing in technology... you're just like a glider coming down."** That decline is the buyer's opportunity — and it's also why the announcement should be delivered with genuine growth excitement.

**Firing tenured, customer-facing staff is more expensive than it looks**
The $40K office employee was kept for a year: "the customers knew her, she worked there 20+ years... you don't want to create that toxic environment." **"What is that [$40,000] weighed out versus getting all the knowledge that's in her head from working there 20 years, and not upsetting customers?"** In a small community, the reputational cost of an early firing damages the very goodwill you paid for.

**Non-financial diligence — the theme of the episode**
- **Key-person age risk, with a live example:** a manufacturer who can spend two months in Florida — but "his office lady is in her 60s and the main guy who sets up his production machine is in his mid-70s." For a strategic buyer that's survivable; **for an individual buyer it is the whole deal.** "That mid-70s guy — if he quits, who's going to reset up that machine?" *The acquisition requires a succession plan for a machine operator.*
- **Employment agreements and their transferability.** Lammers found an inherited salesman's employment agreement had **no transferability clause**, so it simply did not survive the sale — the salesman didn't know. He left it alone for four years, then renegotiated it as "outdated, from the previous employer." Check every agreement for assignment/transferability language.
- **Union status can kill a deal.** One target was unionised while his company was not: "it wasn't gonna work... that alone took six or eight months to get straightened out." It only proceeded because attrition dropped the bargaining unit from three members to two, decertifying it. **"I wouldn't have been able to buy that company otherwise."**
- **Culture before financials.** Lammers: "I have a friend living in Canada — before she'll buy a company, that's the first thing she looks at, before she looks at the financial statements." Compare with `extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`, where Divitkos argues you largely *can't* see culture from outside. Both are right: look hard, and still assume you'll be surprised.
- **Toxic ownership structures are real.** On his last company, Lammers sold 20% across two people (10% to the incumbent CEO, 10% to an outside hire). "Those two didn't get along and it created a very toxic environment."

**Banking lessons from both sides**
- **"Always talk to more than one bank."** He watched his own bank impose moratoria on entire sectors — construction, non-owner-occupied real estate, certain farm loans — driven by both sector dislike and portfolio overexposure.
- **The nightmare scenario he witnessed and names:** an owner-occupied real estate loan with a **balloon coming due that the bank simply refused to renew.** "Imagine being a business owner and all of a sudden you have a note that renews, the bank says they won't renew, and you still owe all this money." Barnett: "I know three people who've been through it." **It happens more than people believe.**

**Labour shortage as a structural condition**
- The apple packer who bought a palletising robot: *not* because it was more efficient, but "we can't hire people, we can't literally find people — and when we can get people we have other more important things that only humans can do."
- Both agree the shift over 20 years is dramatic: employers used to keep a stack of walk-in resumes. **Boomer retirement plus a genuine shortage of carpenters, HVAC techs and trades means existing staff are an asset, not a cost line.**

## Canadian-specific
- **Barnett is Canadian (New Brunswick) and makes the single most important Canadian legal point in this corpus on employees:**
  > **"Where I live, labour issues follow assets, not companies. So even in an asset sale, certain labour things can actually follow to the new owner."**
- This is a real and material difference from the US default. In most Canadian jurisdictions, **employment standards legislation deems continuity of employment on a sale of a business — including an asset sale.** Accrued service, notice and severance entitlements travel with the employees to the buyer. Under Ontario's *ESA*, for example, employment is treated as continuous where a purchaser employs the seller's staff; **Ontario severance can run up to 26 weeks** for long-service employees at qualifying employers. A workforce with twenty-year tenures carries a large, quantifiable, and often unrecorded liability.
- **Union successor rights are stronger in Canada than in the US.** Provincial labour relations acts contain successor-employer provisions that bind a purchaser to an existing collective agreement — in many cases in an asset sale too, not just a share sale. Lammers' escape via decertification would be far harder in Canada.
- **Diligence actions for a Canadian deal:** obtain every employment agreement and check for assignment clauses; calculate the aggregate termination/severance exposure by tenure; get union certification status and any collective agreement; confirm WSIB/WCB standing; and negotiate an indemnity plus holdback for pre-closing employment liabilities.
- Both of Barnett's banking warnings apply directly in Canada: **talk to more than one bank**, and treat any **balloon or demand facility** in the target's existing debt as a live risk, since Canadian small-business credit is heavily demand-based.

## Verdict
**One of the strongest Tier 1 videos in the corpus, and the only one that treats employees as a diligence category rather than an afterthought.** Lammers has done eleven acquisitions and one exit; Barnett has brokered and lent. Neither is selling a course.

Four things to carry into a Canadian service-business acquisition:

1. **Identify the irreplaceable people in week one of diligence, not two weeks before closing.** In a janitorial, landscaping or pest control business those people are the crew leads and the one person who knows every building — and they are usually not on any org chart.
2. **Do not paper key employees at the last minute.** A non-compete thrust at a blue-collar employee days before closing can end the deal. If you need retention agreements, they need to be part of the deal's design from the start, negotiated through the seller.
3. **The "glide path" is the thesis.** A tired owner who stopped investing is exactly the business worth buying — and the staff already know what needs doing. Announce with growth, then listen for ninety days before changing anything.
4. **In Canada, employee liabilities travel with the business — including in an asset sale.** Price the accumulated severance exposure, get an indemnity, and hold back against it. This is the one item on this page that a US-trained buyer will miss entirely.
