# Seller Financing To Buy A Business — Vendor Notes (VTB) in M&A Explained

- **Channel:** David C Barnett · **Track:** A · **Duration:** 35:47
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MJWnQfOl8cI

## Key numbers
**This is the reference item for VTB mechanics. Canadian context throughout — the presenter is an M&A advisor selling $1M–$50M businesses and names CIBC and Scotiabank as the senior lenders.**

- **Standard VTB size: 10–25% of purchase price.** Worked example: $1M deal = $200K cash equity + $600K senior bank loan + **$200K VTB** to bridge the gap.
- **Interest rates: 3–5%** for a low-risk buyer (strategic, competitor, wealthy investor); **5–8%** for a higher-risk or thin-equity buyer. "On average I'm seeing 5–6%, as high as 8, as low as 3."
- **Payment frequency: usually annual**, sometimes quarterly. **Monthly amortisation is rare** — buyers won't accept it, the point is breathing room while they learn the business.
- **Typical amortisation: 1–3 years non-amortising, most commonly a 2-year interest-only period followed by a 2–3 year amortisation.** Some COVID-era deals ran 3 years interest-only then a bullet at 36 months.
- **When VTBs go far above 25%:**
  - *Industry risk* — "banks in Canada aren't really favourable of those industries." A salon/spa seller was told he could have his price only at a **40% VTB**, which he accepted.
  - *Seller/business risk* — environmental issues, key-owner transition risk. "I've seen situations where VTBs are 60, 70, 80% of the purchase price."
  - *Seller preference for yield* — one client sold to a strategic buyer at a **100% VTB, no cash down, at 8% for five years**, because his bond portfolio was returning 4–5%.
- **Bank equity requirement: 20–40% of deal value.** A deferred VTB counts toward it — example given: **VTB 30% of deal, true cash equity only 10%, producing ~40% annual returns on the cash actually invested.**
- Personal guarantees appear in "**only 10% of our deals**."
- Price-adjustment mechanisms typically share **10–50%** of the variance (example uses 20%).

## What to look for — the five reasons a buyer wants a VTB
1. **Financing the gap** without more equity.
2. **Deferred equity.** If structured interest-only for **2–3 years (3 to be safe)** and postponed, the bank treats it as patient capital sitting behind them — which lets you borrow more *and* pay more. This is the single most important structural point in the video.
3. **Skin in the game.** "If I give you all cash you can go off into the sunset and don't really care if I succeed. But if I'm still owed $200,000, I want to be damn sure I get repaid — so I'll provide the right training and stay available."
4. **Offsetting post-closing purchase price adjustments** — the ~90-day working capital true-up is netted against the note rather than exchanged in cash.
5. **A de facto escrow for disputes.** "I thought I was buying a million dollar business when in reality it was worth a hundred thousand" — you set off the claim against the note instead of suing for money already gone.

**Seller-side security stack (what a seller will ask for, so know what you're conceding):**
- **General Security Agreement (GSA)** registered against the corporation; a **second mortgage** if real estate is in the deal.
- **Pledge of shares** — second charge behind the bank. Matters because "the very first thing a bank does when a loan goes bad is turn around and ask the seller to take this over and turn it around."
- **Personal guarantee** (rare, 10%).
- **A leverage covenant** — debt/EBITDA not to exceed 3–4x, breach triggering acceleration or a rate step-up. He explicitly advises against DSCR covenants as over-complicating.
- **Standstill / postponement agreement** — the price of getting equity treatment. The seller agrees the bank can defer even the *interest* if the business underperforms.

## Four VTB structures
1. **Plain vanilla** — e.g. $500K, 5% annual, 5-year term, 24 months interest-only, then amortising ~$166,667/year over years 3–5.
2. **Price-adjustment mechanism** — the note flexes with performance against a target. Worked example: $500K note, $100K/yr amortisation, $3M sales target, 20% share of variance. Sales of $3.1M → seller gets $120K that year; sales of $2.8M → $60K; sales of $2.7M → $40K. *"A very fair way to negotiate uncertainty in the future"* — used where the future is genuinely uncertain (his example of a declining business: a printing company).
3. **PIK (paid-in-kind)** — no cash interest; interest capitalises and compounds. $500K at 5% → $525K → $551,250 → $578,813. Used for high-growth buyers preserving cash, or a desperate seller. High risk to the seller.
4. **Convertible VTB** — where the seller rolls, say, 20% equity, a pre-agreed formula lets them convert that stake into a secured note and exit cleanly. "If they were already butting heads within six months the seller could just say I'm done, I'm converting."

## Canadian-specific
**This is Canadian content, not US.** Directly usable:
- Canadian banks are named as the senior lenders; the "banks in Canada aren't favourable to this industry" point drives the large-VTB workaround for cash-based or cyclical businesses — relevant to any janitorial/service target a Canadian bank views as asset-light.
- **Tax point for the seller (verify with an accountant):** the presenter states VTB interest "is taxed at the capital gains rate… not the income tax rate as if you were getting paid a bonus or a salary," and argues a seller should prefer VTB interest over a future salary or bonus. *Flagged: this characterisation of interest income is doubtful under Canadian rules — interest is normally fully taxable income, not capital gains. Confirm with a Canadian tax advisor before using this in a negotiation.*
- The seller-yield argument is the strongest lever a Canadian buyer has with a retiring owner: **8% secured against a business he knows beats his bond portfolio.**
