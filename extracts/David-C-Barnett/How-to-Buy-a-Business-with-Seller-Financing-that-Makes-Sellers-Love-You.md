## Key numbers
- **Seller financing rarely happens below ~$200K** sale price; you'll be beaten by an all-cash offer. Realistically it "begins around the $500,000 level."
- **Best pricing tier for seller financing: $500K–$1M.** His reasoning: "that awkward child stage where it's too big for most solopreneurs to buy and too small for a lot of private equity funded companies to buy" — hardest tier to sell, therefore the tier with the most seller flexibility. (Data cited is Amazon FBA-specific; he admits he hasn't checked other models.)
- **Earnout duration: typically 6–18 months**, longest he has seen ~3 years.
- **Working capital reserve: hold 10–20% of purchase price** in cash on top of the acquisition capital. On a $1M business bought with $700K down + $300K seller note, he'd still want **$100–200K** of working capital. "That's your uh-oh money."
- **Seller-retained equity of as little as 5%** is named as a cheap, high-perceived-value sweetener.
- Illustrative structure: $1M price, **$700K cash / $300K over 6–12 months.**

## What to look for
**Green flags**
- A seller with an **emotional, specific reason for selling** — a six-month trip, retiring a spouse, paying off the house. That's the reason you can structure around. "Most entrepreneurs are going to tell you 'because I want the money'... but there's usually a much deeper reason."
- A deal in the $500K–$1M band where the seller has been on market a while.

**Red flags**
- Any structure requiring a **personal guarantee** as the primary financing. "A personal guarantee alone is gonna mess you up with your actual house you live in, the car you drive." He does not recommend it under any circumstances.
- **Balloon payments.** "You narrow your ability to succeed... you're narrowing your margin of safety when really seller financing should be helping you with your margin of safety."
- Spending the working capital reserve on the note. If the earnout consumes your buffer, the note has stopped being protection and become risk.

**The milestone design problem — the sharpest content in the video**
- **Revenue-based milestones** favour the seller: they get paid whenever revenue clears the bar, regardless of your profitability.
- **Profit-based milestones** favour the buyer, and he is candid that they can be gamed — "you can just go spend money every single month... to make sure you're always at $4,999 per month in profit so you don't have to pay this seller." He calls doing that "a huge no-no" that will torch your reputation, and notes most sellers will insist on revenue for exactly this reason.
- The *legitimate* case for profit-based: it protects the buyer from exogenous shocks (his examples: a Google or Amazon algorithm update), and a seller told this up front will understand a genuine miss.
- Milestones can also be **project-based** (a redesign completed, a split-test finished) or **retention-based** (a key employee or CTO staying 12–14 months, common above $15–20M, rare below $10M).

## Canadian-specific
- **Nothing Canadian here at all, and very little that's even bricks-and-mortar.** This is US online/e-commerce (Amazon FBA, SaaS) marketplace advice.
- Note also the **terminology trap**: he uses "earn-out" and "seller financing" interchangeably throughout. They are *not* the same instrument. A **VTB / seller note** is fixed debt owed regardless of performance; an **earnout** is contingent consideration that may never be paid. Confusing them in a Canadian LOI will cost you — the tax treatment, the bank's willingness to count it as equity, and the seller's security position all differ. See `extracts/David-C-Barnett/` (the genuine Barnett VTB material) and the CSBFP/VTB notes in `extracts/Canadian-acquisition-finance/Financing-a-Business-Purchase-in-Canada.md` for the correct Canadian framing.
- His claim that "normal financial institutions just do not exist in this industry" is true of online businesses and **flatly false for Canadian service businesses** — CSBFP-backed bank debt is the standard tool for the janitorial, landscaping and pest control deals Jay is targeting.

## Verdict
**Low relevance, mis-tiered, and mis-attributed** — flag it in the index rather than lean on it. It is US online-business content mislabelled as Tier 1 Canadian, the speaker is not David Barnett, and it conflates earnouts with seller notes throughout.

Three things survive the discount and are worth carrying forward: (1) the **10–20% working capital reserve on top of the purchase price**, which is the most commonly skipped line in first-time buyer models and applies equally in Canada; (2) the **revenue-vs-profit milestone asymmetry**, which is genuinely useful whenever an earnout is on the table; and (3) the **"find the emotional reason for selling and structure around it"** approach, which is the same insight David Barnett and the Acquiring Minds guests make far better elsewhere in this corpus. Everything else — the $500K–$1M sweet spot, the 6–18 month earnout norm, "no banks exist" — is an artifact of the online-business marketplace he works at and should not be applied to a Canadian service business.
