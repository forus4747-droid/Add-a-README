## Key numbers
- **Five reasons businesses go up for sale**, in his order: **burnout/boredom/fatigue** (the mental category), **divorce**, **poor health**, **need to relocate**, and **retirement**. *"Retirement is the only one people plan for. **The other four out of five reasons that businesses go up for sale is stuff in life that happens to people.**"*
- **Roughly 1 in 10 business owners** thinks strategically about their business as an asset. The other 90% "think of themselves as the manager or operator... their succession plan is to live forever."
- **~80% of businesses listed for sale online do not sell.**
- **Small businesses trade at 2–3× cash flow** at the smaller end.
- His focus: **"Main Street" businesses — $500K of EBITDA and below.**

## What to look for
### The "day two problem" — the framing that organises everything else
> "So many people are just so focused on **getting a deal done**... **On day two, after the deal is done, you need to have a successful profitable cash-flowing business** — service all the debts, give yourself adequate compensation for the risk you're taking, and **make sure there's enough cash flow for the things that are not accounted for in the typical measures of cash flow that buyers use.**"

**The specific gap he names:** SDE and EBITDA both exclude depreciation and amortisation, *"which means there's no allowance in there for capital investment. **If you forget that machines wear out and have to be replaced, or that to grow the business you might need more trucks, then all of a sudden the cash flow you thought was there really isn't there.**"*

*This is the single most important adjustment for Jay's target industries* — mowers, plows, trucks, sprayers and vans all wear out. See the ten-input equipment cost model in `extracts/Landscaping-snow-operators/EXPERT-Snow-Removal-Pricing-Secrets-Revealed.md`.

### On overpaying — the clearest statement in the corpus
> "**If you overpay for a business, you basically end up taking all the future profits that should accrue to the owner of the business, discounting them to the present, and handing them to the seller.** You're giving the seller not just the value of their business but also a good chunk of what should be yours."

And on losing a deal to a higher bidder:
> "That's good. It means **there's one more dumb or ill-informed buyer that no longer is out there to compete with you.** It sucks — but if you'd matched their offer, what position would you be in?"

His response to buyers who say they're buying rather than starting *to avoid risk*: **"If you overpay for a deal, you're just inviting all that other risk back into your life anyway. So what's the use of buying?"**

### Exter's pyramid — where a small business actually sits on the risk scale
Barnett teaches John Exter's inverted pyramid of liquidity (gold at the point, then cash, T-bills, corporate debt, equities and real estate above). **Privately controlled small business sits at the very top — the riskiest asset class of all.**

The dominant risk is **liquidity**: *"If I buy 100 shares of the phone company and decide tomorrow I don't like them, as long as the market's open I can sell in an instant. **But if I buy a small privately controlled business and after a few months decide I made a mistake, it could take me years to get out — and the fact that I'm trying to sell it just after buying it would cause most buyers to wonder what I'd discovered that was terribly wrong.**"*

### The three kinds of business broker — worth memorising
1. **The shopkeeper.** "You say *I'd like to buy a purple hat* and they'll scour the stockroom trying to please you." This is the broker who puts a 2×-revenue price on a listing because that's what the seller asked for.
2. **The dentist.** "You say *my tooth hurts*, they look and say *you need a root canal.* You say *I want a filling* — and they say *I'm not going to do a filling, that's not what you need.*" **This is the broker a seller should want**: one who explains what the business is worth, why, who the buyer will be, what financing will look like, and whether the cash flow supports it.
3. **The clown.** "An incompetent person who doesn't really know anything, but speaks with a great deal of conviction and sounds authoritative... **these are the ones who can really mess things up.**" Often ex-real-estate agents who "bluff their way through and cross their fingers and hope the attorney gets the paperwork right."

**The consequence:** a seller's absurd asking price often reaches you through a broker, because the shopkeeper wrote it down. *"I looked at a CIM yesterday for somebody trying to sell their business for two times revenue... because they listened to a podcast where someone said these businesses are worth two times cash flow. **They didn't understand the conversation.**"* *(Exactly the error Chris Mondragon makes on camera in `extracts/UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month-in-the-Cleaning-Business.md`.)*

### Industry benchmark data — free, public, and almost nobody uses it
> "In the States they've got the **RMA (Risk Management Association) data**, published — a lot of people can get access through big public libraries or university libraries. You can look up your industry and, if they've got it, see performance **divided into quartiles based on tax return information. And the Canadian data is just on a public website — you can just look it up.**"

*(The Canadian resource is Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada's **Financial Performance Data** tool, built from CRA filings and searchable by NAICS code — revenue and expense benchmarks by size band and province. **This is the single most under-used free diligence tool available to a Canadian buyer.**)*

**His worked example of why it matters:** in one industry, **75% of businesses had revenue under $1.2M, and half were under $480,000.** *"If you looked up that information before getting into that industry, I think it would give you great pause — because you'd realise half the actors in this industry really aren't making any money."*

**Use it to answer, before spending a day on diligence: is this business above or below the median for its industry, and what does the top quartile actually earn?**

### The growth-spike problem — and the structural answer
On a business whose numbers only work because of one recent strong year:
> "**You value it based on what it looked like before**, and the offer is based on what it looked like before, and then you have some kind of **extra seller note that is forgivable** that is wrapped up in the so-called new value. **Because if the new value isn't real and doesn't stick around, you can't be paying for it.**"

And what he'd tell the seller: *"If you want to be paid on these results, then we need another two fiscal years of the same results. **Once we've got three years at the new level**, then we can argue the business has had a material change and it's now bigger and better. I would love to do that deal."*

**Why three years:** *"Somebody can manipulate one year of performance with accounting tricks, especially with cash-basis accounting. You just move some expenses — pay for things in December that you're going to sell in January. **But after three years it becomes much harder for those games to stick.**"*

### Two horror stories about skipping the professionals
1. **The HELOC purchase.** A buyer used home equity to write a cheque for an $800,000 franchise resale. **No banker ever looked at it as a business deal.** Three years later Barnett valued it at **$300–350,000**: *"It wasn't worth $800,000 then — it was probably worth $300,000 then."* His conclusion: **"The scariest deals I come across are when people don't have the need for a bank loan at all."** *(Note this directly contradicts Dan Drake's experience of a smoother diligence without a bank — and matches the Lenahan disaster in `extracts/Acquiring-Minds/How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses.md`. **A lender is free diligence; skip it only if you will do that work yourself.**)*
2. **The accountant who wasn't asked what the buyer thought they were asking.** The buyer believed they'd hired an accountant to opine on *valuation*; the accountant understood the engagement as reviewing the financial statements and tax returns for *accuracy*. When they reported back "it looks good to me," the buyer heard a valuation endorsement. **"I don't really value businesses, it's not really my thing" — and for most accountants that's the same case.** *Put the scope of every professional engagement in writing.*

### Why not to sell to (or buy from) a direct competitor
> "That competitor is not going to value you as much as someone in your industry who is not your competitor. In the back recesses of their mind they're thinking: **geez, if you just went out of business we'd get all your customers anyway** — and the employees too. There's this entitlement: *this is my market, why am I going to pay you to get more people from my own market?*"

**The better buyer is the same industry, different geography:** they understand the business, already run one like it, and acquiring you **opens an entirely new customer set** — plus shared suppliers and better purchase volumes. *"Those are the deals where the greatest synergies can be applied."*

### The corporate-to-operator transition — the honeycomb problem
> "In a big company you're in a **honeycomb world** — all these people doing their job in a great degree of isolation with a high degree of specialisation. The person in HR who does payroll knows everything about payroll but **has no idea where the money comes from on payday** — and they don't need to, because someone in treasury manages the cash. Even people who've managed a P&L in a division **never managed the balance sheet** — every time they needed operating capital to finance receivables, someone else magically delivered the money."

The contrast: *"You've got maybe 25 employees, 20 client-facing and five in the office who all cover a multitude of roles. **No one's really a specialist in anything** — they're multi-disciplined people who figure things out."*

**His recommendation, which almost nobody takes:**
> "If you're going to buy a business in this industry, **have you ever considered getting a job in this industry — even part-time, evenings, weekends?** A person who's not a plumber might still get a job at a plumbing company doing something in the office. **No one does that. But it would mitigate a lot of risk** — and potentially maybe this wasn't for you after all."

### The Tony story — the best illustration of owner-dependency in the corpus
A fried chicken franchise Barnett sold **three times**; the first two deals collapsed at the eleventh hour. The numbers were excellent — which is precisely why they attracted the wrong buyers.

The reason it performed: **the owner was there from first light to 4pm, and his son-in-law ran it from 4pm to 10pm.** A family member on site at all times.

Buyers assumed they could hire a manager. Then they met Tony, who would roll up his sleeves and show them where he'd been burned by fryer grease, and explain that **he personally cleaned the French fry slicing machine every day** because he wouldn't risk an employee's fingers and a workers' comp claim against his record. He ran a **four-day rotating deep-clean regimen** — north side of the kitchen, then east, south, west — and personally supervised it, because employees had cut corners before.

> "**People who worked at banks and government offices came to look at the business because of the numbers. When they sat down with Tony and heard these stories, they quickly realised that if they were going to have that cash flow continue under their stewardship, they were going to have to spend 50 hours a week there.**"

**The two questions Barnett says every buyer must ask:**
1. **"What's the cash flow?"** — that determines the price.
2. **"Will the cash flow continue under my stewardship?"** — that determines whether you should buy it at all. *"That's when you get into organisation, systems, processes... the softer stuff that a lot of entrepreneurs like to ignore. It resides in their head a lot of the time."*

And the self-image question that kills deals late: *"Will I be proud and confident telling all my friends that this is the kind of business I am? **Can you see yourself actually in that business? Picture it.**"*

### How sellers actually choose between offers — the most actionable section
> "**If the seller has been properly prepared, many of those offers are going to have a material amount of seller financing** — because I always told my sellers that the only way a buyer could pay a truly reasonable price, meaning the highest price possible, was with a good degree of seller financing.
>
> In that scenario the seller has to choose not only the most attractive financial offer, but **the buyer they feel most confident in — because ultimately those seller note payments depend on the success of the buyer.** I have had deals where there were multiple offers and **the seller chose the lowest-priced one, because they had the greatest confidence in that particular buyer.**"

**His coaching for buyers, three concrete actions:**
1. **Meet the seller in person — probably a couple of times — before you make the offer.**
2. **Present the offer yourself, to the broker and the seller together, so you can explain the *whys* of how you structured it.**
3. **"There's no reason why you can't make more than one offer simultaneously"** — present two or three structures and let the seller choose.

### On cold feet — the BATNA problem
From cataloguing his own collapsed deals as a broker: buyers who had **previously bought or started a business rarely backed out.** The ones who did were **people quitting a good job** — *"they very tangibly had a strong BATNA [best alternative to a negotiated agreement]."* Late at night before closing, they explore every threat. Often it's family pressure or a spouse who was never on board.

*(He tells his own version: years into his brokerage career, his mother asked when he was going to get a job.)*

## Canadian-specific
- **Barnett is Canadian (New Brunswick), was a Canadian business broker, and advises Canadian buyers.** This is genuine Tier 1 Canadian content, and almost everything in it applies directly.
- **The Canadian industry benchmark data he references is free and specific:** ISED's **Financial Performance Data** tool, built from CRA corporate filings, searchable by **NAICS code, province and revenue band**, showing balance sheet and income statement benchmarks **by quartile**. For Jay this means: before evaluating any janitorial, landscaping or pest control target, **pull the sector benchmark and see where the business sits against its peers.** Nothing else in this corpus offers a free, Canadian, tax-return-derived comparison set.
- **He notes a structural transparency difference:** in North America financial statements are private, unlike many European countries where competitors' filings are public. That opacity is why the benchmark data matters so much.
- **The five reasons for sale** map exactly onto the Canadian succession picture described by Divitkos — and the important point is that **four of the five are unplanned.** A Canadian buyer sourcing off-market should expect to find businesses whose owners have just had a health event, a divorce, or a collapse in enthusiasm — not a tidy retirement plan.
- **The seller-financing point is more important in Canada, not less.** Barnett's claim that the highest achievable price requires substantial vendor financing is the same conclusion the rest of this corpus reaches from the buyer's side, and it is reinforced by CSBFP's asset-only lending. **In Canada, the buyer who structures a credible VTB is often the only buyer who can pay a full price** — which is exactly why the seller's confidence in *you* becomes the deciding factor.

## Verdict
**The best single Track A video in the corpus, and the one to read first.** Barnett is Canadian, has sat on both sides of the table, and is the only voice here consistently arguing *against* doing a deal — which makes him the most useful counterweight to the survivorship bias he names explicitly: *"a lot of what gets talked about is based on people who believe they've had a successful transaction. The people who suffered a failure aren't shouting it from the rooftops."*

Six things to carry into the search:

1. **The day-two test.** Not *can I close this*, but *on day two, does this business service its debt, pay me properly for the risk, and fund the trucks and equipment that SDE conveniently ignores?*
2. **Pull the Canadian industry benchmark data before diligence.** Free, CRA-derived, quartiled by NAICS. If half the industry earns under $480K, that changes what a "good" target looks like.
3. **Structure around unproven growth with a forgivable seller note**, and hold the line that three years at a new level — not one — justifies a new valuation.
4. **Answer the second question honestly: will the cash flow continue under my stewardship?** Tony's fried chicken shop is the warning. In a janitorial or landscaping business, ask specifically what the owner personally does that nobody else does, then price the replacement.
5. **Win the deal on the seller's confidence, not the price.** Meet them twice before offering, present the offer yourself and explain the structure, and consider presenting two structures. In a VTB-dependent Canadian market, **the seller is choosing who will be able to pay them for the next five years** — that is the actual competition.
6. **Get a job in the industry first, even part-time.** Nobody does it. It is the cheapest possible way to discover whether the work — and the life — is one you want.

The final honest note is Barnett's own: **buying a business is a rare act**, and much of the enthusiasm around it is social. *"Sometimes we get influenced where we think we need to do something, almost like keeping up with the Joneses or a status symbol in certain circles."* **Getting outbid by someone who doesn't understand the numbers is a good outcome.**
