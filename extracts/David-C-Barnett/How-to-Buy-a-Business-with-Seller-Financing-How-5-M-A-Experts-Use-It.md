## Key numbers
- **Typical seller note interest: 6–10%.** Priced above senior debt because it sits behind it.
- **Typical seller-financing share: 20–30% of price**; "I have seen more, 50%."
- Illustrative stack on a $1M business: SBA $800K + buyer equity $100K + **seller note $100K**.
- On a $5M deal under SBA rules: **$4.5M debt / $500K equity (90/10)** is *eligible* — "doesn't mean a bank is going to do it."
- **The SBA does not require a seller note at all.** Bruce Marks, citing SOP 50 10 6: "nowhere in there does it say that in an acquisition transaction the seller must hold a note."
- **Amortisation is set by the multiple, not by convention:**
  - Paying **5.5× cash flow** → the note "is probably going to have to be really well structured, like a 10-year loan, very low payments, because there's just not enough room for debt service."
  - Paying **3×** on the same business → "obviously that seller note can be amortised over four years."
- **Brian Beers' franchise formula: 2.5–3× SDE**, structured entirely on **three numbers — down payment, monthly payment, number of months.** Worked example: a store earning **$100K SDE → $250K price → $30K down → $3,000/month × 75 months** (≈$255K total P&I). Terms he's used: 4 years, 8 years, 13 years, "random numbers like 88 months."
- Beers' interest rates: **3% to 6.5%** — "we just make up a number that gets the payment to what we need it to be."
- Beers finances **~25% seller note** on essentially every deal, and has been to a bank **once**.
- **Shanahan's $25M deal: a $1.8M seller note plus $400K from a small bank**, with a couple hundred thousand of his own — a stock sale where he used the company's own cash.

## What to look for
**The mechanics you must know**
- A seller note is **unsecured** and **subordinated** to senior debt. Payment priority: senior debt first, seller note second. If free cash flow can't cover everything, "the seller note may be impaired."
- **Why a seller says yes:** interest of 6–10% versus near-nothing on cash in the bank; tax deferral versus an all-cash close; confidence in a business they know; and it materially raises the odds of closing at all.
- **Why a seller says no:** at high seller-financing percentages "the seller is assuming all the downside risk without any of the upside of ownership."
- **Why a buyer wants one beyond the money:** "the seller is vested in the business's success since they remain vested until the note is paid in full" — and they stay a go-to resource through transition and client introductions.

**The two structural insights that matter most**

1. **A seller note behind everything counts as equity to the bank.** Shanahan on his $25M deal: "that $1.8M seller note — lenders look at that as equity because it's behind everything else... a $1.8M seller's note is like a $1.8M investment to the bank." *This is the single most important structural fact for a thin-equity buyer.* It only works if the note is genuinely subordinated and on standby.

2. **Cash flow dictates every term.** Bruce Marks' line, which he says he has to use constantly: **"Congratulations, you've structured a deal that doesn't work."** Buyers arrive with an LOI, the DSCR fails, and they have to go back and re-trade with the seller. **Model debt service on the total stack — senior debt AND the seller note — before you sign anything.**

**Risk transfer worth copying**
- **Patrick Dichter tied his seller note to revenue retention:** if revenue dropped past a defined point, **the seller note goes away.** He'd modelled the downside explicitly — a $1.2M accounting firm falling to $500K, note extinguished, business still saleable as a shell, leaving $200–300K owed to the SBA. "That would suck, it would take a long time to pay back, but I could do it — it's not going to totally ruin our lives."
- This is the *right* form of the risk-sharing the Acquisitions Anonymous hosts demanded on a risky deal (`extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/A-1M-Pest-Control-Business-Ecommerce-Buzz-or-Bust-AA-297.md`): **a note that self-extinguishes if the thing you couldn't diligence turns out to be false.**

**Negotiation technique — the "three numbers" method**
Beers refuses to negotiate price, interest rate and terms separately. He opens with **down payment, monthly payment, and number of months** — "money out of my pocket, money into their pocket." The seller counters on the same three ("I want $50K down," "I want $4,000 a month," "I'll take $2,000 but over 10 years"). Only afterwards do they set an interest rate and back into principal. It is a genuinely better way to run the conversation with an unsophisticated seller, and worth adopting.
- Note the allocation fight buried inside it: the buyer wants **low interest → more principal → more allocated to depreciable assets**; the seller wants the opposite. (See `extracts/Valuation-craft/Structuring-a-Business-Sale-to-Minimize-Taxes-Asset-vs.-Stock-Sales.md`.)

**Deal sourcing — Beers' franchise playbook (genuinely transferable)**
- Inside a franchise system you already have "the book": **every owner's contact information and their sales figures.** Nothing is public, everything is for sale.
- He builds status deliberately — hosting Zoom sessions for 75 franchisees, interviewing top performers, sharing compensation and advertising plans, sitting on committees. "You become this figure of status... they trust you because they see you, and they like you because you seem like a good person."
- The cold approach: *"We're looking to expand in the next six to 12 months — if you're interested in selling your stores, please make me the first call."* Responses range from "I've been waiting for you to call me" to "my daughter's off to college in a year, then I'll be ready."
- **Density rule for expansion:** he needs **five locations minimum** in a new market to justify a district manager. He also screens out entire states where one large group owns everything.
- **Reputation is the moat:** "if you screw people over, or you're not a good operator, or you cheat your employees — God, people talk, everyone would know that." The franchisor now brings him deals because he's "the easy button."

**Risks to the buyer**
- The note is unsecured, so **you may have to offer a personal guarantee.**
- **Interest may be deferred or accrued to maturity rather than paid currently** — creating a balloon. Check this in every term sheet.
- Advice given: **negotiate the mix (20–30%), be transparent about default resolution up front, watch total DSCR, and keep the note short** to reduce the seller's risk.

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout — SBA structures do not exist in Canada.** Every reference to SBA 90/10 eligibility, SOP 50 10 6, and 10% equity contribution must be discarded.
- **The Canadian translation:**
  - "Seller note" = **VTB (vendor take-back)**. The instrument and its logic are identical; only the surrounding lender differs.
  - The Canadian senior lender is a chartered bank, often **CSBFP-guaranteed** — and critically, **CSBFP finances assets, not goodwill or the business price** (max $1.15M, 85% guarantee). See `extracts/Canadian-acquisition-finance/Financing-a-Business-Purchase-in-Canada.md`.
  - **This is exactly why Canadian VTBs are typically larger than US seller notes.** In an asset-light service business — janitorial, landscaping, pest control — most of the purchase price is goodwill, which CSBFP will not fund. The gap has to come from equity or the vendor. The 20–30% norm quoted here is a *floor* in Canada, not a midpoint.
  - **The "note counts as equity to the bank" point holds in Canada** and is the key negotiation: a Canadian bank will treat a VTB as quasi-equity **only if it is fully postponed/standstill** — interest-only, no principal payments, and no enforcement while senior debt is outstanding. Get the postponement agreement language right or the benefit evaporates.
  - Canadian VTB rates in this corpus run **3–8%** depending on buyer risk, lower than the 6–10% quoted here.
  - **Tax:** a Canadian vendor taking back a note may be able to use the **capital gains reserve** to spread the gain over up to five years (generally minimum 20% recognised annually) — a genuine seller benefit with no US analogue in this form. Worth raising with a reluctant vendor. Verify with a CPA.
- **Verify the "personal guarantee" expectation:** Canadian banks routinely require personal guarantees on small-business acquisition debt, and CSBFP itself caps personal guarantees at 25% of the loan. That cap is a real Canadian advantage worth understanding before you negotiate.

## Verdict
**The best Track A video in the corpus on seller-note mechanics**, and far more useful than the online-business seller-financing video filed alongside it (`How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-that-Makes-Sellers-Love-You.md`), which conflates notes with earnouts. Five practitioners with real transaction counts, and no filler.

Four things to carry into a Canadian deal:

1. **Model the DSCR on the full stack before signing an LOI.** The most common failure mode described here is a deal that dies at the bank because the buyer priced the equity but not the payments.
2. **Amortisation is a function of the multiple.** If you're paying near 5×, the VTB must be long and light; near 3×, it can be short. Decide which trade you're making.
3. **A fully postponed VTB is equity in the bank's eyes.** This is the lever that makes a thin-equity Canadian deal financeable, given CSBFP won't fund goodwill.
4. **Tie part of the VTB to retention.** Dichter's self-extinguishing note is the correct structural answer to the risks you cannot diligence — customer concentration, undocumented contracts, culture — which Divitkos confirms you largely can't inspect from outside anyway.

The franchise-sourcing playbook is a bonus and applies directly to Canadian service businesses: **build visible standing in a small industry community, then be the first call when a tired owner is ready.** In a thin Canadian market with few professional buyers, that is a cheaper edge than outbidding anyone.
