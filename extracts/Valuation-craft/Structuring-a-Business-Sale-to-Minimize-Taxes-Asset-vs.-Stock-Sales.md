## Key numbers
- US top ordinary income rate **37%** vs top long-term capital gains **20%** → the **17% spread** is what the whole asset-vs-stock fight is about.
- **Example 1 — $10M service business, zero basis.** Assets are just fully-depreciated office equipment: **$100K recaptured at ordinary rates** (s.1245), **$9.9M goodwill at capital gains**. Total tax **$2.017M**. Stock sale would be $2.0M. **Difference: $17,000** on a $10M deal.
- **Example 2 — manufacturer** with inventory, A/R, equipment, real estate, goodwill: stock sale **$2.0M** tax vs asset sale **$3.19M** tax. **~$1.19M swing.**
- Buyer side: asset sale = depreciate fixed assets fresh (5-year life, or bonus depreciation). Stock sale = **amortize purchase price over 15 years**.

## What to look for
**Green flags**
- An **asset-light service business** (cleaning, landscaping, pest control — exactly Jay's targets). Example 1 proves the tax gap between structures is *trivial* there. That means structure is cheap to concede and can be traded for real money elsewhere.
- Seller who engaged their accountant **more than a year before** going to market. Their books will be cleaner and the deal will close faster.

**Red flags**
- Asset-heavy target (inventory, equipment, real estate) where the seller has not modelled the asset-sale tax hit. They will discover the $1M swing during diligence and either blow up the deal or demand a price bump.
- A seller taking structural advice **from the buyer**. Ask who their advisor is; if the answer is "you", slow down — a seller who gets ambushed at signing is a seller who re-trades.
- Depreciation recapture on heavily written-down equipment: the seller has no basis, so they owe ordinary-rate tax on the **full** allocation. Watch how they try to push purchase-price allocation toward goodwill.

## Canadian-specific
- **US — verify for Canada.** Everything in this video is US tax code: s.1245 recapture, s.338(h)(10), 15-year goodwill amortization, state-level examples (Georgia, Washington's 7% capital gains tax).
- Canadian equivalents to check with a CPA: **share sale vs asset sale** (not "stock"), the **$1.25M LCGE** on QSBC shares (see `extracts/Canadian-tax/ACCOUNTANT-EXPLAINS-Canada-s-Capital-Gains-Tax.md`), **recapture of CCA** in place of s.1245 recapture, and **eligible capital property / Class 14.1** goodwill rules in place of 15-year amortization. There is **no Canadian equivalent of the 338(h)(10) election** — do not plan around it.
- The *structural logic* transfers cleanly even though the code does not: buyers want assets (stepped-up depreciable base, no inherited liabilities), sellers want shares (single capital gain, plus in Canada the LCGE). In Canada the seller's incentive to insist on shares is **stronger** than in the US because of the LCGE.
- Canadian buy-side consequence: if you must buy shares, price the inherited liabilities — CRA payroll/GST-HST arrears, WSIB/WCB assessments, employment claims — and back them with an indemnity plus holdback or VTB offset.

## Verdict
The most useful idea here is not the tax code — it's the **negotiating arithmetic**. Quantify the structural tax delta *before* you argue about it. On an asset-light service deal that delta is often noise (0.2% of price in Example 1), so concede structure loudly and take payment terms, VTB size, working-capital peg, or non-compete in exchange. On an asset-heavy deal it's a million dollars and you should expect a real fight. Apply the *method* in Canada; discard the *rules*.
