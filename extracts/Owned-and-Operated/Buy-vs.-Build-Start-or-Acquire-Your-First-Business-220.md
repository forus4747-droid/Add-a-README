## Key numbers
- **SBA acquisition rates at ~9–10%** at time of recording (2025). Wilson: "10% interest is too much."
- **SBA down payment: 10%** — so "$100,000 to buy a million dollar business."
- **Both hosts consider $100K too little capital.** Wilson: "you have a real risk of bankruptcy there that is just unnecessary... **maybe figure it out with like $300 grand at your disposal.**"
- **A senior technician at Wilson's company earns $150,000–$300,000 on a W2**, risk-free. "It's more money than most owners make for the first 10 years of their business."
- Carr's own history: earning **$250,000+ on a W2 in California at 27–28**, and he **put $90,000 into his business** to start.
- **The moonshot probability, debated live:** Carr guesses a **10%** chance a $1M-revenue / $200K-EBITDA business sells for $10–15M in five years. Wilson counters **~0.1%**: "there's no chance. If you bought a $2 million business and doubled it every year for five years — who's got the skill set to do that?"
- **The realistic outcome:** "to sell for $1 million is $300,000 of net at a three times. That's a good multiple. **And it's going to take him a decade to get there.**"
- **Overpriced market:** "there are dog-shit companies that I've bid on that are going for like **six times**. And I'm like, you should have gone for a two."
- **Wilson's own trajectory: $2M → $40M in eight years.**
- **Greenfield launch benchmark (from operators who already know how):** his friends launching new locations target **$3–5M in year one**, with **~$200,000 in the first month.** He is explicit that this depends entirely on already knowing how.

## What to look for
**The framing quote:** *"When assets are expensive, build assets."* With SBA at 10% and multiples still elevated, Wilson's position is that building is simply cheaper than buying.

**Buying a one- or two-man shop is building.** Both agree: "starting up a company from zero and buying a two-man shop — same thing." Wilson's one exception was his own first deal: he bought a small HVAC company because it was **the only HVAC company in a suburb** — complete market domination, poorly run, owner had stumbled into a few right things. **Buy small only when you're buying a market position, not a business.**

**"Don't buy construction."** Wilson's most emphatic advice, delivered twice. "Those are the only DMs I get. 'Hey man, I'm going to buy a new construction—' and I'm like, dude, just stop." *(The reasoning is developed at length in `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/4.5M-HVAC-Business-With-33-Margins-Is-It-a-Smart-Buy.md`.)*

**Match the deal to your skill set — the most practically useful framework here**

| Your background | What you can handle |
|---|---|
| **Technician / operator** | Can build from zero, or buy small ($1–1.5M). Can run calls when everyone quits. "The technician can get to that $1 million mark by themselves, maybe with one helper." |
| **Marketing / SEO** | **Stay away from small.** You cannot run the calls, so you're hostage to an operational partner — "nine times out of ten they fail because it's a bad partnership and they feel stuck, because you can't fire that person." |
| **Finance / CFO background** | Needs **$2–3M+**. "What as a CFO are you going to do at a $3 million HVAC company? Very little strategy, because you're fighting fires." **CFO skills don't matter until $10–15M.** |
| **MBA, no trade** | First problem is **finding someone to hold the licence** — and if that person won't run calls, you're hiring a technician too, while cash burns. |

Carr's own case proves the point in reverse: he bought a business, **everybody quit on him**, and he survived only because he could run the calls himself. "Naturally, that suited my skill set really well. But if I'd built, and then everybody quit on me — phone's not ringing, and I'm not learning or running. Now I'm just screwed."

**Culture is the real cost of buying, and Wilson has stopped tolerating it**
> "The biggest headache is cultural... **if I walked into an acquisition, it was four people, and I started to sense [problems] — we would fire the whole team immediately.** I just don't have the patience... **Building is attractive because you can pick the team you work with.**"

He notes this is a change: "three years ago I would have never thought I'd say that." And he names *why* the culture is broken in a business that's for sale:
> "The reason the smaller companies are trading is because **the owners are tired** — and the tiredness is caused by them mismanaging companies, letting the techs run all over them, letting the techs not sell. Then someone comes in: 'Hey, you've got quotas to meet, you've got to generate revenue.' And they're like — *revenue? I just fix things.*"

**The technician-economics gap, which is genuinely surprising**
Wilson's staff are astonished to learn they must generate more revenue than their own wage: "*I covered my wage, I'm good.* And I'm like — what about the gas that got you there?" He blames trade schools. **For a buyer, this is a diligence and integration issue:** a service business whose technicians don't understand or accept a revenue expectation will resist any attempt to raise productivity, and that resistance is what turns a 6-month integration into a clear-out.

**"Marketing ≠ lead generation" — a hiring trap worth remembering**
> "This person's a marketer — doesn't mean anything, 0%, if they don't understand **local service lead gen**, because it's its own thing... I had people come out of marketing college: 'Hey, I got a marketing degree.' Talk to them about lead gen — zero idea. They know what SEO is, but what's the strategy to get to number one? **The people who are best at that are the people who've just been doing it.**"

**Carr's case for buying, which survives the debate**
1. **"The phone was ringing day one."** The zero-to-$1M climb is "extremely brutal for people who've never done it."
2. **You buy brand trust and retain most of the existing customers.**
3. **Leverage:** 10% down, or as little as **5% with a full seller hold** — "you can get in with a lot of leverage."
4. **The time-horizon argument.** The W2-plus-index-fund path makes you wealthy at 50–55. "The reason you take the moonshot is because you're **trying to speed up that time horizon.** I can hit it by the time I'm 40."
5. **"It's better to be in the game than not at all"** — with risk mitigation, chiefly: don't buy construction.

**Wilson's counter, which also survives**
- **Survivorship bias.** "We hear of the Tommy Mellos, the Wilsons, the rapid responses" — not the failures.
- **The high-probability path is inside a growing company.** "You can be an entrepreneur within an organization with none of the risk... when our business transacts, a lot of people are going to make a lot of money — a much higher chance of success with basically zero risk." He notes he's building a mechanism to reward senior leaders at a liquidity event.
- **The opportunity cost is real and large:** five years of foregone $200K+ W2 income is roughly a million dollars of take-home, "for basically pride and lots of headaches."

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout** (SBA rates and 10% down have no Canadian equivalent). **Canadian translation:**
  - There is no CSBFP-financed path to "$100K buys a $1M business." **CSBFP caps at $1.15M and funds assets, not goodwill** — so a Canadian buyer of an asset-light service business will need substantially more than 10% equity, or a much larger VTB. See `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`.
  - **The 5%-down-with-a-full-seller-hold structure Carr mentions is effectively the Canadian norm, not the exception** — a large, fully-postponed VTB is how thin-equity Canadian deals get done.
  - **Interest rate context differs.** Canadian prime and BDC pricing at the time of writing sit below the ~10% SBA rates quoted here, which weakens Wilson's "assets are expensive, build assets" argument slightly for a Canadian buyer. **Verify current rates before applying his conclusion.**
- **Trade licensing is provincial and individual in Canada** — the "who holds the licence" problem Wilson describes is if anything sharper here (Ontario: certified plumber, TSSA gas fitter, ODP for refrigerant handling; pesticide applicator classes for pest control). **For an MBA-profile buyer with no trade, securing a licence-holder is a closing condition, not a hiring plan.**
- **The market-domination logic behind Wilson's one small acquisition applies strongly in Canada**, where secondary markets often support only one or two operators per trade. Being the only pest control or janitorial company in a town of 15,000 is a real moat — see the same argument in AA 298.

## Verdict
**The most honest buy-versus-build discussion in the corpus, precisely because nobody wins it.** Wilson has $40M and would build; Carr bought and would buy again; both are right about their own situations, and the reason is the framework they arrive at: **the answer depends on your skill set and your capital, not on which is objectively better.**

For Jay, three concrete conclusions:

1. **Be honest about which profile you are.** If you can't run the work yourself, don't buy a one-to-four-person shop — you'll be hostage to whoever can, and Wilson's "everybody quit on me" scenario would end you. That argues for buying a business with **an existing crew lead and a real second-in-command**, or for a trade where the licensed work can be hired rather than owner-performed (janitorial, landscaping) rather than one where it can't (pest control applicator licences, gas-fitting).
2. **$100K is not enough capital, in either direction.** Both hosts, from opposite sides, say the same thing. Budget the working-capital reserve on top of the purchase price — 10–20%, per `extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-that-Makes-Sellers-Love-You.md`.
3. **Underwrite the realistic outcome, not the moonshot.** Wilson's arithmetic — a business that sells for $1M needs $300K of net at 3×, and takes a decade — is the base case. The $10M exit is 0.1%, not 10%. **A Canadian service business bought at 3× SDE that pays Jay a good income and sells for a similar multiple in ten years is the plan; anything better is upside.**

The most valuable single warning, and the one most likely to bite: **culture is the cost of buying that nobody prices.** Wilson would now clear house on a four-person acquisition rather than spend six months arguing. Whether or not that's the right call, the point stands — **budget for the possibility that the team you inherit is the reason the business was for sale.**
