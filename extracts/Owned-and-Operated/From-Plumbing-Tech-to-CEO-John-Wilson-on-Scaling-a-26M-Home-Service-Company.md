# From Plumbing Tech to CEO — John Wilson, $26M home service company

- **Channel:** Owned and Operated · **Track:** B · **Duration:** 46:15
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B6HPEtTCE_U
- **tags:** home-services, plumbing, HVAC, acquisition, seller-note, plateaus, overhead, focus, marketing-spend, succession

## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- Took over the family plumbing business at 23, **bought it at 25**: ~**8 people, $1M revenue**. Eight years later: **150 people, $26M revenue**, budgeting $34–35M next year.
- **50% growth this year; ~38% compound annual growth.**
- **Marketing spend ≈ 5% of revenue** — and up 50% year over year on that. "Because we're so dialled in on who we want, getting in front of them isn't that complicated."
- **98–99% residential**, almost no commercial, by deliberate choice.
- Sells **1,500 water heaters a year** — the density is what makes the margin.
- Down to **~20 job types**; most plumbing companies have ~150.
- Killed a **$1M/year septic tank replacement line** because gross margin was low and it took too long.

**The acquisition that doubled him (real terms):**
- Target was about to go bankrupt, **14 employees**. Price **$105,000: $10,000 cash** ("that's all I had") **and the rest a six-year seller note**, only just paid off.
- He went on to buy **eight more before he was 30 — no investors, all cash and seller notes.** "I didn't even know that the SBA was a thing until my eighth acquisition. I thought a bank lending on an acquisition was the craziest thing I'd ever heard of."

## The grind / structural reality — "plateaus"
The most useful framework in the episode. Growth happens in plateaus, each one a period of reinvestment:
- **$0–1M:** the first plateau. You must add an office person / CSR / dispatcher or you work 14-hour days doing books, booking calls and managing the field at once.
- **$2–3M:** add a field manager to manage the revenue producers.
- **$3–5M:** a second manager for a different department.
- **Up to $15M:** you can *sell* your way there on a lean structure — one manager with 25 direct reports, four or five call takers, a dispatcher. Out of $15M you might be "ripping two to three million of cash" because overhead is barely different from a $5M business.
- **$15M → $20M is the wall.** "You have to add $2 million of payroll to get over that hump." Real accounting team not a bookkeeper, real HR not a part-timer, a five-person marketing team, and **one manager for every 8–10 people**. "That is when businesses get their butts kicked — during the reinvestment."
- Diagnostic: "If you're feeling capped, if there aren't enough hours in the day — welcome to the plateau. That's a good thing, because it means there's an answer, and the answer is some type of reinvestment."
- Warning: at ~$3M the business throws off $300–500K of free cash flow, "and that's when people start doing dumb stuff."

## Winning work
- **Marketing is just choosing who you work for.** Early on they chased churches, schools, restaurant contracts *and* homeowners simultaneously and "basically didn't get anywhere."
- That choice cascades: "who you're going to work for determines who you're going to hire, your compensation structure, the vehicles you drive and what's in them, your physical location, overhead staffing, tech stack. It touches every single part of our business."
- **"The less we do, the bigger we grow."** Fewer job types, fewer tasks, fewer customer types.
- Two and only two growth paths: **add scope in one geography** (his choice, made for lifestyle — young kids, didn't want to travel) or **add geographies with narrow scope** (the franchise model — a single-trade branch works at $2M; his three-trade model isn't profitable in a new market until ~$15M).
- Best-in-class operators in his industry **stayed one trade until $30–50M.** He considers himself probably too small to be multi-trade at $26M.

## Staffing and culture
- "Do we treat people with respect and do we create them opportunities to make a lot of money?" Build a comp structure where **the company wins and the team wins** — either side losing is the failure mode.
- The hardest transition is personal: "you can only grow to what you can carry… I have to learn how to become a person that gets things done through other people." He now works through multiple layers, "and sometimes I do great and sometimes I do terribly."
- First manager arrived via acquisition: at 26 he suddenly had three managers and ~28 employees, "the entire team is double your age."

## What sellers hide
Sharp seller-side tell, delivered from the buyer's chair: "as we bought all these companies we'll see these companies that are like $3 million, and the owner is driving a nicer car than I am, he's got his lake house and his boat, and he's not there very frequently." **An absentee owner at $3M with an expensive lifestyle is a signal that reinvestment has been skipped** — the structure you are buying is thinner than the revenue suggests.

## Canadian-specific
US operator (Akron, Ohio). **US — verify for Canada.** The transferable parts are structural, not jurisdictional: the plateau/reinvestment map, the $15–20M payroll wall, one-manager-per-8-to-10, and 5% of revenue on marketing all apply directly. His financing history is the most relevant Canadian read-across — **nine acquisitions funded on cash and vendor notes before he ever touched a bank**, which is the realistic route for a Canadian buyer without large acquisition lending.

## Verdict
**Worth the read — the best operator-side account of how a service business actually scales.** The plateau framework tells you what a business of a given size *should* have in place, which doubles as a diligence lens: if a $3M target has no field manager and no second layer, you are buying an unreinvested business and the cost of fixing it is quantified here.
