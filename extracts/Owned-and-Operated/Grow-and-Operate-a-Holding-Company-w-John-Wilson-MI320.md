**The anecdote worth remembering:** the **2% his father retained is now worth multiples of what Wilson paid for the other 98%.** His reaction is not regret — "I was given an opportunity at 25 that no reasonable person should have given me. To be able to pay that back is the ultimate honour of my life."

## What to look for — the family succession, and what went wrong
**Why it happened at all:** his father was **69** and needed an off-ramp. "That's not something that happens in most multi-generation businesses when the next generation is 25."

**How it was negotiated:** over **weekly lunches, for about two years.** "Ten years later that's still how I negotiate deals, because that's how I negotiated my first one."

**The two mistakes he names explicitly:**

1. **No timeline on the handover.** "We didn't have a timeline. Looking back, that was a big mistake." The problem was structural, not personal: **"The business was very small — incapable of supporting two owners. A million dollars of revenue. He was breaking into his 70s, putting on the brakes; I was willing to take on more risk. Those are two things that obviously do not go well together."** He was handcuffed as the minority partner and could not progress the things he thought necessary. His prescription: *"We should have said exactly four years, or exactly five — whatever it ends up being — and then we should have segued the role better."*

2. **Cap table doesn't settle family dynamics.** "Even after that second transaction, we still co-worked together and still struggled with some of the same stuff **despite me owning 98%. It was more like: cap table was irrelevant in the face of family.**" The father officially retired only about **a year before the interview** — three or four years after Wilson held 98%, and only after friends coached him that he had to.

**What kept it survivable:** "Anytime we would have a disagreement we would sit down and literally say — **hey, our relationship is more important than this problem.** I still handle conflict in the same way."

**The generational fit problem, stated kindly:** "He really felt like a fish out of water in the business those last couple of years. **He liked the small business, very control-oriented, he liked having six to ten people. I have 145 employees today.** That requires a very different set of skills."

## The distressed acquisition — a genuine no-money-down deal
- Target: a plumbing company **losing money every year for a decade**, surviving on a tiny line of credit, $750K and shrinking.
- **Diagnosis of why it was failing:** "It was badly managed. **The owner was attempting to be a passive owner in a very tiny company**, and the people he had equipped to manage were not capable of managing it."
- **Price = the debt.** $110,000, structured as seller note plus $30K cash — i.e. the seller took the note to avoid bankruptcy.
- **The fix:** "We changed the pricing. We changed the incentives. We added marketing." Revenue went $1.6–1.7M (2016) → $2.9M (2018).
- **The lesson he drew — and later revised:** "I saw the power of additive deals and I wanted more of that. Hey, if you can go all the way using acquisitions, then you should do that. **I later found out why that was a terrible idea.**"

## The de-holdco'ing — the most valuable strategic content here
After nine acquisitions across five or six locations, Wilson **merged everything into one trade company under one headquarters, two brands.** The reasoning is the important part:

> "If I looked at a competitor that was my same exact size with the same amount of customers, **I would be a worse operator than them, because they are more focused.** It created a disadvantage, because we had **five payrolls, five QuickBooks accounts, ten bank accounts.** We ended up getting operationally slow. So last year we smashed it all together."

**The corroborating data point:** Chenmark **acquired ~14 companies in two or three years, then spent the next two or three years figuring out who they were.** Wilson's own version: "We tripled in five months at the end of 2021, and **it has taken us every bit of two and a half years to figure out who the heck we are.**"

**The multi-trade struggle, quantified:** "It's really hard to add brand new capabilities totally unlike your core business. **HVAC and plumbing — we use them interchangeably, but those are totally different things.** Different marketing, different staffing, different phone answering. **HVAC just hit its own mass in the last 12 months** — seven years in, at 20 employees, two managers, $4M revenue."

**Why he didn't buy a franchise:** he was attracted to the radical focus of a single-service franchise model, but multi-unit franchising means multi-geography, and "I didn't have the depth of capital or talent." Plus a straight lifestyle answer: **"I really like putting my kids down to bed at night. And I haven't needed money since I was 28 or 29, so more money isn't interesting enough to miss that."**

## Staffing and partnership
- **Brandon (COO/President) was hired off Indeed** — not a network hire. Wilson was looking specifically for **someone who had implemented a franchise system before**, to systematise a business that was ~$3.4M and showing cracks.
- Brandon's background: launched a landscaping company at 16, sold it at 19, ran a 60-employee landscaping business at 22 while paying his way through college debt-free.
- **Division of labour today (~30/70):**
  - **Wilson: recruitment, marketing, accounting, infrastructure.** His logic: *"Everything I needed to grow. I wanted the cash to pay for it, the marketing to drive it, recruitment so I could handle who gets in, and infrastructure so I could force vehicles to be acquired on the timeline for the other three."*
  - **Brandon: revenue growth, operations, gross margin.**
- **His critique of the EOS visionary/integrator split** is worth hearing: "The CEO has to be an active participant in the business. We tried it a few times, and sometimes what that looked like was **me not really doing anything for a couple of months, which isn't very productive** — because I thought I was supposed to be the visionary. **We grew, but we didn't grow personally.**"
- Later admission that reframes his earlier self-image: **"At $3 million you have to be strong in everything, because there's not enough money at $3 million to be strong in financials [alone]."**

## The philosophical core
> "Almost anyone can be an investor and money is kind of a commodity. **The real challenging thing is to grow and operate a business — that's where actual value is created.** The way I see being the CEO of a growing small business: **this is the most active investing you could ever participate in.** You're investing cash flows, you're investing off your balance sheet, you're investing in people. It is nothing but investing. But if you look at it purely financially, you're held back."

He is candid that being "the money guy" made him **lopsided** — he had to learn demand generation and how to hire executives at 26, and calls that correction the work of the last few years.

## What sellers hide
- **Ask whether the business can support two owners.** A $1M service business cannot. Any deal that leaves a seller with meaningful ownership and an operating role needs both a **defined timeline** and a **defined role**, in writing, at closing. Wilson's four-year drift is the cautionary tale.
- **Ask why a struggling business is struggling.** His target had lost money for ten straight years for one identifiable reason — an absentee owner with managers who couldn't manage. **That is a fixable cause. "The market is tough" is not.** Distinguishing between them is the whole distressed-acquisition thesis.
- **A distressed seller may accept the debt payoff as the price.** $110,000 with $30K down bought a business that doubled his headcount. **When the alternative is bankruptcy, the seller's alternatives — not the multiple — set the price.**
- **Ask how many entities, payrolls, bank accounts and accounting files the business runs.** Wilson's five payrolls and ten bank accounts made him slower than a single-location competitor of the same size. If a target is already a mini-holdco, **integration cost is real and takes years**, not months.
- **Ask how long each service line took to become self-sufficient, and which ones still aren't.** Seven years and $4M for HVAC. A multi-trade target may have one real business and one subsidised hobby.
- **Ask who found the COO and what they cost.** The gap between "the owner does everything" and "there's a real second-in-command" is the single largest post-close cost item for a first-time buyer.

## Canadian-specific
- **US (Ohio) — structures need translation, dynamics do not.** The **holdco-as-LLC mistake he names** (an LLC generally cannot own an S corp, which complicated share sales later) has no direct Canadian analogue: in Canada the standard structure is a **holdco corporation owning opco shares**, which enables the **section 85 rollover**, **intercorporate dividends**, **creditor protection**, and — critically — **multiplying access to the $1.25M lifetime capital gains exemption** through a family trust. **Get the holdco/trust structure right before you buy, not after** — retrofitting it triggers tax. See `extracts/Canadian-tax/ACCOUNTANT-EXPLAINS-Canada-s-Capital-Gains-Tax.md`.
- **The family-succession dynamics are directly relevant to Jay's search**, because the Canadian market is defined by exactly this: aging owners with no succession plan (see `extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md` — fewer than 10% have formalised plans). **Wilson's story is what it looks like when the family transfer works, and it still took two transactions, seven years, and a father who couldn't quite let go.** When buying from a family where a child is nominally involved, establish early whether the child is a buyer, a competitor, or an obstacle.
- **The 100-letters-for-5-responses direct outreach ratio** applies unchanged in Canada, and matters more here because broker-listed deal flow is thinner.
- **The seller-note-as-the-whole-price structure works in Canada as a VTB** and is especially available in distressed situations, where the vendor's alternative is receivership. Note the Canadian wrinkle: in a distressed purchase, **secured creditors, CRA trust claims (source deductions, GST/HST) and WSIB arrears rank ahead of the vendor** — so verify what the $110,000-equivalent actually clears before assuming the business comes out clean.

## Verdict
**The best case study in the corpus of a small acquisition compounding into something substantial** — $1M to ~$24M in under a decade, starting from a 25-year-old with a 400 credit score, a $30,000 down payment and a seller note.

Four things to carry forward:

1. **The distressed-acquisition template.** A business losing money for a decade because of an absent owner, bought for the value of its debt, seller-financed, doubling the acquirer's scale overnight. **The critical judgement was diagnosing the *cause* of the losses as management rather than market** — and the fixes (pricing, incentives, marketing) are the same three levers named across this entire knowledge base.
2. **Every partial-ownership transition needs a timeline and a role definition.** Wilson's four years of being handcuffed as a 49% owner — then still constrained at 98% — is the strongest argument in the corpus for **defining, in writing at closing, when the seller stops and what they do until then.** Relevant to any Canadian deal with a seller staying on for transition, which is most of them.
3. **Consolidation beats accumulation.** Nine acquisitions became two brands and one headquarters, because five payrolls and ten bank accounts made him a worse operator than a focused competitor his own size. **Chenmark took the same lesson from 14 companies.** For Jay, buying two or three small books in one trade in one city is a very different proposition from buying three different trades — and the integration work is the actual job.
4. **"The most active investing you could ever participate in."** The honest reframe: this is not passive income and not a financial exercise. Wilson was the finance guy and had to learn demand generation and executive hiring anyway, because **at $3M there isn't enough margin to be good at only one thing.**
