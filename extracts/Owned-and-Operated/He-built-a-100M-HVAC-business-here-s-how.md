## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
**The company:** founded 1986 by Peterman's father (an HVAC technician); sons joined from 2011. Formerly commercial and multifamily — **today 100% residential repair, service and replacement.** Three trades (HVAC, plumbing, electrical) plus excavation; roofing sits in a separate company. **$110–120M across 10 locations in Indiana, Ohio, Michigan and Kentucky.**

**Branch economics — the standout numbers in the corpus for multi-location service businesses:**

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| **Bloomington branch revenue** | **$8–9M** |
| **Bloomington branch headcount (non-field)** | **3 people — branch manager, dispatcher, warehouse coordinator.** "That's the only infrastructure that's at a $9 million branch." |
| Smallest branch (newest, Louisville) | ~$7M |
| **Target branch size** | **~$15M, deliberately sub-$20M** |
| **Headquarters (Indianapolis) share of total** | **40–50%** |
| **Minimum viable new branch, year one** | **$3–3.5M** — new Louisville branch running **$200–300K/month** from a February start |
| Growth on an acquired branch | Muncie, Indiana: **$600K revenue at acquisition (early 2021) → $12–15M this year** |

**Why he caps branch size:** "We're not looking to build a $20 million company in Lafayette, Indiana. As they get so big, **then you've got to start adding infrastructure.** How can I build a 15, sub-20 — because then I can still support it centrally and I don't have to put in a bunch of service managers and field supervisors."

**Wilson's water mitigation line — the best ancillary-service economics disclosed anywhere in the corpus:**
- **Average ticket $5,000–6,000**, doing **~$1.5M topline.**
- **"Because it's attached to the plumbing company, there is no overhead. Literally none. It's like 45 to 50% cash flow, which is just absolutely ridiculous."**
- Added in 2020 (he says "too small for it at the time"), inspired by Roto-Rooter, because he was losing drain-cleaning work to a competitor who offered cleanup.

## Staffing reality
**Licensing — a genuinely clever, transferable practice:**
- Peterman **pays employees to sit licensing exams in other states** and then pays a **monthly stipend** to anyone whose licence the company uses.
- **Deliberate redundancy:** "We don't want just two Ohio plumbing licences. We want three or four."
- Wilson notes the mental hurdle: *"You're literally paying someone to eventually compete with you."* Peterman: **"Yeah, it helps to have that to operate."**

**Onboarding — the infrastructure behind the expansion model:**
- **New branch managers work out of the Indianapolis office for three months before their location exists.** "They're just learning all the ins and outs. Then when they go do it on their own, they already have all of our process and procedure."
- All technicians onboard in Indianapolis, **every other Monday, for three days.**
- **A "direct field support" department virtually rides along with each new technician for their first 30–60 days**, coaching in real time. The alternative he rejects: "Send Bill with Jimmy, and Jimmy teach Bill everything, let me know when he's ready — **oh boy, this is a recipe for disaster.**"
- **Two in-house recruiters** who vet candidates and hand shortlists to branch managers, prioritising new launches.

**Management structure at scale:** director of operations → three regional managers → branch managers, with each regional carrying three or four locations.

**The podcast as an internal comms tool** — an unexpectedly good idea. Peterman's solo episodes are recordings of live 6am talks he gives at each branch. "They're all out in their truck every day — what's the easiest way to consume that content?" It doubles as a recruiting and culture signal: candidates listen before interviews, and it acts as "a cultural beacon... especially when you've got layers and layers of management." Most topics come from **problems the company handled badly**: "Hey, we didn't handle this situation right — and then I'll do a talk on how we should have."

## Winning work
**Market selection — "tier 2 markets."**
- Explicitly **not Chicago.** Targets Dayton, Louisville, Lexington, Evansville and similar.
- Two criteria: the town must **support a business**, and the branch must be able to reach ~$15M **without needing its own infrastructure layer.**
- **Every operator they know picks a second location about an hour away.** Wilson notes he has asked Eco, Tommy Mello, Chris, and Logan — "everyone picks an hour."
- **The sequence is people-first:** "When are you going to build your next location? **When I find the leader that can lead a location** — and then we'll figure out where we want to go based on where they want to go."

**Greenfield vs acquisition — Peterman now prefers greenfield, for culture reasons:**
- **The acquisition problem at small scale:** "When you're buying sub-$5 million companies, **their people worked at a small company for a reason.** Then you bring all your process and procedure in and it can be a challenge... **'Well, we did it this way when Bill was in charge.'**"
- The contrast with what PE does: "When they buy a $30 million company, that already has everything set up — it's literally 'we just invested, you keep doing your thing.'"
- **The greenfield trade-off, honestly stated:** "It's a little bit harder in the startup, because you don't have any calls coming in — you've got to go generate them, so **your marketing costs are higher.** But the ease on the back end is so much better... **I don't have to untrain any bad habits.** A little bit slower start, but then it ramps pretty quick."
- **Launch all three trades at once** (HVAC, plumbing, electrical): one branch manager, one technician per trade, one HVAC install crew, go. Reasoning: **electrical and plumbing are not weather-dependent, so they fund the HVAC ramp.** "It's nice to have all three trades that can toss leads from one to the other."
- **Except excavation**, which stays centralised — six or seven crews serving six of the ten locations — because of capex and because "finding somebody who understands excavation is very difficult."

**Marketing at scale — the capacity-board system:**
- "Giant boards that are basically capacity boards, pulling live data from ServiceTitan," telling the call centre where to route and the digital team where to spend.
- **Traditional media is fixed once placed; digital moves daily** — campaigns shut down when a location is full, spend raised when it isn't.
- **The strain he names:** "You essentially have three separate businesses at one location. So you've got **30 different companies** that our marketing team is trying to build capacity for... **every branch and every service line needs something different at all hours of the day.**"

**The cheap-lead thesis — worth noting for any home services buyer.** With HVAC replacement leads getting steadily more expensive, both hosts hunt for adjacent services with under-competed lead pools: **duct cleaning**, **chimney sweeping** ("literally probably $3 leads... there's no LSA going on in the chimney sweep game"), **appliance repair** (Chris's line went from a text to an existing customer base of 8–12,000 to an **$8M service line**), and **water mitigation**. Peterman's framing: "You've built this base — **what else do they need done on their home?**"

## The grind
- **"We added three locations in a year. It was not fun."** (2021.) "There was a lot of just — what are we doing? How do we manage all of this?"
- The first expansion was unglamorous: a service manager wanted to run his own location, an acquisition fell through, "so we literally just opened up in Lafayette, hired a team and just kind of went for it. **It was very messy at the beginning.**" They were $10–15M in revenue at the time; **"looking back, would I have done that at that point? Probably not."**
- **The focus cost:** "Indianapolis is a big city, you could build a massive company here. It's easy to lose focus and just say 'I'll drop another location and they should do $3 million this year' — **but it comes at a cost, because you give up your focus on other things.**"

## Cash flow traps
- Greenfield locations carry **higher marketing cost per lead** during the ramp, with no inherited call flow.
- Multi-location multi-trade creates a **marketing capacity-management problem that scales multiplicatively** (locations × service lines), and Peterman is hiring more digital staff specifically to fix it.
- Excavation's capex and talent scarcity is why it stays centralised — a warning about which service lines can be replicated and which cannot.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not addressed directly, but the acquisition target profile is instructive: a **$600K-revenue company in Muncie** that has since grown 20-fold under a system. The seller had built something the system could scale but they could not.

## What sellers hide
- **Ask what the branch overhead actually is.** A $9M branch running on three non-field staff is the benchmark. If a target's back office is materially heavier than that per dollar of revenue, either there's fat to cut or the revenue quality is worse than it looks.
- **Ask how licences are held and how many exist per jurisdiction.** Peterman deliberately holds three or four per state. **A target with one licence-holder — usually the owner — is a business that stops on closing day.** This is the same warning as `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`, and it applies to Canadian pesticide, gas-fitting and well-drilling licences.
- **Ask about the onboarding system, specifically.** "Send Bill with Jimmy" is how nearly every small service business trains. That is not a transferable asset, and it means your first year's hires will be materially worse than the seller's tenured staff.
- **Ask what share of revenue sits in the headquarters location.** Peterman's HQ is 40–50% of a ten-branch business. Concentration is normal — but you need to know it before you value the "diversified" story.
- **Ask which ancillary lines exist and what their incremental overhead is.** Wilson's water mitigation at **45–50% cash flow because it rides on existing plumbing overhead** is the pattern to look for: services that use a customer base and dispatch you already pay for. In a Canadian janitorial or pest control target, the equivalents are floor care, window cleaning, duct cleaning, or wildlife exclusion — check whether the seller has left that revenue on the table (upside) or is booking it at fully-loaded cost (already captured).
- **Ask how the business finds work when a location or service line is slow.** Capacity boards and daily digital adjustment are what a professional operation looks like; "the phone rings" is what a business dependent on the owner's reputation looks like.

## Verdict
**A masterclass in what a service business looks like several stages beyond where Jay will start — and useful precisely for that reason.** Nothing here is directly executable on a first acquisition, but three things reframe how to evaluate one.

**First, the branch-overhead benchmark.** *$9M of revenue supported by three non-field staff.* That number is the yardstick for judging whether any service business's back office is right-sized. It also reveals where the leverage in these businesses actually is: field productivity and centralised support, not local administration.

**Second, the greenfield-versus-acquisition argument, from someone who has done both.** Peterman's conclusion — greenfield costs more in marketing up front and far less in culture on the back end, because you never untrain "we did it this way when Bill was in charge" — is the same conclusion Wilson reaches in `extracts/Owned-and-Operated/Buy-vs.-Build-Start-or-Acquire-Your-First-Business-220.md`. **Two operators at very different scales independently identify inherited culture as the main cost of buying small.** For Jay, that is the risk to underwrite: not the numbers, the people.

**Third, the ancillary-service play as a value-creation lever.** Water mitigation at 45–50% cash flow on existing overhead, appliance repair turning a customer list into an $8M line, $3 chimney-sweep leads — the pattern is **buy a customer base and dispatch capacity, then add margin-rich services that use both.** That is a concrete post-close plan for a Canadian janitorial or pest control book, and it's cheaper than buying growth.

Also worth stealing outright: **paying employees to hold licences with a monthly stipend and building deliberate redundancy**, and **using a podcast or recorded talks as internal communication** for a workforce that is in trucks all day and never in an office.

**US (Midwest) — nothing jurisdiction-specific except licensing regimes and the Ohio River energy-standards remark.** In Canada the licensing-redundancy idea is more valuable, not less, because trade and applicator licences are provincial and individual, and a single-licence business is genuinely unbuyable without a plan for the licence.
