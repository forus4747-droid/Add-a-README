His deliberate list of what NOT to buy: big power sprayers ("largely phased out over the past five years"), bed bug heaters that sit unused, a $10–20K website, an office, a warehouse.

## Staffing reality
- **Run it out of the truck.** No office, no warehouse, no admin. (Check provincial chemical-storage rules for vehicles.)
- Techs prefer dense routes: "the techs loved it 'cause they're making more money, they're not having much windshield time."
- **The scaling constraint is people, not demand:** "it's harder to scale 'cause you need more people and it's a people-based business. It takes a lot longer than people are willing to wait."

## Winning work — the density doctrine
- **Pick ONE neighbourhood of a couple hundred homes, not a city.** "You don't need to dominate the entire city your first year. You need to dominate your zip codes."
- **Why density wins, three reasons he names:** reduces drive time, increases visibility (customers see the truck constantly), and visibility builds trust.
- **His actual ramp on a new location:** day 1 = **3 accounts**; day 2 = **7**; week 2 = **~50 jobs**; **end of month one = 200–300 jobs, all in one subdivision** — a full quarterly route built in a single neighbourhood, with no ad spend.
- **The Law of 100** (credited to Alex Hormozi): 100 conversations, 100 doors, 100 door hangers, 100 EDDM mailers, 100 texts, 100 emails — **every day, six or seven days a week.** Thirty days = 2,400 touches per channel.
- **"Nine-arounds"** — after every job, hit **two houses left, two right, five across the street.** Script: *"We're servicing your neighbour Sally Smith today, and because my truck's in the area I'll do this first initial service for XYZ price — saves you money, saves me time."*
- **Yard sign on every lawn**, including friends' and family's.
- **Ask for five referrals before leaving**: *"Since my service looks so good and you're happy with me — do you have five people I can reach out to?"* "More than likely they'll give you one... a lot of times they'll call them for you, they'll walk you to the neighbour's house."
- Paid channels (Google reviews strategy, Local Services Ads, Facebook) come **after the first 100–150 customers**, to amplify density — not to create it.

## The grind
- "Pest control is simple. I didn't say that it was easy."
- "You're going to have a whole street that doesn't answer. You're going to get told no. **You're going to question yourself for the first 60 days.**"
- "**The hardest part is going from being a technician** — doing the work is super easy, anyone can do this work. **Getting the jobs is the hard part.**"
- His honest time horizon: "it's the people who are willing to stay in this industry and endure as much pain as possible over the next 15, 20, 30 years that win."

## Cash flow traps — his four killers
1. **Underpricing.** The mechanism is precise: a new operator Googles competitors, finds an incumbent at **$89/quarter** who "has been doing it since 1950 and never raised their prices," and undercuts at **$39/quarter**. Now they're the cheapest in town, driving across the city, losing money on the drive *and* on the job. "They don't even know their numbers, so they're just guessing."
2. **Not collecting payment information up front.** His best rant: "You can't go get fuel and just be like, 'yeah, I'm just going to pay you a month later.'" **Card on file from day one, in the CRM. "If they're not home and they don't have a card on file, just don't do the job."** — "It's hard to grow a business off of accounts receivable. It's not possible."
3. **Too many services at once.** Pest plus construction plus landscaping plus wildlife. "Stick with one or two services, master those. **Don't add services until at least a million bucks.**" (Same specialization argument as `extracts/Cleaning-operators/10-Years-of-Cleaning-Business-Advice-in-21-Minutes.md`.)
4. **Quitting too early.** Most people get 25–100 customers and burn out.
- Also: **decide premium vs cheap on day one.** "You can't go have a thousand customers and then decide you're going to go to your premium model."

## Why owners quit/sell — the single most useful passage for a buyer
> "If you're making $150,000 a year, you kind of get stuck there 'cause you're making really good money, you're not pushing so hard. **So people get stuck there for so many years 'cause they just live a comfortable life**... there's nothing wrong with that if you want a little lifestyle business. **But when you go to sell it, it's just not going to be worth a whole lot of money.**"

That is the profile of the typical Canadian pest control seller: a one- or two-truck owner-operator, comfortable at $150K a year for a decade, who never systematised, never raised prices, and now finds the business is worth far less than the income it produced. **That gap between a good income and a low sale price is precisely where a buyer's return lives** — and it's also the reason the seller may be emotionally unprepared for the valuation.

## What sellers hide
- **Revenue per customer is the whole valuation.** At 1,000 customers, the difference between a $418 book and a $900 book is **$450K of revenue on identical operations.** Demand the customer count and the actual average annual revenue per customer — then compare to the $400–500 median. A seller well below it has either a pricing problem (fixable, upside) or a service-mix problem (not).
- **The $89/quarter legacy-pricing problem is endemic.** Ask when prices were last raised across the whole book, not just for new customers. An unraised book has real upside — and real churn risk when you fix it.
- **250 services per quarter is the honest limit for one truck.** If a seller claims 1,200 customers on one truck, either the service is being skipped or the numbers are wrong. Count trucks against customers as a first-pass credibility test.
- **Margin claims need the owner's labour stripped out.** A $300K/one-truck business "at 40% margin" is paying the owner $150K to drive. Subtract a fully-burdened technician's wage before you call any of that profit. The industry *median* net margin is **10%** — closer to what a business with hired technicians actually earns.
- **Ask whether payment is on file.** A book billed in arrears carries receivable risk and a collections workload the P&L won't show. A book on auto-pay ACH/credit card is worth more.
- **Map the customer addresses.** Density is the difference between his numbers and a loss. A 1,000-customer book scattered across a metro is not the same asset as 1,000 customers in eight subdivisions, at any price.
- **Ask how many services the business offers.** A pest company also doing landscaping and construction has no specialization, no pricing power, and probably no accurate per-line margins.

## Verdict
**The best build-side benchmark document in the corpus, and it doubles as a buy-side valuation tool.** Every number Jay needs to sanity-check a Canadian pest control acquisition is here: **$400–500 revenue per residential customer per year, ~250 services per quarter per truck, ~10% median net margin at scale, 25–50% for a stabilised owner-operator.** Multiply a target's customer count by $450 and compare to their stated revenue; count their trucks against their service load. Discrepancies are where diligence starts.

It also frames the **buy-vs-build question honestly**. He'd start from scratch with $20K and hit 200–300 accounts in one neighbourhood in month one. That's a real alternative to paying 2–3× SDE for someone else's book — but note what he's actually selling: seven-day weeks, 100 doors a day, and 60 days of self-doubt. **Buying skips the grind and buys route density that would take a year to build.** Price the acquisition against that, not against zero.

**US — verify for Canada, three items.** (1) **Licensing is provincial, not state** — Ontario's structural exterminator licence classes, Quebec's *Code de gestion des pesticides*, etc. Confirm the class needed and whether the *business* or the *individual* holds it; a solo seller's licence generally does not transfer. (2) **Ontario and Quebec cosmetic-pesticide restrictions** limit residential outdoor products and change both the service mix and the achievable revenue per customer — his $450 assumes a US product set. (3) **Seasonality**: his year-round model assumes US pest pressure; a Canadian book faces 5–6 dormant months (see `extracts/Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`), so annual revenue per customer must be earned in fewer visits or held by prepaid annual plans.
