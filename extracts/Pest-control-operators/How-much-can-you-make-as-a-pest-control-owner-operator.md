**Rate card and time per stop:**
- **Average recurring customer: ~$120 per service.** Low end $105–110; legacy customers $75–85 (being price-increased out); older account at $110.
- **Time on site: 18–22 minutes** per home ("I'm usually at home for 20 maybe 25 minutes"). He contrasts with operators who spend 45 minutes.
- **A full one-time service is ~$175.**
- **Property management work: $99 per targeted unit**, ~15 minutes each including drive.
- **The apartment complex: $310/visit, 2 buildings per week, ~1 hour on site.** Eight buildings including the leasing office rotated across four weeks = monthly coverage for every building. That's **~$1,240/month from one account for ~4 hours of work.**

**The route-density day — the most instructive number here:**
- Monday: **5 customers at $120 = $600 in 90 minutes**, all within his own neighbourhood (three houses on one street, one a block away, one seven or eight doors down). Started 9:15, done before 11:00.
- **$400/hour when the route is dense.** The same $120 stop across town is a fraction of that.

**Week to date (Wed):** $600 (Mon) + ~$900 (Tue, 9–10 stops) + $1,310 (Wed) = **~$2,810 in three days.**

**His own extrapolation:** "$1,000 a day, five days a week, you're looking at $20,000 a month" — and he calls his own pace **the low end**: "there's other guys that can do 13, 14, 15 stops a day and work eight or nine hours."

## Staffing reality
- **He is the staff.** One truck, one operator, wife doing some scheduling. This is the owner-operator baseline against which every "pest control business" for sale should be measured.
- Route software: **FieldRoutes.**
- Stated ambition — and the reason businesses like this come to market: **"I want to have a team and I want to get out of a truck."**

## Winning work
- **Property management companies are the growth lever:** "if you can get in with the property management company, do so because they are money, man. They're going to send you work."
- The property-manager relationship is explicitly scoped: "when we have an issue, all we need is that issue taken care of" — no upsell to a $175 full service. Lower ticket, near-zero acquisition cost, high frequency.
- His demand-generation list is unglamorous and entirely local: **be present on social media, collect reviews, great customer service, be involved in the community.** "People just need to know who you are."

## The grind
- Honest about how ordinary it is: "I know it was a pretty boring video... all of my stops were extremely simple, very very basic. I didn't have any rodent jobs, I didn't have any German roaches."
- Physical but not hard: three-floor apartment buildings — "gets me some steps, keeps me moving... at the end of the day it's not very difficult."
- The lifestyle upside is real and is the actual product: he front-loaded a light Monday to take his daughter to a movie. This is why these businesses are bought and why owners hang on to them too long.
- Seasonal shift visible in the work: "as we're getting into the fall/winter time" the job becomes checking rodent bait stations.

## Cash flow traps
- **Re-services are free and they are a cost of doing business.** One of 11 stops earned $0. "That's just the nature of the game — you're going to have a touch-up every now and again." He notes he doesn't have many, which is a *quality* signal.
- **Legacy underpriced customers.** He carries a handful at $75–85 that he is actively raising. Any acquired book will have these; they are found margin, but raising them causes churn.
- **Drive time is the silent killer.** The gap between $400/hour (dense) and a scattered route is entirely windshield time — see the route-density argument in `extracts/Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`.

## Why owners quit/sell
- He is not selling — he wants to grow out of the truck. But the video shows exactly what a buyer of a small pest route is buying: **a job that pays $1,000/day for six hours' work, whose entire value sits in the owner's relationships, route knowledge and reputation.** The moment he stops driving, the $400/hour becomes a technician's wage plus overhead.

## What sellers hide
- **"Revenue per day" is not transferable — route density is.** Ask for the customer address list and map it. His $600-in-90-minutes day was possible only because five customers lived on two streets. A seller quoting daily revenue without disclosing route geography is quoting a number a new technician cannot reproduce.
- **Ask for the re-service rate**, in stops per hundred. He implies his is low and treats that as a point of pride. A book with 10–15% callbacks has a service-quality problem that will cost you labour hours and customers.
- **Ask what share of revenue is the single largest account.** The apartment complex is **~24% of that day's revenue**. One property manager relationship, likely undocumented, probably personal to the owner. That is textbook customer concentration.
- **Ask for the price distribution, not the average.** "All my customers are right around 120" conceals a tail at $75–85. Model the book at actual prices, and model the churn from fixing them.
- **The owner-operator adjustment.** SDE on a business like this is mostly the owner's own labour. To value it, subtract a real technician's fully-burdened wage for the routes the owner drives. What remains is what you're actually buying. A seller presenting $240K/year of "profit" from a one-truck route is presenting a salary.
- **Ask about the property management contracts specifically:** written or handshake, assignable on change of control, and who at the property company is the relationship.

## Verdict
The most valuable **ground-truth** document in the pest set: real stops, real prices, real hours, disclosed by someone with no deal to sell. Memorise three numbers — **~$120 per recurring residential stop, 18–22 minutes on site, and 10–11 stops a day at the relaxed end** — and you can sanity-check any pest control business's claimed revenue against its truck count in about thirty seconds. If the numbers only work at 15+ stops a day, the seller is assuming a pace nobody sustains.

**US — verify for Canada.** Pricing is US Sun Belt, where year-round pest pressure supports quarterly service without a dead season; Canadian route economics face **5–6 dormant months** (see the recurring-revenue extract), so a Canadian $120 quarterly equivalent must carry the winter overhead. Also **applicator licensing is provincial** in Canada and must be held by the person doing the work — a solo owner-operator's licence does not transfer with the business, and the buyer or an employee must be licensed before closing. Verify with the provincial ministry before committing.
