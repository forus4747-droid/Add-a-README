## Unit economics from the owner's mouth
- **One-off service: ~$250**, roughly **1 hour** on site. Then you pay CAC again to replace that customer.
- **Quarterly protection plan: $45–$100/month**, i.e. **$135–$300 per quarter**.
- Entry plan at **$45/mo = $540/yr**. At an **average 3-year retention**, that's **~$1,600 lifetime value per customer**.
- **The core insight: CAC is the same for both.** Same marketing spend buys either a **$250 transaction** or a **$1,600 asset**.
- **500 homeowners × $45/mo = $22,000+ MRR** before a truck rolls.

**Tier structure:**

| Tier | Scope | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Exterior barrier treatment, spider webs, wasp nests | ~$45/mo |
| Whole-home | + exclusion work, rodent/mice prevention, trap setting | from ~$60/mo |
| Premium | + interior preventive treatment, mosquito treatments | $75–$100+/mo |

- **Treatments last ~90 days** — both the reason quarterly is the natural cadence and the honest sales argument for it.

## Staffing reality
- The **exterior-only foundation plan needs no homeowner present.** Tech pulls up, treats the perimeter, clears webs, checks for wasps, leaves a yard sign, triggers an automated follow-up, and drives on. This kills no-shows, locked doors, and scheduling friction in one design decision — the most operationally elegant idea in the operator half of this corpus.
- Techs must be retrained "to stop thinking like laborers just providing a service and instead... think like consultants."
- **Commission structure** for techs who convert a one-off into a recurring plan.

## Winning work
- **Sell the plan immediately after solving the pest problem** — "they're already excited that you've gotten rid of their problem, their guard is down, they know you produce results."
- **The conversion script:** instead of billing the $250 emergency call, offer to start them on the $45/mo plan covering that same pest on the exterior; mention the treatment only lasts 90 days. Then let them choose. Either outcome is a win — full one-time fee, or a locked-in subscriber.
- **Route density** via door-to-door or neighbour outreach: once you have a few homes in an area, canvass the surrounding streets with a discounted plan offer.

## The grind
- Scattered one-off jobs mean techs "traveling from place to place, wasting a lot of time, effort, energy, fuel, and resources." Route density is the difference between a technician servicing several homes in a short window and one crossing the city all day.

## Cash flow traps — the defining one for this industry
- **Seasonality is brutal on a one-off book.** Summer and fall: "phone is ringing off the hook, trucks are moving, cash is flowing." Late fall into winter: "a lot of the bugs go dormant and your phone stops ringing, and suddenly you're stressing out about how to cover your payroll and how to make those truck payments."
- **Truck payments do not go dormant.** Fixed equipment debt against seasonal revenue is the specific trap.

## Why owners quit/sell
- The diagnosis is the whole thesis: **"you don't have a scalable business, you have a high-stress seasonal job."** Owners of one-off pest books burn out on the winter cash crunch, and they sell into it.

## What sellers hide
- **The valuation line, stated outright:** "your company isn't valued based on how many trucks you have or how good you are at killing bugs — it's determined by one key metric, and that's monthly recurring revenue." A seller leading with truck count, tech count, or gross revenue is leading with the wrong metric on purpose.
- **Demand the recurring/one-off revenue split**, in dollars, by month, for 36 months. A pest business with a big summer and a dead January is a one-off book no matter what the seller calls it.
- **Demand real retention data**, not the 3-year rule of thumb. Recurring revenue with 18-month churn is a treadmill; with 4-year retention it's an annuity. This number moves the multiple more than anything else.
- **Check whether "recurring" means auto-billed.** Plans on file with no payment method, no auto-renew, and no signed term are a mailing list, not MRR.
- **Route density is verifiable.** Ask for the customer address list and map it. A dense book supports the stated margins; a book scattered across the region has hidden windshield time that will show up as labour cost the moment you own it.

## Verdict
The most valuation-relevant operator video in the pest set: it names the exact metric that separates a **$1,600 asset from a $250 transaction**, and explains why a pest business with a January cash crisis is a *structural* problem, not a bad month. For Jay, this is the pest-control acquisition thesis in miniature — **buy the recurring book, not the trucks** — and price any one-off-heavy business as a seasonal job with a payroll problem. Two Canadian caveats: the seasonal dead zone is **longer and deeper in Canada** than in the US Sun Belt this advice is written for (a Canadian pest book may see 5–6 dormant months, not 3), which makes recurring revenue *more* essential and makes exterior-only winter service partly impractical; and **provincial applicator licensing** must transfer or be re-issued on a change of control. Verify both.
