- **Why this one is unusual:** the seller is a recent buyer, so you get an honest post-mortem on **what went wrong after an SBA acquisition closed** — the part almost no interview covers.

## Key numbers
- Pinto **roughly doubled revenue in ~18 months** after acquiring George's Scapes.
- **Cost to stand up one new landscaping crew: $60,000–70,000** — truck, one or two mowers, hand tools. "It's not super capital intensive, but it's not *not* capital intensive."
- **His underwriting model assumed ~10% annual growth.** Actual was ~100%. **The working capital was sized off the wrong number.**
- **He approached ~30 banks** for a line of credit — including his own business bank, with visible positive cash flow, existing personal banking relationships, and **an offer to personally guarantee.** All refused.
- **Botkin's sixth acquisition.** Vendors were demanding personal guarantees from newly-acquired entities at spend levels from **$1,000–10,000/month** all the way to **$50,000–100,000/month.**
- Deal structure: cash plus a **significant rolled equity stake**; One Outdoor holds **51%+**; Pinto stays as the Georgia platform lead.

## What to look for
**The post-close working capital trap — the most valuable content in the corpus on this topic**

This is a failure mode that almost nobody warns buyers about, and it stems from three decisions that each look correct in isolation:

1. **The SBA lender offered no line of credit.** Working capital had to be a fixed sum added to the balance sheet at close.
2. **That sum was sized from the target's *historical* financials** — i.e. from the growth rate of the tired owner who was selling, not the buyer who was about to double it.
3. **The deal was an asset sale**, so the operating business became a **brand-new entity with no credit history.**

The result: *"I'm basically having to go to dealerships saying we need to buy a truck — literally the least controversial purchase there is — and we're being faced with huge interest rates, all because we had done an asset sale and created this new entity with limited credit history."*

**The line he wants every buyer to hear:**
> "**If you don't get that line of credit as part of your SBA package, your ability to get it afterwards is essentially zero** for a year or two or three in some instances, until you develop significant credit history."

And Botkin confirms it's universal: *"What Adrian experienced is 100% the normal experience. We experienced it on our first two acquisitions. **I haven't met a newly acquired business owner that has not faced those challenges.**"*

**The personal guarantee stacking problem.** Botkin's framing: "An SBA searcher just personally guaranteed an SBA loan, takes over a business, is already on the hook personally — **and then immediately post-close has to start putting on more personal guarantees** for vendors."

**What it actually cost:**
- Pinto **personally bought a truck outright and lent it to the business as a shareholder loan**, because dealership financing rates were unacceptable.
- Growth decisions got deferred: *"I'm generally of the mindset that if someone comes to you with a good reason to buy a moderately priced piece of equipment, you should do it — especially if it's going to increase productivity. **But there have been times when we haven't been able to say yes.**"*
- The subtler cost — **motivation decay**: "You're kind of immediately demotivated to just go do that again because of what you just went through... **you lose a little motivation to go reach out to new prospective customers**, because you'd have to start this process all over again."
- Botkin's summary: he was spending his time "calling 75 banks to get a loan for a car" instead of on staff, margins, or customers.

**Asset vs share sale — the argument for asset, with a live example**
Will Smith raises the obvious counter: a **share purchase** preserves the entity's credit history and avoids this whole problem. Botkin's rebuttal is a story:
> "The last acquisition we did — **the third day we were there, sitting in the conference room with the owners, they got served papers for a car accident from four years ago.** Luckily we did assets. If it had been stock, it would have been me signing that I got served."

His principle: "Some of these businesses have been around 10, 15, 20, 30 years. **You don't know what's behind some closed doors. Everyone has some cobwebs.**"

**So the trade is explicit and unavoidable: an asset purchase buys you a clean slate and costs you the credit history.** Knowing that, the answer is to **negotiate the line of credit into the financing package at close** — because you cannot get it afterwards.

**The financing-product gap Pinto identifies** is worth noting as a real market failure: "No one is looking at *what percent of employees are the same, what percent of customers are the same, what was the revenue* — okay, this is essentially the same business even if it's under a different entity. **No one's really willing to have that discussion.** They have these guidelines, and if you don't check all the boxes you're nixed."

**On why he chose that lender anyway:** "**Speed and certainty of close were the highest criteria** I looked at." A defensible choice — and a reminder that optimising for closing can cost you for years afterwards.

## Deal structure — rolled equity and the "second bite"
- Pinto **rolled a significant portion of his proceeds into the new entity** rather than taking all cash. It was **a day-one requirement from Botkin** and something Pinto wanted anyway.
- **Why the buyer wants it:** alignment of interest and motivation.
- **Why the seller wants it:** it resolves the valuation impasse. Pinto: "It's difficult to capture all that value today when you believe in a two- or three-year trajectory. If I can roll a significant amount, that allows me to get **that second bite at the apple**."
- Botkin's requirement for all his owners: **"They need to roll a significant stake where it is very meaningful to them and meaningful to us. Our goal and hope is the second bite is worth more than the first bite, even at a much lesser equity ownership."**
- Liquidity for rolled holders comes from either a future sale of the whole platform or **share buybacks at periodic valuations.**

**The valuation standoff, and how they broke it**
The classic problem: the seller wants credit for future growth; the buyer's rule is that they don't pay for what they intend to create. Pinto's approach: *"I said, listen — **for this to work you're probably going to have to feel like you guys are stretching a little**, because there's no other way I'm going to feel like enough value is being captured."*

What made it workable: he had **recently signed, sizeable contracts in hand that weren't yet in the financials** — something concrete to point to, rather than "we believe we can do all this stuff." **That's the practical lesson for both sides: signed-but-unbilled contracts are the legitimate bridge between trailing financials and forward value.**

**Botkin's test for a fair deal, which is worth keeping:**
> "A good deal is when you can put all the deal points up on a whiteboard, have the two principals sit there, and **each say: I would take your side if I were you.**"

And his honest reckoning: "If you had a couple drinks with me I'd say yeah, we probably stretched a little. If you had a few beers in Adrian, he'd say I think I got a little more than X but I should have got more. **I think we're both overall happy.**"

## Thesis and industry structure
- **Botkin's roll-up thesis has migrated from businesses to people:** "Each acquisition we do is bigger than the last, and we've seen that **it's really not about the actual landscaping business — it's about the person running it, the team, the customer base.** The landscaping business is a commodity... **it's a talent game, not a business acquisition game.**"
- **His first few acquisitions had no owner staying on**, and he made himself the de facto owner-operator — deliberately: "**I needed to learn the industry.** If I was going to sit down at a lunch with a landscaping owner, I need to have sympathy and empathy and general understanding of what they go through day to day." *That is a genuinely good argument for a first-time buyer to run the business personally for a while.*
- **Now he requires owners to stay** and roll equity.
- **The service-mix thesis:** "**Commercial maintenance is the focal point.** It doesn't matter if you have 80% construction or 20% construction — **that construction cash either needs to be used to grow commercial maintenance, or those opportunities need to result in commercial maintenance at the end of those projects.**" (Compare `extracts/Acquiring-Minds/Lessons-from-a-Founder-Who-Then-Started-Acquiring-landscaping.md`, where the maintenance route generates the construction work.)
- **How the partnership formed: a year of friendship first.** "It's hard to have trust in someone you meet one day and 90 days later you're closing on their business. **I was able to get to know Adrian on a level that had nothing to do with acquisition.**" Pinto **turned down the first serious offer.**

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout (SBA, Florida/Georgia).** But **the working-capital lesson is the most important thing in this extract for a Canadian buyer, and it is worse in Canada, not better.**
  - Canadian acquisition financing is even more constrained: **CSBFP funds assets only, caps at $1.15M, and does not provide operating lines.** An operating line is a separate facility, separately underwritten.
  - **A new Canadian holdco or opco formed for an asset purchase has exactly the same credit-history problem** Pinto describes. Chartered banks apply the same "how long has this entity existed" screens.
  - **Action for Jay: negotiate the operating line, the equipment financing facility, and the acquisition debt as one package, before closing.** Ask each lender explicitly whether an operating line is included and how it is sized — and push for it to be sized off *your* growth plan, not the seller's history.
  - **BDC is the Canadian institution most likely to bridge this gap** (working capital and equipment financing alongside acquisition debt). Verify current products directly.
- **The asset-vs-share trade is different in Canada.** Botkin does asset deals to escape historical liabilities — but in Canada, **an asset purchase does not escape employment liabilities** (accrued service, notice, severance generally travel with employees) and may not escape WSIB/WCB exposure. See `extracts/David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`. Meanwhile Canadian *sellers* have a strong tax incentive to demand a **share** sale (the $1.25M LCGE — see `extracts/Canadian-tax/ACCOUNTANT-EXPLAINS-Canada-s-Capital-Gains-Tax.md`). **So the Canadian buyer is likelier to end up in a share deal than a US buyer — which incidentally solves the credit-history problem, at the cost of inheriting the cobwebs.** Price the indemnities and holdbacks accordingly.
- **Seasonality changes the working-capital maths further.** A Canadian landscaping business with a seven-to-eight-month maintenance season and a winter snow book has a far more volatile cash cycle than a Florida or Georgia route. **The operating line is not optional in Canada; it is the business.**
- **Rolled equity / second-bite structures work identically in Canada** and are worth proposing to a seller who believes in the trajectory but wants liquidity — it is often a better answer to a valuation gap than an earnout.

## Verdict
**The single most useful episode in the corpus on what happens in the twelve months *after* an acquisition closes**, and the only one that treats post-close financing as a first-class risk.

Three things to act on:

1. **Negotiate the operating line and equipment facility into the financing package before close, and size them off your growth plan rather than the seller's history.** Pinto's model assumed 10% growth and delivered 100%; that gap cost him a year of management attention, forced him to fund a truck personally, and made him defer productive equipment purchases. **The lender's answer after closing is no.** This is arguably the highest-value single sentence in this entire knowledge base for a first-time buyer.
2. **Understand the asset-versus-share trade properly.** Asset gives you a clean slate and costs you credit history; share gives you credit history and the cobwebs. In Canada the seller's LCGE will push hard toward a share deal — take it, but with real indemnities, a holdback, and a searching look for the four-year-old car accident.
3. **Rolled equity is the honest solution to a valuation gap** when the seller believes in the trajectory. It aligns both sides, gives the seller a second bite, and avoids the gameable mechanics of an earnout (see `extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-that-Makes-Sellers-Love-You.md`).

Also worth internalising: **Botkin ran his first acquisitions himself specifically to learn the trade**, and now buys operators rather than businesses. For Jay — buying into an industry he hasn't worked in — that sequence is the right one. Run it personally first; the credibility to buy well from the next tired owner comes from having done the work.
