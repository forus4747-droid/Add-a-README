## What to look for
**The capital structure argument — the most transferable idea here, and it cuts against the grain**

The standard search-fund/ETA approach is a ~90% LTV SBA loan. Foster's objection is precise and worth quoting:

> "The 90% LTV SBA loan works really well **if you're a searcher trying to buy a company and your value creation plan is *I'm going to own this for five or ten or fifteen years and compound organically*.** But if you do what we did and you start with a 90% SBA loan, **you may run out of runway really, really quickly, because you want to do a lot of deals.** We've done 15 deals in two years — that wouldn't have been possible if we had started with that traditional SBA loan."

And Will Smith's framing of *why* most buyers use maximum leverage is the honest one: **"For a lot of people they do it that way simply because they don't have access to equity to do it in any other way."** Not because it's optimal — because it's available.

**Two further advantages of holding equity capacity:**
1. **De-risking the unknown.** They fully equitized the first deal deliberately: *"We wanted to remove any potential stressors until we got comfortable with the health of the business. It was all new to us. We didn't really want that pressure from a lender."* Then they put debt on **retroactively**, once they understood the cash flow profile — freeing capital for the next deal.
2. **Doing deals a bank would refuse.** *"One three-unit acquisition in particular... we knew the situation in extremely close detail, we had employees who had worked for that other owner, there were reasons why the stores were not performing the way they should with the right operator. **That was not an acquisition an SBA lender would have financed.** The stores have been a home run."*

**For Jay this is a genuine strategic fork:** a single Canadian service business held for a decade justifies maximum leverage and a large VTB. A programme of several small tuck-ins does not — it needs equity capacity preserved, because **each maxed-out deal forecloses the next one.**

**The franchise thesis — "paying a royalty for product market fit"**

Foster's framing is unusual and useful: *"I think of our royalty as paying for a concept that has been proven to work in a lot of different markets by a lot of different operators."* He treats support, SOPs and national marketing as upside on top, so he's "always at peace with it."

**Why a legacy franchise is a roll-up vehicle specifically:**
- **A built-in target list.** Buy one unit and you have the contact details, and often the sales figures, of 799 others. *(Same mechanic Brian Beers describes in `extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md`.)*
- **A mature brand of ageing operators** guarantees deal flow: "we knew there would be a frothy M&A environment because it was a mature brand comprised of older operators."
- **Hyper-fragmentation with no consolidator present** — the competitive edge.
- **Integration muscle memory.** "Because the service offering is relatively consistent post-acquisition, the issues we're facing in Appleton, Wisconsin or Rock Hill, South Carolina tend to be pretty similar... **we are not starting fresh in every market.**"
- **Brand trust in a low-trust category.** "People are inherently distrustful of auto repair facilities... people naturally trust brands they recognise." *(Directly applicable to pest control and home services in Canada, where trust is the purchase driver.)*

**The industry-selection funnel is a clean piece of thinking:** auto services → rejected performance/discretionary (uninteresting), **collision** (too many sophisticated operators, smart money already in), **quick lube** (good unit economics, but EV exposure at exit), **car wash** (over-penetrated, capital intensive, slow to develop) → **repair**, "a little bit in an earlier inning."

**They hired the operator before the first deal.** Both are explicit that they are not auto experts — *"Jack and I can only do an oil change with supervision."* **Joe, a master mechanic and former regional manager for a similar brand, joined before the first three stores closed.** Their principle: *"We knew we were going to be operationally involved, but we weren't going to be the operator. We had to have somebody with experience because we wanted to move quickly."*

**And they moved into the market.** All three lived in a rented house in Asheville for ~6 months, in the stores every day. *"I think everyone thought we were a little bit crazy — who were these three guys from the north living in Asheville operating a couple of garages."*

**On the EV headwind** — a good example of separating a headline risk from a P&L risk: oil change is 8–10% of sales and **zero percent of profit** (it's an inspection opportunity); they work on 4–8 year old vehicles; the US car park is a few percent EV and the average car stays on the road 12 years. They deliberately avoid coastal markets where EV penetration is highest, and have trained technicians at EV-certified locations as a hedge.

## Structure details
- **The raise:** ~$2.8M from friends and family and prior professional networks. **"Everyone invested on the same terms. It was as vanilla as vanilla gets."** Both founders put in everything they had — "folks knew we were totally committed."
- **Capital was committed but not called** until each deal, and restricted to Meineke acquisitions or independents converting to Meineke.
- **No timeline pressure**, no forced sale date, no preferred return mechanics described — "flexible capital."
- **Pipeline before the raise:** they had three units under LOI and **seven more in the pipeline** when they closed the first deal. They now own all ten.
- **Real estate is a deliberate second strategy** — buying the dirt under existing units, or buying an independent garage's property and converting it. Real-estate debt is "lower cost, longer amortization, and doesn't have the working capital constraints business debt might have." They also do **sale-leasebacks** to recycle capital.

## The grind / motivation
- McLaughlin: **"I detested the concept of grinding for someone else's benefit"** — wanting a symmetrical relationship between time invested and earnings potential, versus banking and PE where "your earnings potential is really capped."
- On their former superiors: *"I had a lot of respect for them, people far more intelligent than I am... **but I didn't envy the lives that they'd built** — not as involved in family life."*
- Foster's distinction between **investing and allocating**: in traditional PE "you may be doing one portfolio company acquisition over two years... diligencing industries, working on investor materials, turning PowerPoint pages — **things that are frankly not investing.**"
- The competitive advantage of small: **"We're willing to start really, really small and bet on ourselves, in a world where a lot of firms want $5 million of EBITDA."**

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout; auto repair franchising.** Four things translate:
  1. **The leverage-versus-optionality trade-off is sharper in Canada, not softer.** With no SBA equivalent and CSBFP capped at $1.15M, a Canadian buyer has *less* debt available per deal — which makes preserving equity capacity for subsequent tuck-ins even more important if the plan is multiple acquisitions. **Decide up front: one business held long, or a programme of small ones.** They demand different capital structures.
  2. **Real-estate debt is the cheap money in Canada too.** CSBFP finances real property at up to 90% with 25-year amortisation versus 10 years for equipment — the same structural advantage Dan Drake exploited (`extracts/Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`). **Where a Canadian service business owns its yard, shop or depot, that property is the most financeable thing in the deal.**
  3. **Franchise roll-ups are viable in Canada** — and the relevant Canadian brands in Jay's space include janitorial franchises (Jan-Pro, Coverall, Bee-Clean), lawn care (Weed Man, originally Canadian), and home services (Mr. Rooter, Molly Maid). **The same closed-loop target-list logic applies.** But note the constraint from `extracts/UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future.md`: **a franchise agreement is not freely transferable; the franchisor approves the buyer**, so verify transfer terms, territory rights and renewal before building a thesis on one.
  4. **The "hire the operator before the first deal" principle** is the single most transferable operating lesson, and it directly addresses Jay's gap — buying into an industry he hasn't worked in. Wilson and Carr make the same point from the other direction in `extracts/Owned-and-Operated/Buy-vs.-Build-Start-or-Acquire-Your-First-Business-220.md`: **if you can't run the work yourself, you must secure someone who can, before closing.**

## Verdict
**Off-thesis industry, one genuinely important structural argument, and a good model of humility about operating expertise.**

The idea worth carrying forward is the **capital-structure fork**. Almost every other source in this corpus assumes maximum leverage on a single acquisition, because that is what a solo buyer with limited equity can do. Foster and McLaughlin show what the alternative looks like: **raise modest equity from people who know you, keep it callable, buy the first deal unlevered, learn the business, then add debt retroactively to fund the next one.** For a Canadian buyer contemplating more than one small service business — buying two janitorial books in one city, or a landscaping company plus a snow contractor — **that sequencing is the difference between a programme and a single bet.**

Two supporting lessons. **Hire the industry operator before you close**, and be honest that you are not one. And **move to the market and be in the business daily at the start** — the same conclusion Mike Botkin reached in `extracts/Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md` after running his early acquisitions himself to learn the trade.

The honest caveat: **$2.8M of friends-and-family equity from Goldman/KKR/Baird networks is not a replicable starting condition for most buyers.** Their edge was access to patient equity on vanilla terms, which is precisely what a first-time Canadian buyer typically lacks — and why the VTB-heavy structures elsewhere in this knowledge base remain the more realistic path.
