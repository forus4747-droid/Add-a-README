## What to look for — the failed deal, and the five landmines
He is unusually candid: *"We did everything wrong that I think you can do wrong."* The specific failures, each of which is a diligence item:

1. **Customer concentration accepted knowingly.** "Anchored with one large customer... a significant portion of revenue — a higher risk than most people would accept."
2. **No lender in the deal, so no independent scrutiny.** "We didn't do bank loans, there was no SBA, so they didn't necessarily look at that." *A lender's underwriting is free diligence; skipping it removes a check, not just a cost.*
3. **The seller was defrauding customers.** He had "figured out a really sophisticated way to manipulate retreading of tires" and was stealing from customers. The brothers discovered it post-close, **disclosed it to every affected customer, and kept them all.**
4. **The seller immediately tried to poach the employees.**
5. **The lease had been falsified.** They believed three years remained; it was **month-to-month**. The co-tenant (a large distribution company) wanted them gone and the landlord wanted to sell. **They were evicted.** "It's really hard to have a repair shop without a shop." His admission: **"I didn't go back and actually verify the landlord before we signed the deal."**

**What saved a fraction of it:** the seller had **personally signed on some of the loans**, which mitigated part of the loss. They also fire-sold to a competitor and recovered a little.

**Why they didn't sue:** "There's one thing to get a judgment — that's not even 50%, that's a five-year road. **And then you still have to go collect.**" (The seller was later pursued by the FBI for other frauds; they cooperated.)

**The human cost, which is the part most videos omit:** it took **three and a half years** and a return to something familiar (real estate) before his wife would support another attempt. And the partnership damage was worse than the financial: "The relationship with my brother and his wife — that's been a lot more difficult... **he's not allowed to do anything with me yet.**"

His own summary: **"I paid for an education I didn't know I was getting."**

## The HVAC thesis
Four reasons they chose the industry:
1. **Essential.** "Air conditioning breaks down in Tucson, it's 103 degrees, they're here in an hour and I'll pay it, 100%." He contrasts this explicitly with WeWork and a multifamily leasing company — "they just weren't necessities."
2. **Highest recurring revenue among the trades** — "HVAC has this unique membership program, usually around maintenance with some discounts, building loyalty."
3. **Operationally simple.** "It's usually the same 10 to 15 things over and over."
4. **A transparency gap to exploit.** "NPS scores are pretty much the lowest of any industry out there, with the exception of car repairs and car sales." They've sold over half a million dollars online with **upfront pricing**, letting customers shop at their own pace "instead of being pressured in their home."

**The counterweight he names: brutal seasonality — 8–9× between peak and trough months.** That is a working-capital problem of the first order, and the reason a seasonal service business needs an operating line (see `extracts/Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`).

## Deal sourcing — credibility compounding
- **Deal 2 came from a broker who had not yet listed the business**, reaching out to a handful of people he'd heard good things about. The seller was also an Army veteran; they hit it off. **No price was ever put on it — the buyers proposed the number.**
- **Deal 3 came through their supplier.** "We just kept telling everybody: we're looking to grow through acquisition — if you know anyone interested, please make an introduction."
- **Deal 3 was their number one target**, identified because they kept losing head-to-head against them: "Anytime someone asked for a recommendation on Nextdoor or Facebook groups in Flower Mound, they'd have 20 or 30 recommendations before another name came up. We were like — **what is going on?**"
- **Both were off-market with no broker.** "I didn't have to deal with a broker, which was really nice — anything I needed I went straight to the sellers, and I got to guide them on what the process would be, the milestones, why we need to see things."

**The lesson, stated plainly: "You can't do a good deal with a bad person. This is a great person, and we got to do a great deal with a great person."**

## Structure — the most technically useful section
**Deal 3 financing:**
- **SBA loan**, plus **just over 10% seller financing on full standby for a couple of years** — "super helpful from our side."
- **They used balance-sheet strength rather than a large cash injection.** Because the acquisition was in the same NAICS code, the SBA treated it as an **expansion loan**, requiring far less equity.
- Going over the standard SBA limit meant a more onerous underwriting process — **a 76-page credit memorandum** against a typical 15–20.
- **Every one of their deals has had seller financing.**

**On personal guarantees:** three of four partners signed. His practical note: *"They say unconditional guarantees, and that's true but not true. **Lots of things are negotiable as long as you know what you're doing.** We haven't actually had to pledge as much as we thought."* Plus a jurisdictional advantage: **Texas homestead law protects the personal residence** from such claims.

**The tuck-in economics:** on deal 2, "it's less about their numbers, it's about our numbers. **We're just going to end up with technicians — we already have everything else, we can absorb it without any additional headcount.**" Result: strong debt-service coverage, and **no customers lost in the first 30 days.** *This is the argument for buying adjacent businesses in the same trade and geography rather than diversifying.*

## Staffing and comp — the sharpest operating content
- **"Show me the comp plan and I'll show you the outcomes."** Every behaviour follows an incentive.
- **Every technician acquired has received a raise.** They run a defined model — a set percentage of revenue to technicians, to commissions, to uniforms. "I want to pay for the best because we want the best."
- **Technicians do not care about NPS.** They pay a bonus when a technician is **named in a five-star Google review**, because "they can't go point to an 86 NPS to a friend — but they can say *look, we've got 1,500 five-star reviews on Google.*"
- **The lead-handoff incentive:** a technician who spots a replacement opportunity gets a kickback for introducing the comfort advisor, plus a slice when the sale closes.
- **Recognition is not one-size-fits-all.** His story from leading a union team at Lockheed Martin: one member grabbed him by the collar — *"If you ever recognise me in front of everyone again, I'm going to kick you in the shin. I'm happy to be recognised, but one-on-one."*
- **Identity precedes recruiting.** "It took us more than a year to figure out who we are... **once you know who you are, it makes it a lot easier to attract who you want.**" Their answer: a mission, stated values, a compensation philosophy, and a "tech-first" positioning.

## The grind
- **"I'd rather go zero to one than one to three and a half — that has been a freaking nightmare."** The reason: "You're investing in all the overhead and leadership so **margins actually go down**, you don't have pricing competitiveness from your suppliers yet, and you're still trying to compete with the bigger guys. And you've got to hire technicians who are getting paid more elsewhere — **why join us?**"
- They wanted to buy at the standard search-fund criterion (~$5M revenue) but **kept getting outbid**, so they started at $1M: **"We'd rather be in the game than talking about the game."**
- Four partners with day jobs; only one (Scott) ran day-to-day operations at the start — chosen partly for "a high tolerance for pain."
- Five years in, only two of four partners draw meaningful pay. He started a recruiting firm on the side to cover his own bills.

## Why owners sell — three distinct profiles in one video
1. **Deal 1's seller:** a fraudster exiting ahead of discovery.
2. **Deal 2's seller:** a small operator who "tried growing it bigger, didn't necessarily enjoy the responsibility that came with a whole bunch of technicians, so it kind of went back smaller." **The classic ceiling-and-retreat profile.**
3. **Deal 3's seller:** ~50 years old, **had removed himself from the business 95% with a GM running it**, had executed a five-year plan and finished it, and wanted to apply his accumulated goodwill to something new. **The professional seller — and the best business of the three.**

## What sellers hide
- **Verify the lease with the landlord directly, in writing, before closing.** This single omission destroyed a business. Confirm term, renewal rights, assignment on change of control, and the landlord's intentions for the property. Same category as the permits/change-of-control warning in `extracts/Due-diligence/Small-Business-Due-Diligence-Checklist-What-Banks-Won-t-Tell-You.md`.
- **Contact the major customers.** Concentration plus an undisclosed fraud is what killed deal 1; both were discoverable by talking to the customers.
- **A seller who will not permit a lender's diligence is removing a check on themselves.** Even where financing isn't needed, the underwriting is worth having.
- **Ask who the business's reputation actually belongs to.** Their best target was identifiable purely from unprompted community recommendations — a signal available for free, before any conversation.
- **Ask what percentage of the owner's time the business consumes.** Deal 3's seller was 95% out with a GM in place; that's why it was worth $5.8M of revenue and integrated cleanly.
- **Model the seasonal trough, not the annual average.** 8–9× peak-to-trough means a business that looks fine on annual figures can be insolvent in February.

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout (SBA, Texas homestead law, NAICS expansion-loan treatment).** Canadian translations:
  - **No SBA expansion-loan equivalent.** A Canadian buyer adding a same-industry business gets no automatic equity relief; expect the lender to look at the consolidated balance sheet and to want security. **BDC is the most likely source for the layer above CSBFP.**
  - **The full-standby seller note is exactly the Canadian VTB postponement structure** described in `extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md` — and it works the same way, converting vendor debt into bank-recognised quasi-equity.
  - **Texas homestead protection has partial Canadian analogues but they are weaker and provincial** (e.g. limited principal-residence exemptions under provincial execution/exemption acts, generally capped at modest amounts, and none in Ontario for the home itself). **Assume a Canadian personal guarantee puts the family home at risk and negotiate accordingly.** The one genuine Canadian advantage: **CSBFP caps personal guarantees at 25% of the loan** — a real protection with no US equivalent, and a reason to structure as much as possible inside that program.
  - **HVAC seasonality is inverted and just as extreme in Canada** (heating peak in winter, cooling in summer, shoulder-season troughs), and the same 8–9× logic applies to Canadian snow and landscaping. **The operating line is the deliverable.**
  - **Trade licensing is provincial and individual** — TSSA gas fitter registration, ODP refrigerant handling. See `extracts/Owned-and-Operated/He-built-a-100M-HVAC-business-here-s-how.md` on building licence redundancy.

## Verdict
**The most valuable failure story in the corpus, and the best illustration of how diligence discipline is actually learned.** Five landmines — customer concentration, a fraudulent seller, employee poaching, a falsified lease, and no lender scrutiny — cost him his savings, three and a half years, and a working partnership with his twin brother. **Every one was preventable, and every one is now a line item on a checklist.**

Four things to carry into a Canadian deal:

1. **Verify the physical and legal foundations independently of the seller.** The lease killed this deal. Call the landlord. Call the customers. Pull the licences. **The seller is the least reliable source for anything you can check elsewhere.**
2. **A lender in the deal is a feature.** Their all-cash purchase felt fast and cost them everything. Even where financing isn't strictly needed, an underwriter's scrutiny is free diligence.
3. **Buy adjacent, then tuck in.** The economics of deal 2 — absorb the technicians, add nothing else, lose no customers — is the strongest argument in the corpus for **buying the same trade in the same region.** It's also how they got to $10M in four years from a $1M start.
4. **"You can't do a good deal with a bad person."** Deal 1 was a fraudster; deal 3 was a high-character operator who had already professionalised his business. **The character of the seller predicted the quality of the business in both directions.** For a Canadian buyer sourcing off-market from tired owners, this is the single most useful filter — and it's assessable long before the financials are.

The honest counterweight, which he supplies himself: **five years in, at $10–11M of revenue, he has been paid $5K then $30K and is running a side business to cover his bills.** The equity may be substantial; the income is not, and won't be for another 12–18 months on his own estimate. That is the real timeline of a small-business roll-up, and it belongs next to every "buy a business, change your life" claim elsewhere in this corpus.
