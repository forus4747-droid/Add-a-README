# Stacking Small Acquisitions to $5m in Revenue (cleaning) — Kyle Boyden & Jake Furfaro

## Key numbers
- **7 acquisitions → just under $5M revenue / just under $1M EBITDA.** Of that, ~$4M came from acquisition and ~$1M from organic growth.
- **Total of their own cash across all of it: ~$100,000.** Everything else was seller-financed.
- Typical target: **$300K–$500K revenue** cleaning companies. It took **5 acquisitions to reach $2.5M**, and 7 to reach $5M — the last two were much larger.
- Deal terms as a rule: **seller carry, very small cash down, notes from 3 to 10 years, several at 0% interest.**
- Worked example: a company cash-flowing **$7–8K/month**, bought with **$30K down** and a **5-year 0%-interest seller note at $2,200/month** debt service.
- Buy target multiple: around **1x** on the small ones ("as long as I can buy this at a 1x multiple and on seller financing, this could be a deal").
- First venture (property management): **115 units, $15–18K/month revenue, expenses exceeding fees by $5–6K/month.** Seller asked $50K; they paid **$5–8K**. Stripped all staff and lease, went from **-$8K/month to +$6K/month within 30 days**, ran it a year, sold the book.
- House cleaning arm: bought at ~**$300K revenue with 7–8 cleaners**, now **17 cleaners**. ~40–50 employees across the group.

## What to look for
**Green flags they actively hunted:**
- Seller demographics as a screen — owner near retirement, kids didn't want it. They cold-called *hundreds*, using Fiverr contractors to pull lists of every cleaning company in the state.
- **Database depth over current profit**: "there's not a lot of employees and it's kind of small, but it's 38 years worth of past customers. There's 10,000 customers I can market to." The customer list is the asset.
- **Cross-sell architecture.** Commercial janitorial is the "north star"; flooring, window, pressure washing, gutters bolt onto it. Key insight lifted from property management: *be the party that controls the spend and chooses the subcontractor — then sub it to yourself.*
- Weak competition: "our average competitor was a phenomenal cleaner that got so busy she was forced to hire help… at no point was she ever a business owner." Adding a CRM, scheduling software and digital marketing "differentiated us from 90% of the market."

**Red flags / what they'd avoid:**
- **Residential house cleaning specifics:** low margins, complaint-heavy, and clients bond to one specific cleaner — "clients only want their cleaner… if the cleaner calls in sick, revenue's gone." You are handcuffed by both client and staff. Anyone with a mop and bucket can undercut you.
- **Property management:** 24/7 call load, thin margins, and the *owners* are a bigger headache than the tenants.
- Their own first deal was bought with essentially zero diligence — "it was negligent looking back on it now… that is tuition."
- Deal fatigue is real: "each one of these little acquisitions might have taken one to two years off our life expectancy."

**Their own retrospective advice, which contradicts their path:** "I wouldn't encourage anybody to do what we did, but that's a different question than would I do what we did. I think people should go bigger — shoot for above $500,000 EBITDA to leave a job."

## Canadian-specific
US deal (Washington State, SBA on the seventh deal only). **US — verify for Canada.** But the core structure is *more* available in Canada, not less: six of seven deals used no bank at all, only vendor take-backs. A Canadian buyer facing tighter acquisition lending can run this same playbook — small, VTB-financed, cross-sellable cleaning companies — without CSBFP involvement. The 0%-interest note signed off by the seller's own accountant is worth knowing is possible.
