## What to look for
**The real-estate financing insight — genuinely valuable, and rarely stated this clearly**

His SBA lender laid out two scenarios:
- **Business only, SBA 7(a): 10-year amortization** at a given rate.
- **Business plus real estate: 25-year amortization at a lower rate.**

Because the real estate dominated the price, stretching the amortisation from 10 to 25 years at a lower rate made **the monthly debt service roughly the same either way** — *but now the $90,000 of rent stayed inside the business.* **Same cash outflow, materially more free cash flow, plus a hard asset behind the loan.**

> "It was a deal that I wouldn't have done without the real estate."

*(Note the threshold discussed: the 25-year amortisation generally requires real estate to be more than 50% of the total package. His was 80%+, so it never came up. Verify the current rule before relying on it.)*

**The seller's counter-offer was better than his own offer — because their motivations weren't financial**

His LOI: **just under $2M, SBA-financed, ~7% interest.**

Their counter, two days later:
- **+~$100,000 on the price (to ~$2.05M)**, but
- **seller financing at 5%**, 25-year amortisation with a **5-year balloon**, same 10% down.

**His monthly outflow was lower despite the higher price.** He called his banker to sanity-check it; the banker — who lost the loan as a result — told him: **"I want to close this loan with you, but don't turn that down. That's a great deal."**

**Why the seller preferred it:** they wanted **an income stream for their children**, and they had **an aversion to taxes** and liked spreading the gain out. Drake is honest that this may not have been optimal for them on a present-value basis — "I couldn't tell you they're looking for the better deal. **I think they're just looking for what they wanted.**"

**The principle, which is the takeaway:** *"Negotiate for what was important to them and what wasn't that important to me."* He credits the book *Negotiate Without Fear* and applied its frame throughout the LOI: **what's contentious to both (usually price), what's important to them and not to you (give it), and what's important to you and not to them (take it).**

**A second, underrated benefit: no bank in diligence.** "Not having a bank in diligence made things less of a headache. There were a handful of things that, if there was a bank involved, would have made things a lot more challenging — versus being able to have face-to-face conversations: *okay, with this thing coming up, here's how I think we should address it.*"

*(Note this cuts directly against `extracts/Acquiring-Minds/How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses.md`, where the absence of a lender removed a check and contributed to a total loss. **Both are true: a lender is free diligence and an obstacle.** The difference is whether you have the discipline to do the diligence yourself.)*

**Buying below your own threshold — when it's defensible**
He had disqualified sub-$300K SDE deals, then bought one listed at $140K. What made it work:
1. **The add-back was real and verifiable** (rent to the owner), taking effective SDE to ~$230K.
2. **The real estate transformed the debt structure** and added a tangible asset.
3. **His wife retained a strong remote career**, so the business didn't need to produce a large near-term outcome: *"I had a backstop... we can really focus on doing this the right way, be sure not to take outsize risk, but we don't need to try to squeeze performance out of this thing in year one."*
4. **Bend deal flow was genuinely thin** — "you don't know what's under a rock until you turn it."

**The business quality signals he identified**
- **MRO (maintenance, repair, operations)** — Grainger is the public comparable.
- **The moat is inventory breadth on critical-uptime parts:** electrical components for heavy machinery across a wide range of ages, specs and models. "When an excavator goes down and they need a new starter or alternator... our key competency is we have a wide array that nobody else has."
- **The pricing power argument, which generalises well:** *"If you are a small piece of your total customer spend, it's a nice place to be, because you're critical but you won't necessarily be scrutinised. What you'll spend on a critical electric component at the register versus what it costs you to be down an extra day or two — the math isn't even close."*
- **Low capex, and that's the point:** "My checkbook isn't as scared as if I had to buy a bunch of new machines... **with an inventory management model, if we spend money on it we can probably sell it.** Is it at the profit we want? Not necessarily. But that's the one thing that's helped me sleep — inventory is a risk that is fairly manageable."

## Key-person and retirement risk — the best treatment in the corpus
Three employees, all 10+ years' tenure, all older. **"When I came into the business the average age of our employees dropped by about 15 years."** His framing is unusually clear-eyed: **the risk isn't that they'll quit, it's that they'll retire.**

**On the departing owner:** "He's mechanically a genius — I've been told by several of our customers nobody has a deeper knowledge than him. **We can't replace that, because I don't think anybody can.** So we have to use our customer reputation and our service model to win going forward... **that's a key-person risk that pragmatically, that's maybe not recoverable.**" (Owner staying through roughly year-end.)

**How he asked the awkward question — worth copying.** One employee raised it first; he went to the other directly.
- **The sales rep** said, in only their second conversation: *"I love what I do, but I'm this old — not sure how much longer I have, just want to let you know."* Drake's response: *"Understand — you've given your life to this business, you need something that fits your lifestyle. Would you be willing to help train somebody in the interim?"* **Answer, without a pause: absolutely.** (Amusingly, months later the same man says "I get high on selling" and won't retire.)
- **The warehouse manager**, asked straight: *"This decision is entirely yours, but how long have you been here and how long do you want to be here? I think you've got great talent, we want to keep you — but if you have any thoughts on where you want to go, now's your time."*

**His mitigation strategy: deliberate over-resourcing.** "Getting our team a bit over-resourced so we can have a little slack to fill those holes when they inevitably come up." And a self-aware warning: covering gaps personally "is how I will become working *in* the business versus *on* the business."

**Will Smith's framing of the upside is worth keeping:** if you can extract the knowledge from the owner's head into the organisation, "you've added a tremendous amount of value... **the flaws or weaknesses are also the opportunities.**"

## Pre-close employee access — an unusual and replicable advantage
The sale was **known in town for almost a year**, so there was no secrecy to protect. The broker and seller **encouraged him to talk to the employees.** He spent **10–12 hours across five or six days** with them before closing.

> "I was able to use it as almost a diligence tool. It added a level of diligence, and I was able to start that chemistry and get a head start on day-one activities."

**And then he acted on what he heard, immediately.** He asked what would help them do their jobs better, got two simple answers, and **delivered both in weeks one and two: a company credit card for taking customers to lunch, and an inventory/POS system.**

*(Contrast with `extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`, where Divitkos argues you can learn almost nothing about culture pre-close because access is restricted. **Drake got the access — and it was the single biggest diligence advantage in his deal.** Where a seller permits it, take it.)*

## Why owners sell
- The seller was **in his 80s**, with an explicit interest in **an income stream for his children** and **spreading out the tax hit** — not maximising proceeds. He also, notably, **fell and went to hospital** the day Drake first visited, so they never met on that trip.
- The broader profile: a 50-year-old business, three long-tenured staff, an owner whose knowledge was entirely in his head, and a building he owned personally and rented to his own company.

## What sellers hide
- **Find the add-backs the listing didn't make.** The $90K of owner-paid rent wasn't presented as SDE — "I almost had to make my own SDE." **Always ask who owns the premises and what the business pays them.** In an owner-occupied situation, that rent is either an add-back or a purchase opportunity, and often both.
- **Ask the age of every employee and their retirement intentions, directly and respectfully.** Drake's two scripts above are the model. In a three-person business, one retirement is a third of the workforce.
- **Ask what the owner personally knows that nobody else does**, and accept that some of it may be unrecoverable. Then price it — and start documenting from day one.
- **Ask for pre-close access to employees.** Where the sale is already known, or the seller is comfortable, this is the highest-value diligence available and it costs nothing.
- **Understand the seller's actual objectives before proposing terms.** Drake's better deal came from the seller's tax aversion and desire to provide for children — neither of which is in a CIM. Ask what they plan to do with the money.
- **Check vendor concentration too, not just customers.** "We almost have as many vendors as customers — I don't necessarily think that's good."

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout (SBA 7(a) amortisation rules, KeyBank).** Canadian translations:
  - **There is no CSBFP equivalent of the 25-year real-estate amortisation advantage** in the same form — but the underlying logic transfers exactly. **CSBFP does finance real property, at up to 90% and with amortisation up to 25 years, versus 10 years for equipment/leaseholds.** So a Canadian deal that includes the premises may genuinely support a longer amortisation and a bigger loan than one that doesn't. **Verify current CSBFP terms with the lender** — this is one of the few places Canadian financing is structurally *favourable*, and it deserves modelling on every deal where the seller owns the building.
  - **Owner-occupied premises are extremely common in Canadian small business**, and the rent-to-self add-back is correspondingly common. **Always ask.**
  - **The seller's tax motivation would be different in Canada but no less powerful.** A Canadian vendor taking back a VTB may use the **capital gains reserve to spread the gain over up to five years**, and may want a **share sale to access the $1.25M LCGE** (see `extracts/Canadian-tax/ACCOUNTANT-EXPLAINS-Canada-s-Capital-Gains-Tax.md`). Understanding which of these matters to a specific vendor is exactly the lever Drake used.
  - **Retirement/key-person risk is if anything more acute in Canada**, given the boomer-ownership demographics described in `extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`.
  - **A thin secondary-market search is the Canadian norm.** Drake's willingness to look at a below-threshold deal in a small city, and to find the structure that made it work, is directly applicable to a Canadian search outside Toronto/Vancouver/Montreal.

## Verdict
**One of the best Track A episodes in the corpus for a first-time buyer**, because Drake is a careful, unglamorous operator whose edge came from structure and empathy rather than aggression.

Four things to carry forward:

1. **When the seller owns the building, model the deal both ways.** The rent add-back plus a longer amortisation turned a business he had disqualified into one he bought. In Canada, where CSBFP treats real property far more generously than other assets, **this may be the single most underused structuring lever available to a small-deal buyer.**
2. **Find out what the seller actually wants before you argue about price.** An 80-year-old vendor optimising for his children's income and his own tax bill handed a buyer a *better* deal than the buyer had asked for. **The motivations behind the motivations are where the value is** — and they are never in the listing.
3. **Get in front of the employees before closing if you possibly can, and then deliver something small for them in week one.** A company credit card and a POS system bought more goodwill than any day-one speech.
4. **Ask about retirement plainly.** In small businesses with long-tenured staff, retirement risk is larger than turnover risk and far easier to plan for — if you ask.

The honest caveat, which Drake supplies: he bought a business generating **~$230K of effective SDE**, against a **$300K target**, and he could only do it because **his wife's income removed the pressure.** That is a real precondition, and it is worth stating explicitly rather than glossing over — a thin-margin first acquisition is much safer with a second household income behind it.
