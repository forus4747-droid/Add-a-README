**The construction upside — the number that reframes commercial landscaping:**
- An **$800/month maintenance client** commissioned an **$80,000** landscape replacement.
- One LA building: **$150,000** of work, because a new tenant (a SpaceX spin-off building rockets) wanted whatever they wanted and the landlord said yes.
- A Fortune 50 client with an increased budget wanting all the grass removed: **"that's like $700 grand."**
- **His framing:** "The more buckets you have, the more different clients you have, the more chances you can get that extra few hundred grand... It's like you're playing the slots — **but I'm already sitting at the table.**"
- Crucially: **"they don't bid it out. You're my landscaper, so you're doing the project."** The maintenance route is the option on the renovation.

## What to look for
**The $1M owner-operator ceiling — and what it means for buyers**
Loftis confirms the pattern: founders in fragmented service industries stall around **$1M**. His own bottleneck was administrative — "my strengths are not organizational, I don't like computer busy work... **the quickest I could get an office manager, the better.** It wasn't cheap, but I was a lot more organized and I could grow because I had that extra time."

But he pushes back hard on dismissing those sellers:
> "**Those owners do have a lot more figured out than people want to give them credit for** — the way the routes are set up, the people that work there, the trucks, the equipment... **there is definitely value there,** and I don't think that should be overshadowed by an app or something."

**The improvement list — every one of his three acquisitions was fixable with basics:**
- **None of the three had a website.**
- The first seller ran the business on a **1993 computer**, billing in 15-minute increments, **mailing ~60 paper invoices a month** and waiting for cheques. Loftis moved everything to **online QuickBooks and emailed invoices** immediately.
- Sellers returning calls "every few days"; one ran the whole business on a **flip phone**.
- Operational basics he had to add: **gas cards**, **truck tracking**, **letting clients text the work phone**.
- **His advice to younger buyers is genuinely good:** "You're a wizard on your phone. Put that same energy into contacting your customers... *I don't know the answer yet but I'll be over there tomorrow*, or *I'll change your invoice right now on my phone and send it back*. Normally it would take a week to get something mailed."

**Customer reaction to the change of ownership: almost none.**
> "I thought these people were like *oh my god, a new landscaper, I'm going to go get quotes.* Honestly, no one cared. I sent a letter out — *here's my new contact information* — and **maybe a third of them even wanted to meet.** You care a lot more about it because you just bought it. To them it's *yeah, we send you $562 a month, whatever.*"

**Buy recurring, not project work.** He passed on a target because "the guy only had construction work and I wanted recurring revenue... **with the maintenance you can maintain and have a cash flow that's pretty steady.**"

**Commercial over residential:** "Commercial buildings are the least amount of headache. They're running a no-BS business in their $5 million building, they have no time to worry about you. If you're doing what you're supposed to, they're not going to call, they're not going to complain — you get in, do your work, send them a bill, **and they pay it like it's electricity or the water bill.**"

**Deal sourcing — the BizBuySell reverse-listing hack**
He **posted himself as a buyer on BizBuySell** ("looking for a commercial landscape company to buy — if you're a broker, please call me"), filling in the seller fields as best he could and putting his criteria in the description. Logic: brokers and anyone Googling "selling landscape business" land there. **A higher-end broker found it**, had a landscaping company attached to a larger property-management sale, and did the deal — Loftis ground $50K off the price. "I don't know that it would work again, but it worked once."

He also confirms the inbound effect: after the first deal, "word gets out in your little geography and all of a sudden you get all this inbound." And he never stops looking: "after the third month... I haven't stopped looking at deals or meeting guys that have businesses and saying **hey, when you do want to sell, I'd love to talk to you.**"

There are **no brokers specialising in landscaping** — "these guys are selling all kinds of stuff, ice cream shops, whatever."

**Rejection is normal.** One seller told him **to his face he was too young** (he was 26). Another chose a tire-shop owner instead — then that buyer dropped out and the broker called Loftis on a beach in Hawaii. He wired money 30 days later.

**Structure: all three were asset sales, deliberately**
> "I don't want to inherit workers' comp — that's a big deal. So basically **everyone was fired on a Friday, in effect, and hired on a Monday**, transfer all the truck titles, give them a new PO box."

**Key-person retention — the "golden handcuffs" move**
On the third deal (an hour away), the account manager was wavering. "In my mind: *you're an hour away from me, I need you.* So **I overpaid him — gave him like a $6 [an hour] increase.** You're not going anywhere. Down the road if it doesn't work, I'll figure it out then, **but I can't afford for this manager to leave in the first year.**" He still works there.

**Seller transition — his strongest advice to a green buyer**
> "Ask for a longer training period. They always put on BizBuySell two weeks or two months — **say give me two months included in the sale, and then I'll pay you as a consultant for six months to a year.** That is invaluable. **You have to do it if you're green.**"
> "And in the beginning, **do it his way.** You might think *why does he do it this way?* — just accept it, because he's hitting the cash flow you're buying. **Listen, shut up, and work hard**, and you'll be surprised what you pick up in six months to a year with that guy next to you."
> Corollary: **make sure the seller isn't "moving to Idaho tomorrow."**

## The grind
- Three years to $1M: "I hauled ass. There were two years in there I didn't take a Saturday off." Friends invited him to Lake Havasu; "I'm going to make $700 tomorrow, you guys don't get it — I need to save for this next truck."
- Bought trucks on **Craigslist**. Started by knocking on doors, "doing any job I could get my hands on."
- On wiring the money the first time: **"I don't have a house to look at afterward. It's less tangible — you're buying a cash flow. Once you truly understand that, it's less scary."**

## Cash flow traps — and the single best operator insight in this extract
> "**One thing that isn't talked about a lot for landscaping is how close to the knife's edge most your employees are.** Saying *hey, we'll get you your checks tomorrow* even though they thought they were getting them today — **that's like a death sentence.** These guys are on the knife edge, they have no savings, nothing. When you look a guy in the eye and say *you're getting your cheque Friday* — **I don't care if you have to pawn your car, you better get the money there.** Because that speaks volumes, and that consistency builds trust."

**Payroll timing is not a cash-management variable in this industry. It is the entire employment relationship.** Any working capital model for a landscaping acquisition must treat payroll as inviolable and size the reserve accordingly.

## Why owners quit/sell
- Not stated directly, but the profile is consistent across all three targets: **owner-operators around $500–800K who stalled**, never built a website, never modernised billing, and were personally handling every callback on a flip phone. They hit the ceiling Loftis describes and could not get past it.

## What sellers hide
- **Ask for the maintenance/construction revenue split, by year.** Construction and enhancement work is lumpy and can be enormous ($80K, $150K, $700K on his route). It flatters a trailing-twelve-month figure and does not recur. **Normalise it: what is the base maintenance revenue, and what is the multi-year average of enhancement work?**
- **Ask for the route list with monthly billing per building.** 230 buildings at an average is a very different asset from 40 buildings with three big ones. Route density also determines drive time and therefore true labour cost.
- **Ask what the seller does personally that isn't in the numbers** — his sellers *were* the invoicing, the callbacks, the bidding. Budget an office manager, because that is the specific hire that unblocks the $1M ceiling.
- **Value the assets independently and treat them as the floor.** His third-deal logic — "$120 grand of assets at street value, so I can't lose much" — is a discipline worth copying: **price the trucks and mowers at auction value, and see how much of the purchase price is genuinely at risk.**
- **Identify the one person who cannot leave and price their retention into the deal.** A $6/hour raise is cheap insurance against losing an account manager who holds an hour-away operation together.
- **Ask what the seller's transition commitment actually is**, in writing, with a term and a fee. Two weeks is not a transition.
- **Check workers' comp / WSIB history.** He structured every deal as an asset purchase specifically to avoid inheriting it.

## Canadian-specific
- **US (California) — but this is among the most transferable episodes in the corpus** because commercial landscape maintenance works the same way in Canada, with one enormous difference: **seasonality.** A Southern California route bills twelve months a year. **A Canadian commercial landscaping route bills roughly seven or eight months of maintenance and must pair with snow and ice to survive the winter** — which is exactly why the snow-pricing material in `extracts/Landscaping-snow-operators/` matters. When valuing a Canadian landscaping business, **the winter contract book is not an add-on, it is half the business**, and an unpriced or uncapped seasonal snow contract is a liability (see `How-to-run-a-Snowplow-Business...`).
- **The asset-sale-to-avoid-liabilities structure does NOT work the same way in Canada.** Loftis fired everyone Friday and rehired Monday to avoid workers' comp exposure. **In most Canadian jurisdictions, employment is deemed continuous on the sale of a business — including an asset sale — so accrued service, notice and severance travel with the employees**, and **WSIB/WCB liabilities can attach to a purchaser.** See the detailed note in `extracts/David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`. **Get a WSIB clearance certificate and a Canadian employment lawyer before copying this structure.**
- The **"knife's edge" payroll point is if anything sharper in Canada**, where landscaping crews often include temporary foreign workers (SAWP/TFW streams) whose status, housing and pay obligations are regulated. **Verify LMIA compliance and worker status in diligence** — it is a real, findable liability that a US-focused checklist would miss entirely.
- **No landscaping-specialist brokers** in Canada either; expect generalist business brokers and direct outreach.

## Verdict
**The single most useful episode in the corpus for someone buying a Canadian landscaping or grounds-maintenance business**, and one of the few sources that compares building and buying from direct experience of both. His verdict on that comparison is unambiguous: **"Organic is very difficult. It's not sexy, it's arduous. I don't know that I would do it again — I would buy first and then build. I'd buy one big one and bolt on from there."**

Five things to carry forward:

1. **The seller profile is precise and repeatable**: a $500–800K owner-operator, stalled at the ceiling, no website, paper invoices, flip phone. The improvements are cheap and mechanical — QuickBooks Online, emailed invoices, same-day callbacks, gas cards, a text-capable work number. **That's a real margin and retention story, not a hypothetical one.**
2. **Buy the recurring maintenance route; the enhancement work is the free option on top.** An $800/month client producing an $80,000 project — uncontested, because you're already the landscaper — is where the returns actually come from. But never underwrite the deal on it.
3. **Commercial beats residential** for headache and payment reliability, in Canada as in California.
4. **Negotiate a long seller transition, and then defer to them.** Two months included plus six-to-twelve months as a paid consultant, and do it their way at first. For a buyer with no trade background this is the difference between an 80% account-retention outcome and a disaster.
5. **Pay everyone exactly when you said you would, without exception.** It costs nothing and it is the whole basis of trust with a crew that has no savings.

The honest counterweight, which he supplies: **~$200K of owner earnings on $1M of revenue is what an owner-operator makes, and it took him three punishing years and no Saturdays to get there.** The acquisitions were faster than the build — but the build is what taught him to run the acquisitions.
