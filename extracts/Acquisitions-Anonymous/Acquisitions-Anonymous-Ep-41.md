- **Relevance note:** two deals, **both off Jay's thesis** — a LEGO minifigure e-commerce reseller and a livestock trade publication. **No service-business content.** What survives is a set of analytical moves that apply to any small acquisition, plus two clean examples of key-person risk in a form that is easy to miss.

## Deal 1 — MinifigureLand.com (LEGO minifigure reseller)
| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| **Asking price** | **$1.6M** |
| **"Cash flow"** | **$925,000** |
| **Stated EBITDA** | **$524,000** |
| Revenue | **$5.3M** |
| Inventory | **$300,000** (~8 months of stock at cost, on Bridgman's estimate) |
| Established | 2013, Santa Ana CA |
| Premises | **400 sq ft on a month-to-month lease** — "I would be willing to bet you dollars to donuts this is a storage unit" |
| SKUs | **11,000+ different minifigures**; ~$8–10 average price |
| Order volume | **~500,000 individual orders/year** |
| Seller terms | Stays **60 days**, carries **up to 10%** in a seller note |

**The diligence flags, in order of usefulness:**

1. **The cash-flow-versus-EBITDA gap.** $925K "cash flow" against $524K stated EBITDA. Bill: **"I'd definitely like to understand the delta between those."** *This is the single most transferable line in the episode.* A $400K discrepancy between two profit measures in the same listing is either add-backs the broker hasn't justified or two different definitions being used interchangeably. **Always reconcile them, line by line.**
2. **Order-count economics.** "All else being equal in e-commerce you want **fewer orders to do the same amount of revenue**. These guys are doing half a million orders a year... if 1% of your transactions lead to a customer service issue, you're doing a lot of customer service. If 1% lead to a chargeback, you're dealing with a lot of chargebacks."
3. **The supply-chain question determines the entire value.** Two possibilities: (a) **an open wholesale account with LEGO**, in which case "this is a freaking slam dunk — all you've got to do is be the bridge between the LEGO catalog and good e-commerce best practices"; or (b) **buying rare figures at collector shows and arbitraging scarcity**, in which case "that's a hobby business" and the value sits in one person's head. **Nobody can tell from the listing, and it is the first question to ask.**
4. **The niche-expertise trap** — the transferable version of key-person risk. Mills: *"You have to know which ones are super rare and really valuable... I bought this LEGO Yoda for five dollars and I'm about to sell it for fifty. **I would get hosed.**"* Girdley extends it to a friend's card-shop business and a second-hand watch dealer: *"You want to be the kind of person who sits up and reads the watch forums on the weekends — **or you need the right staff who will be that kind of nerd.** You only do really well in this market if you have somebody like that on staff."*
5. **Platform dependency on a single supplier's goodwill.** Girdley's Beanie Baby warning: *"If LEGO decides to Beanie Baby you — they overproduce, they become less scarce, they kill the golden goose. **It all comes down to: can you trust LEGO not to run you out of business by getting greedy.**"*

**The "where do they suck?" thesis** — Bill's habitual first question when evaluating a business: *"I like to go, where do they suck, what are they not doing right at all."* Here: 15,000 eBay reviews but only ~17 Amazon listings, and zero direct sales on their own site. **"You could probably double this business overnight just by getting all the SKUs they have on eBay onto Amazon."** Plus an obvious content play (LEGO build instructions ranking on keyword-driven search) and a subscription box nobody has built.

## Deal 2 — Livestock trade publication
| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| **Cash flow / EBITDA** | **$450,000** — the two match, which the hosts read as a good sign about capital intensity |
| Revenue | **~$2M** |
| Asking price | **None stated** |
| Staff | **10+, explicitly non-union** |
| Circulation | **~10,000 print**; 1,000+ online subscribers; 20,000 monthly online viewers |
| **Revenue mix** | **83% advertising / 13% subscriptions / 3% online ads / 1% other** |
| **Customer tenure** | **Top five advertisers have averaged 30 years each; the average reader has subscribed 13+ years** |

**Bridgman's analysis is the model here.** He does the arithmetic nobody else does: **$2M × 13% ÷ 10,000 subscribers = ~$26 per subscriber per year.** His conclusion: *"They either haven't found a good way to monetise, or they're under-monetising."* For the self-declared leading source of news in an industry, that's an anomaly worth explaining — and potentially the whole thesis.

**The growth idea:** become "the Bloomberg for the livestock industry" — pair editorial with a paid data product. He cites **FreightWaves** as the proven model: a media arm and a data business with **"negative customer acquisition cost,"** because the editorial constantly references and sells the data product. Girdley adds a parallel: a former Wall Street analyst in Iowa running an agriculture newsletter said to be **~$20M revenue and ~$18M of cash flow.**

**Bridgman's caution is the right one:** FreightWaves works because freight and logistics contain enormous companies that can pay enormous sums for data. **"I just don't know what that is for the livestock industry... maybe the average livestock business is substantially lower, such that there's not that same pricing power."**

**The key-person risk here is the writers.** Girdley: *"If I buy it and I have captive content creation and copywriting, and six months after close my staff decides — you know what, we really liked the other guy, we don't want to work for you, or it's time for us to retire... **I can't write for this magazine, I'm not a subject matter expert.**"*

**The strategic-buyer argument, which is genuinely good:** *"If you have any business at all related to livestock in the mountain west, you should buy this right away. It's probably not going to decline, it's very sticky. **If you could pay four times EBITDA and it doesn't grow at all, that's a 25% return even with no debt** — and then you've got a free option on using it to promote what you're already selling. **Having a media arm for any conglomerate is the slam-dunk crown jewel.**"*

## Canadian-specific
- **None. Neither deal has any Canadian dimension, and neither industry is in scope.** Four things transfer:
  1. **Reconcile "cash flow" against EBITDA against SDE on every Canadian listing.** Broker CIMs use these terms loosely and a large unexplained gap is the first thing to chase — this is the same lesson as `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/A-1M-Pest-Control-Business-Ecommerce-Buzz-or-Bust-AA-297.md` on reading a listing against itself.
  2. **Niche expertise held by one person is key-person risk, even when it doesn't look like it.** In a Canadian service business the equivalent isn't rare LEGO figures — it's **the estimator who knows how to price a snow contract, the tech who knows every building on the route, the applicator who holds the licence.** Ask what knowledge exists only in someone's head, and whether they're staying.
  3. **Month-to-month leases are a live threat.** See `extracts/Acquiring-Minds/How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses.md`, where a falsified lease destroyed a business. Verify term and assignment with the landlord directly.
  4. **Long customer tenure is a real and checkable asset.** Advertisers averaging 30 years is the print-media version of a janitorial book where every contract is a decade old. **Get the tenure distribution, not the average.**
- **One Canadian note worth flagging on the "non-union" disclosure:** the listing volunteers it because a US buyer cares. In Canada it matters *more* — **provincial successor-employer rules bind a purchaser to an existing collective agreement, often even in an asset sale.** See the labour note in `extracts/David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`.

## Verdict
**The weakest episode in the corpus for Jay's purposes** — two businesses he would never buy, in industries with no overlap with janitorial, landscaping, snow or pest control. It is included for completeness and should be near the bottom of any reading order.

Three habits are worth extracting anyway:

1. **Reconcile the profit figures before anything else.** A $400K gap between "cash flow" and EBITDA on a $1.6M asking price is the whole negotiation.
2. **Ask "where do they suck?"** — the systematic search for the obvious thing the current owner isn't doing. In Jay's industries the answers are consistent and cheap to fix: no website, paper invoicing, no card on file, no price increases in five years, no specialty-service upsell, no reactivation of dormant customers. That list, drawn from across this corpus, is a value-creation plan.
3. **Locate the expertise.** Whether it's LEGO scarcity pricing, watch provenance, trade-publication editorial, or knowing how to bid a commercial snow contract — **find out whether the business's judgement lives in a system or in a person, and whether that person is staying.**

The **negative-CAC media insight** is a genuine curiosity worth remembering: a content or data asset that markets an operating business you already own can be worth more to a strategic buyer than to a financial one. For a Canadian service-business owner, the small-scale version is unglamorous but real — **the reputation and review profile you build is the marketing asset**, which is exactly the mechanism behind Edwin Eaton winning seven figures of data-centre work from LinkedIn posts (`extracts/Cleaning-operators/Scaling-Your-Cleaning-Business-Boosting-Profits-Efficiency-in-Janitorial-Services-Ep.-149.md`).
