## What to look for — the three transferable lessons
**1. "SBA pre-qualified" means nothing. Heather Endresen, an actual SBA lender, is unambiguous:**

> "**SBA pre-qualified — I'll just say it, it doesn't mean anything, it's just marketing.** It didn't go to SBA for sure, and probably didn't even go to a credit officer at a bank. It's probably just a salesperson who offered to look at it with the broker, and had no incentive to tell the broker *this one might be kind of tough*."

Her explanation of bank structure is the valuable part: **"There's a big separation of church and state inside banks between the sales side and the credit side. The credit side makes all the decisions."** A term sheet is signed by a loan officer — a salesperson — and is **always** contingent on credit committee. **"Every letter from a bank is non-binding."**

And the deeper point: a pre-qualification issued **before there is a buyer** is meaningless by construction, because *"we don't know who's buying, we really don't know the price, we don't know the structure — how much equity versus how much seller note. It totally depends on all of those things."*

**This applies directly in Canada.** A broker's claim that a deal is "bankable" or "CSBFP eligible" is a marketing statement, not a credit decision. **Get your own lender's credit-committee view, on your structure, with your equity.** It's the same warning Bruce Marks gives from the other direction — "congratulations, you've structured a deal that doesn't work" (`extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md`).

**2. "Plus inventory" pricing is a red flag, not a footnote.**
> "**Plus inventory — we never like that.** You've got to sell, whether it's inventory or receivables and cash, **there should be some working capital left in the business and it should be included in the price.** A price *plus* just never makes any sense."

The Canadian equivalent for a service business: **a purchase price quoted without a working-capital peg**, leaving receivables and payables to be argued about at closing. **Set the peg in the LOI**, based on the aged receivable listing — particularly acute in janitorial (net-30 corporate clients) and snow (end-of-season collections).

**3. A declining industry does not go to zero — it flatlines, and the question is where you are on the curve.**

Girdley's counter-intuitive argument, after both hosts wrote the business off as a cash-is-dying play:
> "You see it with mainframes — companies like USAA and a lot of these big banks still run on stuff they bought from IBM 70 years ago running COBOL. **People don't understand how long the tail is.** My favourite is fax machines: usage is down 90%, but there are still whole industries where all you can communicate with is a fax — doctors' offices, healthcare, government. **The market goes down 90% and then it flatlines at 10%, and there are interesting niches you can find there** — because everybody does what you and I just did, which was write it off entirely."

**Heather's rebuttal is the practical one, and it's the lender's view:** *"That's the challenge — **do you know where you are on that curve?** Are you already flat, or is it still going down? Lenders don't like those; you want to see that it's flattened out for a while before you'd feel the cash flows are stable. **And I'm not sure that's a 3.4× multiple type industry.**"*

## Red flags they identify
- **Monopsony risk** — Girdley's term of the episode: *"The opposite of a monopoly. When you have only one buyer, you have all the pricing power to dictate whatever you want. The natural thing all these big buyers — Target, Walmart, now Amazon — do is leverage their position as the only buyer of choice and basically dictate terms, take away all the profits and capture it."* Applied here: *"I'm selling something totally generic, it's unbranded. **Amazon's going to capture all the profit they can and put me on subsistence wages over time.**"*
- **No moat and a replaceable supplier.** "They have a partnership with a Chinese factory somewhere, it gets branded with their thing, they put it on Amazon and eat the middle." His worry: **"They're going to figure out how to list on Amazon at some point and cut you out — and they may have already done it."** (Same test as `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/A-1M-Pest-Control-Business-Ecommerce-Buzz-or-Bust-AA-297.md`: find the supplier, and if you can find them easily, so can everyone.)
- **Extreme SKU concentration** — five SKUs at 95% of revenue.
- **Very short history with recent growth.** Launched 2020, and *"it hasn't been $625K for very long... so what's driving that? That could just be a one-time big account."* **Always ask what a growth rate is actually made of.**
- **The customer base itself.** Both hosts note, half-jokingly, that the businesses still using large amounts of cash skew toward those "trying to hide from taxes or doing something else they shouldn't."

## The best line for a first-time buyer
Girdley's framing of what kind of moat a non-expert buyer should look for:

> "At least it's one where there's moats that aren't *am I a smart buyer*. **If you take a smart kid out of Wharton and put him in a construction business, the moats there are pretty straightforward — you're not counting on him to compete against a whole world of people who understand FBA.** You're just going to make sure that person really understands how the northwest corner of Atlanta does contracting."

**This is the single most relevant sentence in the episode for Jay.** A local service business's moat is **geography, route density, contracts, licences and reputation** — none of which require you to out-think a global market of specialists. An e-commerce or collectibles business requires you to be the smartest operator in a worldwide arena. **Local beats clever.**

Heather confirms the lender's view: *"Most banks see it that way too — they don't like small FBA deals unless the buyer has that kind of experience, and even then they may not like it."*

## On social proof — a worthwhile aside
The episode opens with several minutes on fake Twitter accounts and plagiarised content. The transferable point:

> "It's easy to confuse the types of social proof we've learned to trust in the real world with social media social proof. If somebody's been a member of your country club for a decade and hasn't been kicked out, that's a pretty good sign. **Then you go on Twitter and someone has 195,000 followers — well, he must be a great guy. Absolutely not.**"

Girdley's own standard, which he calls the **"H-E-B factor"** after a friend who wouldn't wrong anyone in a deal because he didn't want to have to avoid them in the aisle of the local grocery store. **In a small Canadian market, that is exactly the reputational dynamic Jay will operate under** — and it's the same asset Nathan Lenahan identified when he found his best acquisition target through unprompted community recommendations (`extracts/Acquiring-Minds/How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses.md`).

Also worth noting: Girdley remarks that when he syndicated a deal, **almost nobody asked him for references.** *"Feel free to call references and ask around — I encourage you to do that."* **Ask sellers, partners and lenders for references. Most buyers don't.**

## Canadian-specific
- **None in the episode.** Four applications:
  1. **Treat any "bankable / pre-qualified / CSBFP eligible" claim in a Canadian listing as marketing.** Get a credit-side view on your own structure.
  2. **Set the working-capital peg in the LOI.** "Plus inventory" or an unspecified receivables treatment is a fight you will lose at closing.
  3. **Ask where a declining or seasonal market sits on its curve.** Relevant to Canadian service niches facing structural change — for example **residential lawn-care segments affected by Ontario and Quebec cosmetic-pesticide restrictions**, or **short-term-rental cleaning facing municipal STR crackdowns** (see `extracts/UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future.md`). The market may not vanish, but you must know whether it has already flattened.
  4. **Choose a business whose moat isn't your own cleverness.** For a Canadian buyer without industry experience, that argues strongly for **contracted, route-dense local services** over anything competing in a national or global market.

## Verdict
**Off-thesis deal, three genuinely useful lessons, and one of them comes straight from a lender's mouth.**

The **"SBA pre-qualified means nothing"** explanation is the most valuable thing here, because it generalises perfectly: **a broker's financing claim is a sales assertion made before the buyer, price and structure exist.** In Canada, where acquisition financing is scarcer and CSBFP's asset-only rules bite hard, taking a broker's word on bankability is how a buyer spends three months on a deal that was never financeable. **Get your own lender in early, with your actual numbers.**

The **"plus inventory"** point converts directly into the working-capital peg discipline, which matters on every service-business deal with receivables.

And the **"moats that aren't *am I a smart buyer*"** framing is the best single-sentence articulation in this corpus of *why* Jay's thesis — local, contracted, route-based Canadian service businesses — is the right one for a first-time buyer without industry experience. You don't have to be the cleverest operator in the world. You have to understand one town.
