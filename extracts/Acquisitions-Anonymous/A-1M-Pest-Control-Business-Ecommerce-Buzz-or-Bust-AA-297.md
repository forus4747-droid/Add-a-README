## Key numbers
- **Asking price $2.0M. Cash flow $1.26M. Revenue $5.6M. Established 2021** (three years old at time of listing).
- **Multiple: ~1.6× cash flow** — and the hosts treat that as the loudest red flag in the listing, not a bargain.
- **$140 average order value.** 100,000 email subscribers. Listing claims 200,000 monthly visitors in the headline and ~500,000 in the body.
- **70–100 orders shipped daily** → ~3,000 orders/month. Against 500,000 visitors that is a **~0.6% conversion rate** (the hosts do this math live).
- **80% of all sales come from one product — a mosquito zapper.**
- **80% of sales attributed to "Australia and the US" combined** — deliberately lumped.
- Inventory: **7–30 days of stock**, 3PL facilities in Asia, Australia and the US, factory orders every **2–3 weeks**, "favorable payment terms."
- Team: **7 full-time employees** (video editing, customer service, marketing) plus **3 freelancers** (video editing, graphic design, web design).
- Historical context: in 2021 Amazon aggregators were paying **4× EBITDA** for businesses like this — "and that's why they're all bankrupt today."

## What to look for — this is a masterclass in reading a listing
**Red flags**

1. **A cheap multiple is a warning, not an opportunity.** "There is a reason this thing is priced at under two times cash flow... you'll be lucky if you get your capital back here. **This will start feeling like 10 times cash flow inside of a year.**" When the market prices an asset at 1.6× and you think you see value, assume the market has priced the decay correctly and work out what it knows.

2. **Geography lumping.** The listing says Australia + US = 80% of sales, then says sales peak October–March. Bill's read: "otherwise it would be counterbalanced by American summer, which it clearly is not — so **most of the sales of this business are in Australia**." *The seasonality line contradicts the geography line.* Cross-reference every claim in a listing against every other claim; brokers rarely scrub for internal consistency.

3. **Concentration in a commodity.** The hosts are explicit that one product at 80% of revenue is *not itself* the problem at this scale — "you can easily get one product to five million bucks on e-commerce." The problem is **what** the product is: "80% of sales are on a race to the bottom, no moat SKU. If 80% of sales are on a proprietary patented game or something, great."

4. **The supplier test — a diligence trick worth stealing.** Sign the NDA, identify the product, then search **Alibaba** (B2B) or **AliExpress** (B2C) for it. "If I was able to find it so quickly, is there really a moat here? If it's pretty freaking easy for you, that should give you a lot of pause." The Amazon version: search the product and count how many identical units are listed under different brand names — "if it looks like it's the same product, it's coming from the same factory."

5. **No managers, only executors.** Seven employees in video editing, customer service and marketing, three freelance designers — **and no strategist among them.** "On day one you show up and they're like 'what ads do I make, boss?' — and unless you make ads as good as the guy that just sold you the business, your ads are going to be bad." *The transferable skill is the owner's, and it isn't in the asset list.*

6. **The serial flipper pattern.** "Whoever owns this does this every six months and has a couple of these going in parallel and has [the broker] on speed dial — once they have two years of financials it's SBA eligible, they call." **Two diligence questions the hosts propose: how many other businesses does this owner own, and how many have they sold?** They guarantee both numbers are high.

7. **The inflection-point sale.** "You start a business, you scale it up and then you reach this inflection point where 80% of your sales is a bug zapper, and you know what's about to happen... you've got two choices: come up with new hero products, or call [the broker] and sell." **Sellers sell at the top of a product's curve, not the bottom.**

**What would make it buyable**
- Only extreme risk transfer: "a seller note type share revenue with no obligation — so if this thing tanks, [the seller] doesn't get paid either. **The only way I would buy this would be with dramatic risk share by the seller.**"
- What's actually missing is a moat, and they name the three kinds worth having: **brand** (Nike-level, vigorously defended trademark), **IP** (proprietary formulas, patents), or **distribution** (Bill's own business: shelf space at the largest national retailers).

## Canadian-specific
- **None — and this deal is off-thesis for Jay entirely.** It is an Australian-founded, China-manufactured, 3PL-shipped, Facebook-ads-driven e-commerce brand. Canada and New Zealand are explicitly the rump 20% of revenue.
- The *reasoning* transfers to Canadian service-business diligence with almost no modification:
  - **The cheap-multiple warning** is directly applicable. A Canadian janitorial or landscaping book offered at 1.5× SDE has something wrong with it — usually a lost contract the seller hasn't disclosed yet, or a customer concentration about to unwind. Find it before you congratulate yourself.
  - **The internal-consistency test** on listings applies verbatim to Canadian broker CIMs.
  - **The "no strategist among the staff" test** is the single best question for an owner-operated service business: strip the owner out on paper and ask who makes decisions. See `extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md` on de-founding a business.
  - **The moat question in a service business** is not brand or patents — it's **recurring contracts, route density, and switching costs.** That is why the recurring-revenue thesis (`extracts/Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`) matters so much: it is the only moat these businesses have.
- Note also: **SBA eligibility drives the US small-deal market** in ways that have no Canadian equivalent. The Canadian analogue, CSBFP, finances *assets* and would not touch a deal like this at all. That difference is why Canadian asset-light deals need large VTBs.

## Verdict
**Off-thesis as a deal, high-value as a teardown.** Nothing about Australian bug zappers helps Jay buy a Canadian service business, and roughly a third of the runtime is banter about dingoes and dragonflies. But the *analytical moves* are the most transferable in the Acquisitions Anonymous set, and three of them belong in the due-diligence checklist:

1. **Treat a below-market multiple as a question, not a discount.** Ask what the seller knows about the next twelve months that you don't.
2. **Read the listing against itself.** The seasonality sentence exposed the geography sentence. Brokers write these documents in sections and rarely reconcile them.
3. **Ask what the owner personally does that nobody else in the business can do.** Then price the business as if that person is gone — because they will be.

Also worth keeping: the **serial-flipper questions** ("how many businesses do you own, how many have you sold?") and the **structural response to an un-diligenceable risk** — not a lower price, but risk transfer through a performance-contingent seller note. In Canada that means a large VTB with a genuine standstill, which is the recurring structural theme across this whole knowledge base.
