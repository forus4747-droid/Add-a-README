## Key numbers
**The business — 51 years old, small-town Florida, five SIC codes:**

| Year | Revenue | EBITDA | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | $9.8M | **$2.0M** | 20.4% |
| 2021 | $11.0M | **$1.7M** | 15.5% |
| 2022 | $12.0M | **$2.4M** | 20.0% |

- Services: well drilling, well pump replacement, aerators and softeners, irrigation, water treatment — **plus lawn fertilization, home pest control and weed control.**
- **$1M of real estate included.** No asking price disclosed. Financials are **compiled** (not reviewed or audited). Management team in place.
- **Licences required: state well drilling, state irrigation, state pest control.**
- **Expected trade: 4–5× EBITDA**, so roughly **$9.5–12M** including real estate.

**The financing arithmetic — the heart of the episode:**
- **SBA 7(a) hard cap: $5M.**
- Heather's rule of thumb: **an SBA 10-year loan at then-current rates supports ~3.75× adjusted EBITDA.**
- $2.4M × 3.75 = **$9M of theoretically supportable debt — but only $5M is available.**
- **→ a $4M gap** that must be filled with equity, a seller note, or both.
- **The SBA 504 program can finance the real estate above and beyond the $5M 7(a) cap** — "a little extra runway." Useful, but it doesn't close the gap.
- **Bill's proposed structure:** $5M SBA + $1M buyer equity = **$6M at close (~2.5× EBITDA)**, then **$1–2M more over five years** via a revenue-share forgivable seller note tied to EBITDA targets and transition assistance → **an effective 3–4× total**, with the back half at risk.

## What to look for
**Heather's "No Man's Land" — the most quotable structural insight in the corpus**
> **"$2.4 million in EBITDA is what I call the No Man's Land. It's over two million and it's below three. Being below three means it's going to be very hard to get any kind of non-SBA debt. Being above two means you're going to go well above the $5 million maximum. So you are too big for SBA, too small for everything else."**

And the compounding problem: **conventional lenders won't touch key-person risk.** "Conventional lenders don't like to lend into a situation where there's that much key-man risk. They'd rather lend into businesses that are professionalized, have a management team." So the deal that most needs conventional debt is the least likely to get it.

**Red flags**
- **Five SIC codes = five businesses.** The margin history gives it away: revenue rose steadily (9.8 → 11 → 12) while margin swung 20.4% → 15.5% → 20.0%. Heather: "it's hard to analyze a business like this when you've got such disparate lines... probably the mix changes a lot from year to year." **Demand revenue and margin by service line, for at least three years.**
- **Licences are held by individuals, not the business.** Bill spells it out: "typically it is the owner of the business that is licensed and everybody else operates underneath his licence. If he retires and leaves, you the new buyer need to achieve those licences." Heather: with three separate licences, "it raises the degree of difficulty by quite a bit." **Ask who personally holds each licence, whether they're staying, and how long qualification takes.**
- **The classic contradictory sentence: "Owner would like to retire but is willing to stay on in some capacity."** Bill: "That's like 'owner likes pizza but also hates cheese.' This guy does not want to stay on at all — he's hedging so it doesn't scare you away." **Treat every hedged transition promise as a no until it's in the LOI with a term and a payment.**
- **Well drilling is construction-linked.** "You're going to build something, you're going to drill wells. If you're not building something..." The *maintenance* revenue is the durable part — "there's a certain lifespan where they've got to go in and do heavy maintenance. I think that can be a really good business." **Split project revenue from recurring maintenance revenue before valuing anything.**
- **Compiled financials** on a $12M business after 51 years is thin. Expect a quality-of-earnings exercise.

**Green flags — the counter-argument, which is the more interesting one**
Girdley's read is that the "mish-mash" is actually **counter-cyclical diversification**: home pest control and weed control are recurring and recession-resistant, well pump replacement is non-discretionary maintenance, and new-construction drilling rides Florida's in-migration. **"I think people are going to look at this as a good mix, not a mish-mash."**

There's also a genuine competitive-moat observation about small markets: **"This hodgepodge is more common in small towns... there isn't enough demand to have lawn fertilization and home pest control as standalone businesses, but there is enough demand to have one company that does everything. And you don't have this army of private-equity-backed pest control companies coming after you in small-town USA."** *In a thin market, being the only multi-service operator is the moat.*

**The key-person reality check, honestly stated**
Girdley suspects the owner "knows everybody in the town, is seen at the morning coffee counter on Main Street, and that's how they get all their business — you're going to have to decide to be that person."

Bill's response is the one worth internalising: **"That's kind of table stakes. It's very hard to find a small business to buy with one or two million bucks of EBITDA that does not rely a little bit on being the owner in the market. It's just hard to not do that at this size."** Owner-embeddedness is not a disqualifier at this scale — it's the price of admission, and it's why these businesses trade at 4–5× instead of the double digits software commands.

**The carve-out idea — and why it usually fails**
Girdley's PE playbook: buy the whole thing, split it in two, sell the lawn/pest business to a PE-backed platform (which "will definitely pay much more than four or five times") and the well drilling to a drilling platform. Sell the parts for more than the whole.

The rebuttals are the valuable part:
- **Bill:** both halves must be individually big enough. "If you've got 2 million of EBITDA in one thing and 400K in the other, the smaller one can be a lot harder to sell." And you must establish **during diligence** whether it's actually separable — shared staff, shared trucks, shared overhead.
- **Heather, decisively:** **"Figuring out the cost structure of a carve-out like that is extremely difficult. I've seen people do it, and I know what it looked like afterwards, and I've never seen anybody do it right."** What people assume shared costs will be, versus what they turn out to be, "is usually pretty different."
- Bill's conclusion: **"That gap between the multiple you pay and the multiple you sell at needs to be pretty good. You need a good margin of safety there. Paying five is not going to get it done."**

**On forgivable/earnout seller notes under SBA rules** — Heather notes banks disagree: some say a revenue-share note is a prohibited earnout, others allow it. "**There's nothing in the SBA SOP about earnouts** — a lot of the rules you hear people throw out are just their bank's interpretation." Workable if total consideration stays within the appraised valuation and the DSCR holds.

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout (Florida, SBA 7(a), SBA 504, SIC/NAICS codes). But the structural lesson translates, and gets worse.**
- **Canada's "No Man's Land" starts far lower.** The CSBFP caps at **$1.15M** and finances *assets only* — not goodwill, not the business price. There is no Canadian equivalent of a $5M SBA 7(a) acquisition loan, and no equivalent of the 504 real-estate top-up. A Canadian buyer looking at a business with **$2.4M EBITDA** has essentially three options: conventional bank debt secured on hard assets and real estate (which for an asset-light service business is a fraction of the price), BDC subordinate/growth capital, or a very large VTB.
- **Practical consequence:** the $4M gap Heather describes on a US deal would be proportionally *larger* in Canada on a smaller deal. **This is the structural reason the Canadian playbook leans so heavily on VTBs** — see `extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md` on a postponed VTB counting as equity to the bank, and `extracts/Canadian-acquisition-finance/Financing-a-Business-Purchase-in-Canada.md`.
- **BDC is the Canadian institution to look at for exactly this gap** — it lends where chartered banks won't and offers subordinate financing that behaves like the mezzanine layer missing from this deal. Verify current terms directly.
- **Licensing is provincial in Canada and the same individual-vs-business problem applies:** pesticide applicator licences (Ontario's structural/landscape exterminator classes, Quebec's certification regime), water-well contractor licensing (Ontario Reg. 903 requires licensed well contractors and technicians), and irrigation certification. **Confirm for each licence: who holds it, whether it attaches to a person or the corporation, whether it survives a change of control, and how long re-qualification takes.** In Ontario and Quebec, **cosmetic-pesticide restrictions** also materially change what a lawn-fertilization and weed-control business can even sell — a US comparable's margins will not transfer.
- The **small-market moat argument applies strongly in Canada**, where secondary markets are thin and PE roll-ups are less active than in the US Sun Belt. A single multi-service operator in a Canadian town of 10–15,000 may face no institutional competition at all.

## Verdict
**The most relevant Acquisitions Anonymous teardown in the corpus for Jay's thesis** — a small-town, multi-service, licence-heavy home services business combining pest control and lawn care, sold by a retiring owner who *is* the business. That is very close to what a Canadian search will actually turn up, just larger.

Four things to carry forward:

1. **The financing-gap arithmetic.** Debt capacity ≈ 3.75× adjusted EBITDA on a 10-year amortisation is a useful first-pass ceiling in any jurisdiction. Compute it, compare it to the price, and the gap is the negotiation — equity, VTB size, and VTB terms. Then note that Canada's institutional debt ceiling is far lower than the US $5M, so the gap is proportionally bigger and the VTB must be correspondingly larger, longer, or interest-only.
2. **Licences are personal, not corporate.** For any Canadian pest control, well, or irrigation business, this is a closing condition, not a diligence footnote. Establish it in week one.
3. **Multi-service is a moat in a small market and a valuation penalty everywhere.** The same diversification that makes the business defensible locally makes it hard to value, hard to finance and hard to sell. Buy it for the former; **price it for the latter.**
4. **Don't buy on a carve-out thesis.** Two experienced practitioners here say separating shared cost structures reliably goes wrong. If the return depends on splitting the business, you need a margin of safety far larger than a 4–5× entry gives you.

The most durable line for a first-time buyer, though, is Bill's: **at $1–2M of EBITDA, every business depends on the owner being known in the market.** That is not a red flag to be diligenced away — it's the job you're buying. Plan to be the person at the coffee counter.
