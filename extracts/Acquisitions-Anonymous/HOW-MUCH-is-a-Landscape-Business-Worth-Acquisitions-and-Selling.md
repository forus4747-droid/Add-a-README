# HOW MUCH is a Landscape Business Worth? — Mike Andes (Augusta Lawn Care)

## Key numbers — margins by service line (the most cited numbers in the corpus)
- **Fertilization / granular applications: 70–80% margin.** "The best multiplier in our industry… it's very repeatable, mostly a sales game, plug-and-play and scalable."
- **Mowing: 10–15% margin.**
- Ranking of what buyers pay up for: **fertilization highest → irrigation close behind → maintenance/mowing lower → installation lowest.**
- Customer annual value worked example: a **$900/year** customer staying 4–5 years at a 10–20% margin is worth **$500–800 of profit over their entire life** — "that's a lot of work to keep a customer for five years." The target is to bundle mowing + cleanup + fertilization + trimming to move a **$1,500/year customer to $3,000/year**, doubling revenue with no new customer acquisition.
- The scale illustration: **500 customers at $3,000 = $1.5M** beats **1,500 customers at $1,000** — same revenue, but the second needs far more office staff, trucks, trailers, employees and drive time. "You'd much rather have 20 great customers that pay you $100,000 a year than 2,000 customers that pay you a thousand."
- Gym acquisition cross-example: he bought an Anytime Fitness partly because members were on **12- and 24-month contracts at $40–50/month**, giving **over half a million dollars of contractually guaranteed income** — "if I came in and did a horrible job of operating, I knew that guaranteed income was coming."
- He assigned **$50,000 of valuation to retaining one specific trainer**, and made hiring a particular office manager a **contingency of the sale.**

## What to look for — the seven drivers of value
1. **Recurring vs installation.** An install is worth only that one project: "you install and then you never hear from that customer again." Recurring services have "unlimited potential for 10, 20 years." Look at the trade press: the companies bought are **fertilization, pest control, sprinkler maintenance and mowing** — "you almost never see a hardscape installation company being bought out."
2. **Cyclicality risk.** Landscape installation tracks the housing market: "in 2007/2008 that's why so many landscape install companies got wiped out — the ones that remained were the companies that did a lot of maintenance."
3. **Skill concentration.** If 20% of revenue is hardscaping that only the owner or one project manager can do, **a buyer must write that 20% off the top line.** "It's number one hard to buy because you need a very educated buyer, and secondly if that skilled person leaves, the revenue is destroyed."
4. **Contracts.** Signed 9- or 12-month contracts solve winter cash flow *and* "take away the edge from the customers when they hear the business has been acquired" — the obligation survives the change of ownership.
5. **Team structure and owner position.** The ladder is *field → office → estimator → CEO.* "If they're in any of those first three phases, their company's value is much, much lower." A buyer can replace a CEO; they cannot replace an owner who is also the estimator with all the customer relationships.
6. **Documented systems.** But with an honest caveat most sources skip: **"in theory you can make a really good training manual and if people don't use it, it's useless."** His working solution — run a full training day, video the whole thing, edit it down (50 minutes for mowing crews, ~90 minutes for landscape), and for office work record 2–3 minute screen captures of every task into a searchable folder (~80 videos and counting). "If Liz got hit by a bus, the office staff could go in there and watch some videos."
7. **Stable margin.** The maturity tell: revenue naturally swings across the year, but **does the margin hold?** "If in June you do $100,000 at a 20% margin, in January you do $50,000 — is your margin still 20%, or does it shrink to 2%?" Wildly varying annual margins signal a business still figuring things out.

## The absentee-ownership test
> "Try to leave your business for a day. Then seven days. Then two weeks. If you can stay away for two solid weeks and everything goes fine — sales still come in, the business is profitable, people still show up — you're setting yourself up for a very successful sale."

He found this out by accident, leaving for a week for a conference: "things still happened, sales still came in, estimators somehow figured it out." **The exercise doubles as diligence: ask a seller when they last took two consecutive weeks off, and what broke.**

## What sellers hide
- The blunt version of the "buying a job" problem: "there are companies I've looked at where it's all just the job for the business owner. There's no value attached to that entity — the person buying is buying a job."
- **Owner-dependence in the team, not just the tasks:** "if I sold my business, about a third of them would probably leave, because they've bought into what I'm doing and the mission behind me." He names this as a vulnerability in his own business. Ask a seller directly how much of the crew is loyal to *them*, and check whether compensation is tied to how the owner personally sells.
- The buyer's negotiating asymmetry, stated openly: "there are so many ways to come up with a valuation. **If you're the buyer you want to go with the lowest one; if you're the seller you want to be educated enough to charge the highest.**"

## Canadian-specific
US operator (Washington State), franchise-adjacent. **US — verify for Canada.** The service-line margin hierarchy and the recurring-vs-install logic transfer directly; Canadian winters make the contract/cash-flow point *more* acute, since a 9- or 12-month contract is what carries a Canadian lawn business through a five-month dead season. Note also that fertilization in Canada is subject to provincial cosmetic-pesticide restrictions (notably Ontario and Quebec), which may compress the 70–80% margin line here — **verify before applying that number to a Canadian target.**
