## Key numbers
- **Revenue $4.1M. Cash flow (EBITDA) $2.2M. Claimed margins 56–67%.**
- **30–50 disaster restoration projects per month across 10 large cities.** More than one a day.
- **Seven employees. No office. Fully remote. No inventory.** All fulfilment via a subcontractor network.
- Founded **2016**. Mitigation work only (water, fire, biohazard) — **no repair work.**
- Billing direct to the **building owner's insurance company** on completion.
- Listed on BizBuySell with **no broker and no asking price** — almost certainly for sale by owner.
- Market size claimed: **$255B/year** property claims industry.
- **The implied markup:** with subs doing all the work at ~50% margins, "if the subcontractor charges $100 for the job, they bill the job out at $200."

## What to look for
**The margin question — and how to interrogate any too-good margin**

Bill's framing is the reusable part. At $4.1M revenue and $2.2M EBITDA, roughly **$1.9M covers everything else** — subcontractors, seven salaries, insurance, software, and marketing. His conclusion: **"there doesn't seem to be room in this P&L for any advertising."**

That leads to the diagnostic question: **where do the leads come from?** Only two answers are possible, and they demand completely different deal structures.

| Lead source | What it means | Deal implication |
|---|---|---|
| **SEO / lead generation** | The business is a lead-gen platform; subs are fulfilment | Transferable and scalable — "the obvious thing to do is go into the next 20 cities" |
| **Insurance carrier relationships** | The owner's personal relationships or an approved-vendor listing | **"They may just like that person, and they also could change jobs"** — needs heavy deferred/contingent consideration |

**Bill's evidence against the SEO explanation is the sharpest analysis in the episode.** He searches "Charlotte flooded basement" live: the top ~60% of the page is paid ads (ServPro twice, Sunshine Restoration, restoration-local.com "at your door in 60 minutes or less," Ally Property Rescue "45-minute on-site response") — competitors literally one-upping each other on response time. Below that, the organic results are aggressively optimised ("Basement Waterproofing Company in Charlotte NC" with the city in the URL and title).

His point: **a real lead-gen business in this category would be buying ads, because the economics obviously work — and buying ads would crush the margin to well below 50%.** So either they're "SEO complete wizards," or there is something undisclosed. "We must be off track."

**On moats — a genuinely useful taxonomy**
- **Being on an approved-vendor database is usually NOT a moat.** Mills, from experience: some lists cost $2–3K/year to join and maintain, "we've never had tons of work come as a result of that."
- **Credentials that ARE moats** are the ones that are genuinely hard to obtain: **DOD/secret/top-secret clearance** (a plumber, IT tech, HVAC tech or janitor who can enter secure facilities), manufacturer certification for a specific chain (his example: a friend whose business changes locks for hotel chains — "do you do certified work for this manufacturer? Once you have those credentials, all the doors open"), or municipal vendor listings for regulated work like **backflow prevention testing**.
- The general principle: **ask what specifically prevents the next operator from doing this, and whether that barrier survives the owner's departure.**

**Why the middleman position exists at all — the working-capital insight**
Mills explains why subcontractors tolerate a 50% haircut instead of going direct:
- **Subs want to be paid immediately; insurers pay in 45–60 days.** The middleman floats them. "He is paying the subcontractor and then he's waiting 45 or 60 days to get paid by the insurance company."
- Subs "don't have a website, don't have a brand, and the insurance companies don't call them."
- Subs don't want the paperwork, bureaucracy and claim-filing rigour required to get paid by carriers.
- **Consequence for the buyer: this business needs real working capital**, even though it looks asset-light. You pay the subs before the insurer pays you, on 30–50 jobs a month.

**Red flags**
- **"This is the best it's ever going to look."** Bill's adage, and the single most useful sentence for a first-time buyer: **"as the deal progresses it only gets worse. You don't find these gold nuggets lying around."** Expect the 56–67% margin to normalise — Mills suggests underwriting at **~35%** and testing whether the deal still works.
- **Capital intensity has been pushed onto subs.** Mitigation requires trailers of air movers and dehumidifiers mobilised within hours, billed at pre-negotiated daily rates per unit. The seller owns none of it — "which makes me a little bit suspicious of how high their margins are."
- **"Remote, low overhead, easy" is a claim to distrust.** Mills: "anytime you're putting somebody in somebody else's building to perform work, things will go wrong... **we have more headaches with subs than we do with employees.** Subs are kind of like the Wild West." Ten cities, more than one job a day, and someone will break something and the owner will say *get here now*. "This is not smart passive income."
- **A mature, franchised, consolidating category.** ServPro, First Response and PE-backed multi-unit operators are already in it. "It's not the new thing — buildings have always leaked, pipes always burst."

**On no-broker deals — worth quoting in full**
> "When there is no broker you are going to want a bullet in the head, multiple times. This seller is going to think this business is worth 50 million dollars, or he's going to want unreasonable terms, or he's not going to be able to produce the diligence documents and will accuse you of thinking he's a shady person. He's going to try to change the price at the last minute. **Doing a deal without a broker is the worst.**"

Bill's own case study: on a no-broker acquisition, the seller's brother — an *estate* attorney — refused standard reps and warranties, including a rep for fraud, and nearly blew up the deal. Bill offered to pay for any of three M&A lawyers he recommended; she hired a fourth of her own choosing who also nearly destroyed the deal and then billed him. He paid it.

**The counterweight:** a no-broker listing is also where market inefficiency lives — "that's sort of what you hope you'll find, a really good business that's not represented by a broker."

**The valuation ceiling — Mills' "does it pencil" test**
> "You can only pay so much, because the cash flow has to support the debt service... you can't pay more than a purchase price that reflects debt service over $1.75–1.8 million of total cash flow. **Anything above that is basically predicated on growth** — 'we're just going to grow so much that the debt service doesn't matter.'"

That is the same discipline as Bruce Marks' "congratulations, you've structured a deal that doesn't work" in `extracts/David-C-Barnett/How-to-Buy-a-Business-with-Seller-Financing-How-5-M-A-Experts-Use-It.md`, stated from the buyer's side.

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout.** Nothing about Canadian restoration markets, though the industry structure (ServPro, Paul Davis, First On Site and franchise networks) is comparable in Canada.
- **The transferable Canadian applications:**
  - **The margin interrogation method** applies to every Canadian service business Jay looks at. Reconstruct the P&L from the outside: subcontractor cost, wages, insurance, vehicles, advertising. If there's no room in the stated margin for a line every competitor obviously spends on, ask why.
  - **The lead-source question is the core diligence question for any local service business** — janitorial, landscaping, pest control alike. Is demand generated by a system (SEO, paid ads, tracked referral programme, contracts) or by the owner's relationships? See the same point in `extracts/Cleaning-operators/10-Years-of-Cleaning-Business-Advice-in-21-Minutes.md` on marketing attribution.
  - **The subcontractor classification risk is materially worse in Canada.** A restoration business running entirely on "subcontractors" who work under its brand, to its schedule, billing through its claims — the CRA control/integration test would scrutinise that hard. See the detailed Canadian analysis in `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Independent-Contractor-vs-Employee-Cleaning-Business.md`.
  - **Working capital against insurer receivables** is the same in Canada. Restoration and remediation work billed to Canadian insurers runs on similar 30–60 day cycles; set the working-capital peg on the aged claims listing, not on a formula.
  - **The DSCR ceiling bites harder in Canada** because there is no $5M SBA equivalent. On a $2.2M EBITDA business, Canadian institutional debt would cover far less of the price — see `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`.

## Verdict
**A strong teardown of a business Jay would never buy, teaching three habits he'll use on every business he does look at.**

**First, the margin interrogation.** Rebuild the P&L from the outside and find the line that's missing. Here it was advertising: a 50%-margin lead-gen business in a category where every competitor buys ads is either extraordinary or misrepresented, and the base rate says misrepresented. Mills' response — underwrite at 35% and see if it still works — is the correct one. Combine with Bill's adage: **the deal is at its most attractive on the first day you see it.**

**Second, the lead-source question, which determines the deal structure.** Systematised demand is transferable and worth a full multiple; owner-relationship demand needs heavy contingent consideration. Ask it early, verify it independently, and structure to it.

**Third, the DSCR ceiling as a hard constraint.** Mills' "does it pencil" test — price capped by what the cash flow services, with anything above that being a bet on growth — is the discipline that keeps a buyer solvent. In Canada, where institutional acquisition debt is far scarcer, that ceiling is lower still.

The bonus lesson, and the one most likely to matter in practice: **no-broker deals are where the bargains and the disasters both live.** If Jay sources directly from a retiring Canadian owner — which the Divitkos and Beers material both recommend — he should expect to fund the seller's professional advice himself and budget the patience for it.
