## Key numbers
**The deal (Axial listing, Northeast US, residential plumbing + HVAC):**

| Year | Revenue | EBITDA | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | $4.0M | $1.1M | 27.5% |
| 2023 | **$4.5M** (+12.5%) | **$1.4M** | **33.1%** |

- **2,500 active accounts**, stated average customer spend **>$1,500** (Mills computes $4.5M ÷ 2,500 = **$1,800**).
- **60% hydronic heat and plumbing (boilers), 40% HVAC.**
- **23 employees.** Exclusively repair and replacement — **no new construction.**

**Wilson's industry benchmarks — the most valuable numbers in the episode:**
- **"Active account" definitions differ by trade:** plumbing = **touched in the last 36 months** (you don't need a plumber more than once every 2–3 years); **HVAC = 18 months** (furnace tune-up, AC tune-up, so once or twice a year).
- **Average ticket by job type:** boiler **$15,000–17,000**; HVAC system **$9,000–11,000**; tank hot water heater **~$3,000.** Northeast average tickets and household incomes are higher than the Southeast.
- **Reactivation upside, real example:** a friend in Chicago bought a company with **4,000–5,000 active accounts and took it from $6M to $18M in 24 months**, largely by reactivating the dormant customer list. "That 2,500 can turn into a really substantial new lead source."
- **The infrastructure step-change comes at ~$5M revenue / 20–30 technicians** — which is exactly where this business sits.

## What to look for
**The "Apex value" profile for residential home services** (Wilson, crediting Chris Hoffman):
1. **Residential service** — repair and replacement.
2. **Under 10% commercial exposure.**
3. **No new construction.**
4. **Good tech stack** — ServiceTitan or one of the two or three other top CRMs, so a buyer can actually handle the data.
5. **Strong Google reviews / GMB presence.**

This is a genuinely useful screen. Score any home-services target against those five before doing anything else.

**Why the 33% margin is real but not sustainable — the diagnosis**
Wilson reverse-engineers the org chart from the margin: **"my bet is they either don't have a service manager, or the owner IS the service manager. Probably no install manager. Two people in the office picking up the phone, maybe a third part-time doing dispatch. No real marketing."**

His verdict: **"Real, maybe. Sustainable, no — because if you grow this business at all you have to actually start figuring out real customer acquisition, and that costs something."** And: "You're not going to maintain 33% margins doubling this business. No way."

**Underwrite the normalised margin, not the historic one.** The historic margin is what the business earns while under-investing in management and marketing — i.e. while being unsaleable-in-two-years.

**The multi-trade problem at $4.5M**
Asked whether being both plumbing and HVAC at this size is a good idea, Wilson: **"It's a bad idea."**
- **Aaron Gainer (EC Plumbers, $75M across Columbus/Dayton/Cincinnati) stayed pure plumbing until ~$30M** — late by industry convention, and "probably the right decision, because then they became this best-in-class plumbing company and accelerated their growth."
- Wilson on his own three-trade business in the mid-$20Ms: **"I have friends that are the same size but pure HVAC or pure plumbing, and their businesses are just better than mine — way more focused, way higher margin, way better cash flow."**
- **Why owners do it anyway — the diagnosis that matters:** "There are these plateaus in your business and you don't know what's next... launching HVAC looks easy because once we know the steps to something it looks easy. If I've taken a company from one million to five, in my brain I'm like, well, I know how to take a company from one to five, that's easy — ignoring all the actual difficulty of running two markets." **They're looking for an easy-button step change instead of doubling down on the one thing.**
- His inference about this specific business: **"That's why they launched the other trade — because they couldn't figure out lead flow in the first one."**

**Why "no new construction" adds value — and how owners get trapped in it**
Wilson's account of how residential service companies drift into new construction is one of the best passages in the corpus:
> "Guy launches a plumbing company. I need food, I call my friends, I call my family, I'm on Facebook... you need work, so guys get into new construction because it sort of falls in their lap... **Normally it's the stability of the owner's home life that gets these owners into new construction** and doing projects they just shouldn't. If they'd taken that same amount of time, instead of laying pipe, they could have gone door to door, they could have figured out how to market. **It's almost always a lead flow problem.**"

And why they keep it: "They hang on to it because they're scared of the down season — 'ah, it keeps us busy in the winter.' **Well, you should learn how to market. That's really the answer you're looking for.**"

*This maps directly onto Canadian seasonal service businesses* — the landscaper who takes on construction work, the pest controller who does one-off jobs in winter. **Ask why the off-season work exists: is it a strategy, or a symptom of not being able to generate leads?**

The counterpoint is given fairly: Mills' own roofing business is 100% commercial, ~50% new construction, average order value **$350,000** — "we're capturing 20 years' worth of margin in a four-to-six-month period." And Matt Ballard runs a **$55–60M nearly-all-new-construction HVAC business in Vegas.** The problem isn't new construction; it's new construction *inside* a small residential service business, where it's two different customer types, two different technician types, and two different businesses.

**Union exposure — a Northeast-specific red flag**
Neither host can tell whether the listing omits union status because there is none or "so it doesn't scare people away." Wilson notes **residential service is unionised in Boston**, which is unusual, and that Mills' estimators plug in roughly **one-tenth the cost per crew per day** that union shops do on comparable commercial work. **If a listing is silent on union status in a union geography, ask directly.**

**Dense urban vs suburban**
Wilson won't opine on dense-city operations — "these trucks are not moving... their billable would have to be two or three times mine to cover the traffic. Maybe they can only do two jobs a day." His recommendation for a first-time buyer is **suburban**, and the reason is unexpectedly good: **peer availability.** "How many cities have the problems we just described? Maybe ten. The other 4,000 cities all have peers to talk to who don't have those problems."

## Canadian-specific
- **US throughout** (Northeast US, Axial, SBA 7(a), ServiceTitan). But this is the most transferable Track A episode in the corpus for a Canadian home-services search.
- **Boilers and hydronic heat are a strong Canadian analogue.** The installed base of residential boilers across Ontario, Quebec and Atlantic Canada is large and aging, and the ticket sizes ($15–17K) are broadly comparable. A Canadian hydronic/plumbing service business with an aging boiler base is very close to this deal.
- **Union exposure matters more in Canada, not less.** Construction and trades unionisation is significantly higher in Canada than the US, and **provincial successor-employer rules bind a purchaser to an existing collective agreement — often in an asset sale as well as a share sale.** See the Canadian labour note in `extracts/David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`. Get certification status in writing early.
- **Trade licensing is provincial and often individual.** In Ontario, plumbing and gas work require certified tradespeople and TSSA gas-fitter registration; refrigeration/AC work requires ODP certification. Confirm who holds what, and whether the company's licence depends on a departing owner.
- **The financing ceiling is far tighter.** Wilson thinks this "probably can get done with SBA 7(a)." A Canadian buyer has no such instrument — CSBFP caps at $1.15M and finances assets only. On a $4.5M-revenue business with $1.4M EBITDA, expect a much larger equity plus VTB requirement. See `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`.
- **The "peer availability" argument favours Canadian secondary markets** for a first-time buyer, and cuts against Toronto/Montreal core operations for the same reasons Wilson gives.

## Quotable lines
1. "If I'm paying more than 50% of revenue I'm like, do I like it that much? ...If you're paying more than one times revenue for a company this small in plumbing/HVAC, I think you lost the plot."
2. "It's almost always a lead flow problem — so then they end up with new construction. It's not well thought out. It's 'I'm doing this in order to feed my family.'"
3. "You could rebuild this business in two years for a hell of a lot less than $5 million."

## Verdict
**The single most useful Track A video in the corpus for anyone buying a residential service business**, because it comes from an operator with real scale in the exact trade being valued — and because his valuation framework is fundamentally different from everyone else's.

**Wilson's heuristic, which belongs in KNOWLEDGE.md:** he does not price on EBITDA multiples at all. He prices on **50–60% of revenue**, has paid 1× revenue only once (asset-heavy), and considers anything above 1× revenue for a small plumbing/HVAC business "losing the plot." His reasoning: **"I don't care what your EBITDA was. You have a machine that produces X amount of revenue at X gross margin — that's the thing I care about."** Below gross margin, he's replacing the cost structure anyway.

**The metrics he actually asks for** are a ready-made diligence list for any local service business: **phone calls per day, number of Google Business Profiles, number of reviews, number of active accounts** (with the definition specified). "If you get 50 phone calls a day, I can do something with this. If you get three, I'm going to tap out." Nothing about the bookkeeper's salary or the office headcount, because those get replaced.

Three further things to carry forward:

1. **Reverse-engineer the org chart from the margin.** A 33% margin at $4.5M means somebody isn't being paid — usually the owner is doing the service-manager job and nobody is doing marketing. Add those costs back before you value anything. This is the single most reusable analytical move in the episode.
2. **Reactivation of a dormant customer list is a real, large value-creation lever** — $6M to $18M in 24 months on 4–5,000 accounts. When evaluating a Canadian target, ask for total historical customers, not just active ones, and check whether the CRM can actually reach them.
3. **The buy-versus-build check.** "You could rebuild this business in two years for a hell of a lot less." Wilson's advice is to pressure-test any price against **industry peers in Facebook groups and trade associations**, not against finance-trained buyers: "If you told a group of people who used to work at Bain that you'd pay a three times multiple, they'd say that makes total sense. If you told a guy in the industry, he'd say — what are you talking about, you could rebuild this in a year or two."

The closing market observation is also worth noting for a Canadian searcher: **a $4–5M home-services deal draws "thousands of eyeballs"** in the US; above $10–20M it's "really crowded." Canadian markets are far thinner — which is Jay's structural advantage, and the reason a deal at 3–4× SDE may still be available there when the same business would be bid to 5–6× in the US.
