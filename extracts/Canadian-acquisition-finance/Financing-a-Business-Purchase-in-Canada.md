## Key numbers — how CSBFP actually works
**The core insight: CSBFP does not care what the business sold for. It cares what *stuff* is being financed.** That is the opposite of the US SBA model, and it is why most US acquisition content misleads Canadian buyers.

- Industry Canada **guarantees 85% of the qualifying loan**; you apply to a bank, credit union or participating lease-financing company, and *they* apply for the guarantee.
- **Eligibility cap: businesses with revenue above $10M/year do not qualify.**
- **Total program limit $1.15M**, broken down as:
  - up to **$1,000,000 for real estate** used by the business — **90% of the purchase financeable**;
  - up to **$500,000 for equipment, machinery, leasehold improvements and fit-ups** (this covers flooring, wall coverings, décor, demising walls in a *rented* location — the landlord's property is still accepted as collateral);
  - **$150,000 for "intangibles" and working capital**, added in 2022 — goodwill, franchise fees, inventory, and operating expenses. Can be taken as a term loan **or a guaranteed line of credit** (the line makes more sense for inventory; the term loan for goodwill).
- **Amortisation: up to 25 years on real estate, up to 10 years on everything else** (that is how long the guarantee lasts).
- **Loans are assumable** — if a target already has a CSBFP loan and you qualify with the lender, the lender can switch the names and the guarantee continues. *You can structure an offer that assumes the existing debt.*
- Lenders apply their own **overall debt-to-equity requirement** on top: expect to need roughly **25% of the total project** as your own money, or outside collateral.

## What to look for
**The structural problem for Jay's thesis, stated plainly:**
> "It does not offer a whole lot of financing for someone who's buying a business that has very little in the way of tangible stuff. Your service business that doesn't have these assets — you're not going to be able to access very much out of this program except for that $150,000 allowance."

Consequence, in his words: **"Traditionally in Canada, people that have businesses that are asset light have come to expect that they are going to have to finance more of the transaction through seller financing."** A janitorial or cleaning company is exactly this case — a truck, some floor machines, and goodwill. Plan for a large VTB, not a large CSBFP loan.

**How bankers actually assemble the deal (the "jigsaw"):** they will jam as much as possible into the guaranteed envelope, then write additional non-guaranteed loans alongside it. A real example: one guaranteed loan for real estate, a second guaranteed loan for equipment under $500K, and a third conventional loan for the business itself secured by personal guarantee or **a lien on the buyer's house.** This is why stories circulate of Canadians getting "the whole thing financed" — it was three loans, only two of them CSBFP, and the buyer had outside net worth.

**Appraisals you will need:**
- A **certified machinery and equipment appraisal** to establish the fixed-asset value the loan attaches to. The program uses **"fair market value in continued use"** — deliberately the definition that produces the highest number.
- To use the $150K against goodwill you need a **second valuation** (a "most probable selling price" report) establishing the going-concern value; goodwill is the residual after fixed assets and operating capital.
- Appraisal also required when buying equipment from a **non-arm's-length party**. Buying from a dealer? The invoice suffices.
- Watch the denominator when comparing offers: banks tend to lend a percentage of **fair market value**; leasing companies a percentage of **orderly or forced liquidation value**. "75% from the bank" can beat "90% from the leasing company."

**Bank policy sits on top of the program and varies enormously.** Only **two banks in Canada** will finance restaurant acquisitions under it, and then only equipment and furniture; others "wouldn't touch a restaurant with a ten-foot pole." **Shop lenders.** And: "the banker makes all the difference — the more experienced the banker, the easier it's going to be." Banks have been actively training small-business advisers on acquisition deals in the last couple of years.

## Canadian-specific
Entirely Canadian — this *is* the Canadian answer to "where is our SBA?" Composition of what you buy (per his slide): purchase price = **fixed assets + operating capital (inventory, AR, cash) + goodwill**. CSBFP reaches the first bucket well, the third barely. **Worth the read — essential, and the necessary correction to every US acquisition video.** Read it immediately after the VTB video: together they explain why a Canadian buying an asset-light service business finances it with a large vendor take-back plus a modest CSBFP slice, not with a US-style 90% acquisition loan.
