## Key numbers
- Bought **Mikrolink (Toronto)** in 2014: **~$6M revenue, 33 employees**, at **~5x EBITDA**. Sold ~7 years later on a **revenue multiple** — the whole return came from the gap between buying on earnings and selling on revenue.
- Deal structure: **60–70% cash at close** (bank debt + investor equity) and **30–40% seller note**, amortising over **3–4 years** at an interest rate "many multiples of the prevailing rates at the time, which were basically zero."
- Senior debt from **one of the big five Canadian banks**; loan fully repaid within the **first three years**.
- Investor outcome: **~4x gross return on equity over just under 7 years.**
- Search fund asset class: average **~5x returns, ~30–32% IRR**, outperforming private equity, private debt, VC, real estate and public markets since the mid-1980s.
- Searcher's own equity is earned, not bought — **up to 30%: 10% granted at acquisition, 10% time-vested over 4–5 years, 10% performance-vested** on returns to investors. He personally started with "$100,000 of debt from business school" and no cash.
- Seller left **"much closer to 10% than 50%"** of valuation on the table by choosing him over PE/strategic buyers.

## What to look for
**Why this seller sold to an individual instead of the higher bidder — the most useful section for anyone approaching Canadian owners:**
- They disliked private equity: being "one asset in a portfolio," no personal relationship with the operator, a finite ownership period — **and critically, PE needed the father and son to stay on.** The father was in his early 70s and wanted out.
- They disliked strategics: fear the company "would cease to exist and be gobbled up 12 to 24 months post-sale," and that **half the employees would be rationalised** because the acquirer already has HR, finance and developers.
- What won it: **"I'm a succession plan that's looking for the right business to succeed, and I think yours might be it."** Plus the small things — keeping the company name, being both owner and operator, having a portfolio of one, and **99.9% of his net worth tied to their single illiquid asset.**
- The bar: **"Price has to be good enough and structure has to be good enough."** Qualitative factors only decide it *after* that bar is cleared.

**Pricing insight — think in dollars, not multiples:** sellers usually have an absolute number in their head, often divorced from the economics. Sometimes that works for you (his did divide elegantly into EBITDA); sometimes it is fatal ($30M in her head, $500K of EBITDA). "When they see that number for the first time with all those zeros following it, it can be validating."

**Issue LOIs frequently — a buyer's best time-saving tool:**
- "LOIs are barely worth the paper they're printed on… as a buyer you don't really give up much. But as a seller you give up a lot — you give up exclusivity."
- They **surface valuation expectations instantly** ("if I think you're worth 5 million and you think you're worth 50, I walk away that day") **and structural expectations** — most first-time sellers assume 100% cash, and a formal document saying "10% roll and a 30% seller note" reveals immediately whether that is palatable. "It separates the real sellers from the tyre kickers."

**Seller note mechanics clarified on air:** the seller is a lender, ranking **behind the senior bank but ahead of all equity including the buyer's**. Seller notes typically carry **no covenants** — EBITDA can move, indebtedness can move, and the seller cannot take the business back. But no equity distribution can occur if it impairs the note, and the tier-one bank's agreement typically blocks dividends, recaps or a sale without prior approval.

## The failed sale — the best cautionary content here
He tried to sell in **2018** via a formal banked process, solicited **12–13 bids**, went exclusive with a PE firm, and it **collapsed during purchase-agreement drafting after about a year.** Two causes, both self-inflicted and both diligence-relevant:
1. **Performance deteriorated during the process** because selling is a full-time job and running the business is more than a full-time job. "The time and attention of myself and my management team got diluted."
2. **Key person risk concentrated entirely in him.** Management was "myself and three other people"; by the 2020 sale it was "myself and seven other people." The specific tell: **he did the entire management presentation himself**, which made obvious to buyers that everything ran through him.

The 2018 PE offer required him to **roll 50% of his proceeds**. The 2020 buyer was a strategic with a deep management bench who **didn't need him at all** — so he got a clean, fast (**3 month**) exit with no roll, at the cost of "probably some valuation."

**Burnout, stated plainly:** "I was experiencing burnout, which up until that point I thought was just a fancy way to say I was tired, but came to appreciate was actually a clinical diagnosis that required meaningful treatment." He went to his board and said he wanted to hand over "a stable patient."

## Canadian-specific
**Strongly Canadian.** Toronto-based deal, senior debt from a big-five Canadian bank, and an explicit note that **entrepreneurship-through-acquisition is formally taught at the top US business schools but far less so in Canada** — meaning less searcher competition for Canadian sellers. The chef-vs-food-critic distinction between operators and investors, and the observation that "the people who bring a company from 0 to 5 million are not always the people who bring it from 5 to 20," both frame why founder-run Canadian businesses are systematically under-optimised and therefore buyable.

## Verdict
**Worth the read — top-tier Track A, and Canadian.** Three things to take: the exact language that beat higher bidders with a retiring owner, the case for issuing LOIs early and often as a filtering device, and the anatomy of a failed sale caused by owner-dependence — which is simultaneously the thing that makes a target cheap and the thing that will make *your* eventual exit hard.
