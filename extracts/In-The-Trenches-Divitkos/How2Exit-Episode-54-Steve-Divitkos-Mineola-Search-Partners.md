## Key numbers
- **~530 search funds raised since 1985** (originating at Harvard and Stanford GSB).
- **Average investor IRR: mid-30s%. Average multiple of capital: 5×.** He claims this outperforms public markets, private equity, private debt, infrastructure, real estate and venture capital. *(Stanford search fund study figures — verify the current edition before quoting.)*
- **Median age at the start of a search: 32**, with wide variance.
- **~15 investors** on the average cap table.
- **Average hold period: 5–7 years.**
- **His current investment mandate: $5–30M revenue, $1–5M EBITDA, ~40 employees** — with wide variance.
- **Typical search-fund target: $5–30M revenue (average $7–10M), $1–5M EBITDA (average ~$2M).**
- His own search: **18 months**, purchasing a software company in **late 2013**, exiting **late 2020**.

## What to look for — the search-fund buy box, stated explicitly
This is the clearest statement in the corpus of **what kind of business a first-time CEO should buy**, and it applies whether or not you raise a fund:

- **Owner succession** — "almost always... the founder CEO is looking to exit the business in some orderly way over an agreed-upon period."
- **Service and technology oriented**, more often **B2B than B2C**.
- **Asset-light** — "don't require a lot of capital to scale."
- **Recurring revenue** — "it lends itself quite well to a first-time CEO running it."
- **20+ years old, already profitable, already growing, established product-market fit, dozens of employees.**

**His summary framing is the one to remember:** *"You're looking for both a business model and an industry that lends itself very well to a first-time CEO."*

**Examples he gives of businesses that fit:** software, **alarm monitoring**, **staffing agencies**, managed service providers, B2B services, healthcare.
**The antithesis:** *"a mining company — that would be the antithesis of a search fund investment."*

**For Jay's thesis this is a useful test.** Janitorial and pest control **pass cleanly** — asset-light, recurring, B2B-heavy, sticky, and full of succession-driven sellers. Landscaping/snow **passes with caveats** (equipment intensity, extreme seasonality). Home services with heavy new-construction exposure **fails**, which is exactly what John Wilson argues independently in `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/4.5M-HVAC-Business-With-33-Margins-Is-It-a-Smart-Buy.md`.

## The industry thesis will probably change — and that's normal
> "Very frequently she does **not** buy a company in any of those industries."

Two reasons, both from his own search:
1. **The sheer number of industries you've never heard of.** *"I would get an offering memorandum and in many cases my first reaction was: oh my goodness, who knew that companies like this existed, who knew that this industry existed."* And after a decade in the space: **"Almost daily I learn about a company operating in an industry I barely knew existed."**
2. **Deep-diving one industry surfaces a better adjacent one.** *"Maybe the valuations are too high, or not enough companies are for sale, or there's a private equity firm consolidating the industry and you just can't win on price. And in the course of evaluating industry X you learn about an industry that's tangential to it — maybe a service provider to it, maybe they sell products into it — and you say, this is actually more attractive."*

**The practical implication for Jay:** hold the industry thesis loosely. The four target industries are a starting point, and the adjacent categories that surface during the search — **facility services, fire and life safety inspection, commercial laundry, waste and recycling, grounds and parking maintenance, restoration** — deserve the same evaluation. The *characteristics* (recurring, contracted, route-dense, succession-driven, asset-light) matter more than the label.

*(The host offers a nice illustration: a "roofing company" in Oklahoma that never touched a roof — it answered phones, bid jobs from satellite photos, and farmed the work to contractors. **A lead-generation business wearing a trade's name.** Always establish what a business actually does.)*

## Liquidity — more options than most buyers realise
He is explicit that an exit is not the only route:
- **Sale to a strategic or to private equity** — the most common.
- **Recapitalization** — changing the debt/equity mix after the original deal.
- **Share repurchases** — *"something that I did as a CEO. I bought out the interests of investors at a suitable return for them, and as a result myself and existing investors got a bigger share of the pie."*
- **Buying out every investor over time.** He describes a searcher who wanted to run his business forever and used company cash flow plus debt to do exactly that: **"what started as a cap table with the search entrepreneur and 15 investors turned into a cap table of one."**

**This matters for Jay because it reframes what a VTB or an equity partner actually costs.** Outside capital does not have to mean an eventual forced sale — it can be retired from cash flow. That's a genuinely useful negotiating position with a nervous vendor or a private investor.

## Zero-to-one vs one-to-N — the argument for buying rather than starting
> "Venture capital investing is like lottery ticket hunting... in a portfolio of ten, nine businesses return less than one times the investment but one returns 100 times. **With search funds it's very different, because the businesses we're buying tend to be 20+ years old, are almost always profitable, almost always growing, already have product-market fit, dozens of employees, recurring revenue.**"
>
> "**It's easier for me to take a business making $10 million in revenue with 300 customers and — if it has a really sticky product — raise the prices by 10%. I've just created value. That's a lot easier than trying to find my first customer with my first product.**"

The price-increase example is the concrete one, and it recurs throughout this corpus: **unraised prices are the single most common piece of found value in an owner-operated service business** (see `extracts/Cleaning-operators/2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions-Grow-Profit-Succeed-Ep.-38.md` and `extracts/Cleaning-operators/Independent-Contractor-vs-Employee-Cleaning-Business.md`).

## Stage-appropriate leadership — why these businesses come to market
The host proposes that different skills are needed at $0→$1, $1M→$10M, $10M→$25M and $25M→$100M. Divitkos agrees and says **it is the reason search funds exist at all**:

> "Founder CEOs who founded the business 20 years ago and are still running it — **oftentimes the realisation that leads to a sale in the first place is: I've taken this business as far as I can.** I'm incredibly good at taking a business from nothing to something, but now that we're at $10 million of revenue, a new set of eyes, a new set of skills, maybe a new infusion of capital is necessary to get us to the next inflection point."

And he applied the same logic to himself: after seven years, "either something new or something different is required as it relates to me — **which is one of the reasons that led to the sale of our company.**"

## What sellers want — the non-financial criteria, again
The father-and-son sellers of his software company had been **seeking liquidity for a couple of years without finding the right fit**:
- **They didn't want a strategic buyer** — *"they didn't want their business to effectively be swallowed up and become unrecognisable only a few years after the acquisition."*
- **They weren't excited about private equity**, "with all of the things that often tend to come with those types of acquisitions."

**A business can be for sale for years and still not have found its buyer, because the seller is screening on something other than price.** For a Canadian buyer facing thin deal flow, stale listings are worth revisiting on exactly these grounds.

## What an investor actually does — and the standard to hold them to
His list of what searchers ask investors for is a useful checklist of the decisions a solo buyer must make alone: what a good versus bad business looks like, industry selection, deal structuring, **how to keep the seller with skin in the game**, the mix of equity and debt, and **how to identify key-person risk in the seller and structure around it.**

One tactical role worth noting: **investors can be introduced to a seller to prove certainty of close.** *"Ability to close the deal is a variable that sellers consider very deeply. They don't want to go through the pain of diligence and legals only to find out the buyer doesn't have the wherewithal."* In a Canadian off-market deal, **a letter from your lender or a call from your capital partner is a real competitive advantage**, not a formality.

**His standard for a good investor:** *"the best investors are those that never imposed their will on a searcher — as active or as passive as the entrepreneur wanted or needed them to be."*

## Canadian-specific
- **Divitkos is Canadian** and invests across North America, though this interview is US-framed. The Canadian implications:
  - **His buy box maps well onto Canadian succession-driven service businesses**, which is the structural opportunity described in `extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`. The constraint is size: **his $5–30M revenue band is above where most Canadian janitorial, landscaping and pest control businesses sit.** A Canadian buyer will typically be looking well below the formal search-fund range — which means less institutional capital available and less competition.
  - **The formal search fund structure is far less established in Canada.** Business-school ETA programmes, investor networks and specialist counsel all exist here but are thin (he hedges elsewhere: counsel experienced in search "at least in Canada"). **Expect to self-fund, use a VTB, or raise from local private investors** rather than a 15-investor search cap table.
  - **Mineola explicitly invests in self-funded searchers and equity gaps**, not only traditional search funds — a named Canadian-founded source of gap equity worth knowing about.
  - **The share-repurchase / buy-out-your-investors path is fully available in Canada** and pairs well with a holdco structure (see the Canadian holdco note in `extracts/Owned-and-Operated/Grow-and-Operate-a-Holding-Company-w-John-Wilson-MI320.md`).

## Verdict
**The most substantially overlapping video in the corpus** — a third telling of a biography already covered twice — but it contributes three things the others don't, and they're worth having.

**First, the buy box, stated as a list.** Owner succession, service or technology, B2B, asset-light, recurring revenue, 20+ years old, already profitable. **Run any Canadian target against it.** Janitorial and pest control pass; construction-exposed home services fail; landscaping/snow passes with equipment and seasonality caveats.

**Second, permission for the thesis to change.** Most searchers do not buy in the industries they set out to buy in, for two good reasons — the world contains more industries than anyone knows, and evaluating one surfaces better adjacent ones. **Jay should hold the four target industries as characteristics rather than categories.**

**Third, the liquidity menu.** An exit is one option among several; **buying out investors from cash flow until the cap table is just you** is a legitimate end state, and knowing that changes how you negotiate with vendors and private investors from the start.

The asset-class return figures (mid-30s IRR, 5× MOC across 530 funds) are the strongest available argument that this path works — but they describe **institutionally-backed searchers buying $5–30M-revenue businesses**, which is not what a solo Canadian buyer of a $1–3M service business is doing. **Take the buy box and the discipline; don't take the returns as a forecast.**
