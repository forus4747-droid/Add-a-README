## Key numbers
- **Fewer than 10% of small businesses have formalized succession plans.**
- **The most common succession outcome is no succession at all — the company winds down.**
- Target zone for entrepreneurship-through-acquisition: **$5–30M revenue, $1–5M cash flow** — "too large to be purchased by a single individual, but too small to be purchased by private equity."
- Seller demographic: **owners aged 55–70**; the "silver tsunami," described as the largest transfer of wealth in recorded economic history.
- Small businesses across North America **employ 90% of the workforce** (his figure; verify — Canadian SMEs employ roughly 88% of private-sector workers per ISED, so the order of magnitude holds).
- His own journey: **2 years searching + 7 years as CEO = 9 years**, bought at 27.
- Search fund mechanics: raise a small pool from **~15–20 investors/institutions** to fund a **two-year search**; those funders get **first right of refusal** on the equity of whatever company you buy.
- Value created over the hold: management team **2 people → 7**; finance and accounting department **0 → 4**; verticals served **5 → 1**.

## What to look for
**Green flags — the seller-fit profile, which is the most actionable content here**

The company he bought was a 20-year-old software business owned by a father (70s) and son (mid-late 40s), with no succession path. What won him the deal was **not price**:
- The founders were **"not just looking for a check"** and **"not looking to go to a beach the day after closing."**
- They wanted to **look into the whites of the eyes of the person who was going to run their company.**
- They liked that he was a **portfolio of one** — all his time and attention on their business, no fund with a dozen other assets.
- He committed to **keep the company name**, **retain the employees**, be **growth-oriented**, and **continue the local charity sponsorships** that mattered to them.

He framed himself as **"a succession plan looking for the right small business to succeed."** For Jay, buying a Canadian owner-operated service business from a tired boomer, that is close to a script.

**Red flags**
- **You cannot diligence culture from outside.** "I learned more in my first two weeks as a CEO than I did in the six months of due diligence that preceded my purchase." Owners restrict access to leadership and staff for legitimate confidentiality reasons — "if the deal falls apart on the 99 yard line, what happens there?" — so accept that **~90% of what there is to learn about culture, you learn after closing.** Price that uncertainty; don't pretend to eliminate it.
- **Doing too much for too many.** "Trying to do too much and being too many things to too many people is one of the most common flaws that I see in small businesses."
- **Founder-centric operations.** A two-person management team means the business *is* the founder.

**The value-creation checklist he actually executed (a post-close playbook, not a theory):**
1. **Focus** — narrowed from five industry verticals to one. "The returns to focus exceed the returns to diversification."
2. **Convert revenue to recurring** — from a one-time perpetual licence to a monthly subscription that renews annually. *(This is exactly the pest-control and janitorial thesis in a different industry — see `extracts/Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`.)*
3. **De-founder the business** — management team 2 → 7, so the CEO became less important to daily operations.
4. **Build the internal disciplines that didn't exist** — finance/accounting, customer success, QA, all from zero.
5. **Invest in systems** — CRM, ticketing, QA tooling. The acquirer valued this as **"corporate hygiene that we're not going to have to spend time and money on."**

## Canadian-specific
- **Divitkos is Canadian, bought and ran a Canadian company, and now invests in Canadian search.** This is genuine Tier 1 Canadian material.
- His explicit view: **"as a Canadian I say this all the time, I think Canada needs more entrepreneurs and a much more robust entrepreneurial ecosystem."** The Canadian search-fund/ETA ecosystem is thinner than the US one — which cuts both ways for Jay: less institutional capital available, but also less competition for the boomer-owned businesses.
- The succession gap is if anything **more acute in Canada** than the US given the boomer share of business ownership; the CFIB has repeatedly put the share of Canadian owners planning to exit within a decade near or above 75%. Verify the current figure before quoting it.
- Practical implication: in a market with few professional buyers, **seller fit is the buyer's edge.** A Canadian owner in a small market who cares about their name, staff and town has almost nobody to sell to who will honour that. Jay can win deals on non-price terms that a US searcher would have to outbid for.

## Verdict
**One of the highest-value Track A videos in the corpus**, and the best statement of *why the opportunity exists at all*: a demographic wall of untransferred businesses, almost no formal planning, and sellers who care about outcomes money can't buy. Three things to carry forward.

**First, the pitch.** "A succession plan looking for the right small business to succeed," backed by concrete commitments — keep the name, keep the staff, keep the sponsorships, no portfolio, full attention. Write this down; it is how deals get won without paying the top price.

**Second, the honest limit of diligence.** Culture is 90% unknowable pre-close. That is an argument for a **larger VTB and a longer seller transition**, not for more diligence weeks — structure your protection into the deal rather than pretending you can inspect your way out of the risk.

**Third, the post-close playbook** — focus, recurring revenue, de-founder, build the missing functions, buy systems. Note that his was a software company, so verify the shape (not the logic) against a service business; but every one of the five moves maps directly onto a janitorial or pest control book.

The honest counterweight, which he supplies himself: **this is not the easy path.** "Rolls uphill... a bad decision maybe leads to a layoff, maybe leads to an employee who can't make their mortgage payment next month, and that's a heavy weight." His support structure — therapist, business coach, peer group (EO/YPO), and a mentor-investor who is *not* on your board — is the most practical burnout-prevention advice in the corpus, and it belongs in the plan from day one, not after the first hard year.
