## Key numbers
**The asset class**
- Search funds have existed since **1985**; roughly **500** started and finished by the time of recording.
- **Average investor IRR: mid-30s%. Multiple of capital: above 5×.** He claims this makes search funds more performant than private equity, private debt, infrastructure, public markets and venture capital. *(His figures, from the Stanford search fund studies — verify the current edition before quoting.)*
- Typical structure: **~15 investors** (high-net-worth individuals and institutions) fund a **two-year search** with a couple hundred thousand dollars, in exchange for **right of first refusal on the equity** of whatever company is acquired.
- **Searcher equity: ~⅓ granted at closing, ⅓ time-vested over ~4 years, ⅓ tied to the net IRR delivered to investors.**

**His deal**
- **Search took 1 year 8 months** (ecosystem average **21–24 months**).
- **Funnel:** hundreds to thousands of businesses reviewed at a cursory level → **4–8 IOIs** → **1–3 LOIs** → one exclusivity, one close. He notes he was unusually selective about issuing LOIs.
- **Capital structure: ~60% equity / 40% debt.**
- **Equity value quadrupled in the first five years.** Held seven years; exited **September 2020**.

**The target's quality metrics — the profile worth memorising**
- **~350 customers, none more than 5% of revenue.**
- **Under 5% annual customer churn** — "that provided me with a really valuable buffer, such that I knew I didn't have to recreate the revenue wheel every year."
- Recurring revenue, long operating history, already profitable, already generating cash, straightforward operating model, sticky product.

**Value created over the hold**
| Function | At entry | At exit |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing | **0 people** | ~15 combined with sales |
| Sales | 3 people | (as above) |
| Management team | **0** | **7** |
| Departments built from scratch | — | sales, marketing, finance & accounting, product management, customer success |

## What to look for
**Why investors back a first-time CEO — and what it implies about which businesses to buy**

His mother's objection is the best framing of the question: *"You're asking people to give you money and you've never managed as much as a lemonade stand. Nobody has ever reported to you, including an administrative assistant. You don't know what company you're going to buy, you don't even know what industry."*

The answer is that **investors are underwriting the business type as much as the person.** Search investors target businesses that "lend themselves rather well to a first-time CEO": simple operating model, recurring revenue, sticky products, low churn, long operating history, already profitable.

**That is the acquisition screen, stated from the investor's side.** For Jay, buying without institutional backing, it matters more, not less: **buy the business that forgives a first-time operator's mistakes.**

**"Enough right with it and enough wrong with it" — the best single formulation of a buy box in the corpus**
> "It had to have **enough right with it** to know that I could go in and make first-time CEO mistakes and not risk the survival of the business. But it had to have **enough wrong with it** such that I could actually create value over a five to seven year time period."

A business with nothing wrong with it is priced accordingly and offers no upside. A business with too much wrong with it will not survive your learning curve. The target sits between.

**The founder-wearing-four-hats problem, quantified**
> "The founder was happily wearing three or four hats that would otherwise need to be occupied by three or four other individuals — because he had 30 years of experience in the industry. He could simultaneously be head of marketing, head of sales, head of product management and head of development. **But when that founder exits, you need to replace those work streams with singular individuals.**"

**This is the single most important cost adjustment in small-business valuation.** The seller's SDE looks high precisely because one person is doing four jobs. Budget the replacement hires before you price the deal — see the same diagnosis applied to a home services business in `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/4.5M-HVAC-Business-With-33-Margins-Is-It-a-Smart-Buy.md`.

**The bank retrade — a red flag with a specific defence**
Two weeks before closing, the bank he had gone exclusive with returned from credit committee having **completely changed the terms.** When he compared the new sheet against the offers he'd declined 30–60 days earlier, **the bank he had rejected now had the better deal.** He called them in a panic and asked whether they could close in two weeks. They did.

**Two lessons.** First: **keep your losing lenders warm.** He had term sheets from six banks; that breadth saved the deal. Second, his general observation:
> "In the average deal there's probably **one to four instances in which the deal feels like it's about to fall apart** — and if you don't have that in closing a deal, either something's wrong or you're very lucky."

**Diligence has a hard ceiling — and the thesis will probably be wrong**
> "Even if you run the world's best due diligence process, I can almost promise you that **within your first month of running your company you will learn more about it than in the 12 months of due diligence that preceded it.**"

His illustration is remarkable and worth the whole video. Going in, the thesis was to **diversify away** from transportation and logistics — cyclical, low margin, slow moving, too many eggs in one basket. **Within 18 months they did the exact opposite: fired over 25% of the customer base and focused the entire company on transportation logistics exclusively.**
> "That was a scary decision to make. That also was probably the best decision we made as a board and as operators over seven years of ownership."

**Corollary: don't over-commit to your investment thesis in the LOI or to your investors.** Buy a good business, then find out what it actually needs. And note the direction of travel — as in `extracts/In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Succession-Focus-and-Why-Small-Business-Is-Hard-with-Steve-Divitkos.md`, **the value came from narrowing focus, not broadening it.**

**The growth-without-sales-and-marketing signal**
"The company was growing pretty substantially in the absence of internal sales and marketing. So we said: if it's growing at X% in the absence of both of these things, imagine how fast it could be growing if we actually built them." **That is a genuine, checkable diligence green flag** — but he's honest that "building these things from scratch is almost inevitably harder than every investment memo would have you believe." Multiple executive-level hiring mistakes, then more mistakes by those executives at the individual-contributor level.

## Canadian-specific
- **Divitkos is Canadian and this was a Canadian deal.** He notes he "was fortunate enough to pair myself with counsel who was very experienced in the search fund space, **at least in Canada**" — the hedge is telling, and it's the practical Canadian point: **specialist search/SMB acquisition counsel exists in Canada but is scarce.** Find them early; a general commercial lawyer will cost you time and terms.
- **Six banks, six term sheets** is achievable in Canada — the Big Five plus BDC plus credit unions — and given the retrade story, running a genuinely competitive lender process is not optional. See also Terry Lammers' "always talk to more than one bank" in `extracts/David-C-Barnett/Terry-Lammers-David-C-Barnett-Talking-to-Employees-About-the-Sale-of-a-Business.md`.
- **His 60/40 equity/debt split reflects a software business with no hard assets** — a shape Canadian lenders find difficult. For an asset-light Canadian service business, expect to need a similar or higher equity proportion, because CSBFP funds assets rather than goodwill. Hence the VTB emphasis throughout this knowledge base.
- **Canadian search capital is thin.** Divitkos founding Mineola to invest in Canadian search is itself evidence of the gap. For a self-funded Canadian buyer, the relevant point is that he **explicitly invests in self-funded searchers and equity gaps**, not only traditional two-year search funds — that is a real, named source of Canadian gap equity.

## Verdict
**Essential Track A material and the most complete single account in the corpus of what buying, running and selling a small business actually involves** — from someone Canadian, with a real seven-year hold and a quadrupled equity value.

Five things to carry forward:

1. **The buy box: "enough right with it and enough wrong with it."** Paired with his target's quality metrics — **350 customers, none over 5% of revenue, under 5% annual churn** — that is a usable screen for a Canadian janitorial or pest control book. Both numbers are checkable in diligence and both should be asked for by name.
2. **Price in the replacement of the founder's four hats.** This is where most first-time buyers overpay: the SDE they're capitalising includes three or four salaries that aren't being paid.
3. **Run a competitive lender process and keep the losers warm.** The retrade two weeks before closing is a routine event, not bad luck — and having five other banks in reserve is what makes it survivable.
4. **Expect the thesis to be wrong.** He planned to diversify away from his biggest vertical and instead fired a quarter of the customer base to double down on it. Buy a good business; decide what to do with it from the inside.
5. **Get the support structure on day one.** His stated **biggest regret is not hiring a CEO coach.** He joined EO in year two and calls it very valuable, but spent his early years "trying to impress my board rather than genuinely utilising them," and believing a CEO has to have all the answers. Combined with the four pillars he names elsewhere — therapist, coach, peer group, mentor-investor off your board — this is the same conclusion the burnout material reaches from the opposite direction (`extracts/Operator-burnout/Making-50-000-Per-Month-And-I-Still-Almost-Quit-My-Business.md`).

The emotional register is worth noting too, because it recurs across every serious operator in this corpus: **imposter syndrome, loneliness that hiring does not fix, and a personal toll that "eventually I came to pay."** The two-year sensation of "running somebody else's company" is specific to acquisition rather than founding, and is worth expecting rather than being surprised by.
