## Key numbers (for context only)
- The deal reviewed: **$2.5M trailing-twelve-month revenue, ~$3M run rate, ~$100K TTM profit, 18 employees, 1,000–10,000 customers, asking $30M** — i.e. **~12× revenue or ~300× earnings.**
- **SaaS comps Divitkos supplies:** the average **public** SaaS company traded at ~**15× revenue** — "but the average public SaaS company has **$600 million** of revenue, not $3 million." The average **private SaaS M&A transaction in North America in 2021: ~7× revenue.**
- **His own valuation framing when searching:** he thought in terms of roughly **6× cash flow.**
- **LTV:CAC rule of thumb: 3×.** And the formula for customer lifetime: **1 ÷ gross logo attrition rate.**
- **Only 9% of these businesses have formalised succession plans** (his figure here; he says "fewer than 10%" elsewhere).

## What to look for — the screening discipline, which is the real content
**Time is the searcher's scarcest asset.** *"When I was searching for a business to buy, that was my full-time job for almost two years. Time was not only my most finite asset but also my most valuable asset — I had to be **ruthlessly efficient** with it."*

**The two biggest time-wasters he identifies:**
1. **Sellers who were never going to sell.** *"It's common that business owners want their ego stroked, or they want a free valuation, and they were never going to sell to you in the first place."*
2. **Valuation expectations that can never be bridged.**

**His fix: get value expectations on the table in the first conversation.**
> "If I thought a business was worth roughly **six times cash flow** but the owner thought it was worth **six times revenue** — the delta between those is so large that with certainty it's never going to be bridged. So because time is your most finite asset, **you move on. You don't even bother with a second conversation.** But if I think it's worth six times cash flow and the owner thinks eight times cash flow, that's at least worth another conversation."
>
> "**The question I wanted to answer with every conversation is: is this worth my time on the next conversation?**"

**Then ask how they arrived at the number — the most human insight in the corpus:**
> "I would often ask the owner *how'd you arrive at your number*, and they would say to me, kind of stone-faced: **'Well, that's just what I need to retire.'** Guys like us might chuckle at that, but for a selling business owner, from their perspective, **that's a perfectly reasonable justification for valuation.**"

Girdley's shorthand for it: **"business is worth my mortgage balance."** *(This is exactly the situation Chris Mondragon walks into in `extracts/UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month-in-the-Cleaning-Business.md`, where a successful operator believed his $1.4M-revenue business was worth $3–4M because he thought valuation was a multiple of gross.)*

**And the crucial follow-on: it cuts both ways.** *"If a software business wants to sell for $10 million but they're generating $10 million of ARR — hey, that's a great buying opportunity. But if this owner wants $30 million to retire and she's generating three million... it's a pretty quick deal killer."* **An owner's retirement number can be below fair value as easily as above it. Ask.**

**Unsophisticated advisors are a problem, not an opportunity** — a genuinely counter-intuitive point:
> "There's a very common misconception that having an unsophisticated buyer and/or seller is unambiguously a good thing. **That's actually wrong.** Unsophisticated business brokers or investment banks might list a company that's worth two times revenue for ten times revenue — that makes the deal very hard to get done. **Unsophisticated legal counsel takes a purchase and sale agreement that should take a month and turns it into eight months**, because they're not familiar with what market terms should be. **Anytime you see an unsophisticated advisor, that's more of a challenge than an opportunity.**"

*(This is the same lesson Bill D'Alessandro learned the expensive way with a seller's estate-attorney brother in `extracts/Acquisitions-Anonymous/2M-of-EBITDA-and-50-margins-in-disaster-remediation-AA-194.md` — and it's why Mike Loftis recommends paying for the seller's advice if necessary.)*

## The diligence checklist he applies — and it generalises
1. **Situate the asking price in context.** Compare against public comps *and* private transaction comps, and adjust for size. A headline multiple "in line with public comps" is misleading when the public comps are 200× bigger.
2. **Is the revenue truly recurring?** *"In some instances companies try to position revenue as recurring, but when you get deep down into it it's actually **reoccurring** revenue. There's a very important difference — **the former typically justifies a much higher valuation than the latter.**"* **This is the single most transferable line for Jay's industries:** a pest control book of quarterly-plan subscribers is *recurring*; a book of customers who happen to call every spring is *reoccurring*. See `extracts/Pest-control-operators/How-to-Transition-from-One-Off-Pest-Control-to-a-Recurring-Revenue-Model.md`.
3. **Consistency of growth through cycles.** "Have they been able to grow revenue through economic cycles?" With a business too young for 2008, he'd look at 2020 as the stress test. *(For a Canadian service business, the equivalents are 2008–09, 2020, and the 2022–23 inflation and labour-cost shock.)*
4. **Customer concentration.** Even with a thousand customers — "I'd want to check that out anyway."
5. **Customer satisfaction data** (NPS in software; in a service business, complaint logs, review counts and renewal rates).
6. **Key-person risk, stated precisely:** *"What you often see in really small companies is that **customer relationships are proprietary to a business owner who's been running it for 20 years**, and/or it's the business owner who is responsible for a large majority of new customer acquisitions. **If that's the case there's huge key-man risk.** You want to make sure that new sales and existing customer relationships are not proprietary to the seller or a small number of team members."*
7. **Unit economics — but with heavy caveats.** LTV:CAC is "by no means a perfect metric and there's a lot of grey area." Many small businesses genuinely don't know either number; young companies can't know customer lifetime because they haven't existed long enough. **"Anytime a listing says LTV:CAC of five — look into specifically how that's been calculated, because there are many ways this number can be massaged."**
8. **Expect messy financials.** *"For companies of this size it's not at all uncommon to have really messy financial statements... when acquiring SMBs it's rarely as easy as the formula might suggest."*

## The quality test: mission-critical vs discretionary
> "Me and every other investor in the world like to look for businesses that sell a **mission-critical product that is either very time-consuming, very disruptive, or very expensive to get rid of** — often mission-critical software operating in the back-end plumbing of the business. **This is not a mission-critical buy. This strikes me as a discretionary expense**, likely more cyclically exposed... when their customers are suffering financially, that probably manifests in a higher than normal attrition rate."

And the closing advice, aimed squarely at first-time buyers:
> "**It's a lot easier and a lot less expensive to simply keep a customer than to acquire a new one.** Particularly if you're a first-time operator, you want a business that has much stickier products and much stickier revenue streams than this one likely does."

**Applied to Jay's industries, the mission-critical test ranks them usefully:**
- **Pest control on a recurring plan** — near-mission-critical for restaurants, food processing and healthcare (regulatory requirement); discretionary for residential.
- **Commercial janitorial** — mission-critical for medical, data centres and food service; closer to discretionary for a small office.
- **Snow and ice management** — genuinely mission-critical (liability, access, occupiers' duty).
- **Landscape maintenance** — the most discretionary of the four, and the first thing a commercial property manager cuts.

## Canadian-specific
- **Divitkos is Canadian and searched in Canada**, though this episode is about US-listed SaaS deals. Four translations:
  1. **The "what I need to retire" valuation is the defining feature of the Canadian small-business market**, where fewer than 10% of owners have a formal succession plan and most have never had a business valued. **Ask the number in call one, then ask how they got there.** It saves months.
  2. **Unsophisticated advisors are a bigger risk in Canada**, where specialist SMB M&A counsel and brokers are thinner outside the major centres. Budget for it — and consider funding the seller's advice, as Loftis and D'Alessandro both ended up doing.
  3. **Recurring versus reoccurring is the core valuation question in Jay's target industries.** A contracted, auto-billed janitorial book deserves a materially higher multiple than an equivalent revenue figure made of ad-hoc callouts. **Demand the contract file and the payment-method file, not the revenue summary.**
  4. **"Are the customer relationships proprietary to the owner?"** In a Canadian small-town service business the answer is usually *yes* — which is not disqualifying (Bill D'Alessandro: at $1–2M of EBITDA it's "table stakes"), but must be **structured around** with a long transition, a VTB with retention linkage, and early introductions to every significant customer.

## Verdict
**Off-thesis deals, but the best material in the corpus on the single question that wastes more of a buyer's time than any other: what does the seller think it's worth, and why.**

Three things to operationalise:

1. **Put valuation on the table in the first conversation, and screen ruthlessly.** The only question each call needs to answer is whether there should be another one. A gap between 6× cash flow and 8× cash flow is a negotiation; a gap between 6× cash flow and 6× revenue is a goodbye.
2. **Ask how they arrived at the number.** "What I need to retire" is not irrational from the seller's chair — and it is as likely to be *below* fair value as above it. It also tells you exactly what to solve for, which is the same lever Dan Drake used to get a better-than-asked deal in `extracts/Acquiring-Minds/Buying-a-Business-to-Build-a-Life-Dan-Drake-Interview.md`.
3. **Separate recurring from reoccurring revenue before you apply any multiple.** In janitorial, landscaping and pest control this distinction *is* the valuation, and sellers will not make it for you.

Add to the diligence checklist: **mission-critical or discretionary?** and **are new sales and customer relationships proprietary to the owner?** Both are answerable early, cheaply, and determine whether the rest of the work is worth doing.
