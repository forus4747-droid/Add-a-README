# QUESTIONS — what this corpus could not answer

Everything below is a **known gap, an unverified claim, or a contradiction between sources.**
Nothing here is settled. Treat each item as a question to put to an advisor, a lender or a seller —
not as a finding.

Three kinds of item, marked:

- ⚠️ **Verify** — a specific factual claim in the corpus that is doubtful or out of date
- 🇨🇦 **Canada gap** — the corpus answers this for the US and not for Canada
- 🔍 **Corpus gap** — no source covers it at all, or the sources disagree

---

## 1. Claims in the corpus that need checking before Jay relies on them

### 1.1 ⚠️ "VTB interest is taxed at the capital gains rate"

A Canadian M&A advisor states on camera that vendor-note interest "is taxed at the capital gains
rate… not the income tax rate as if you were getting paid a bonus or a salary," and builds a
seller-persuasion argument on it.
[`David-C-Barnett/Seller-Financing-To-Buy-A-Business-Vendor-Notes-in-M-A-Explained.md`]

**This looks wrong.** Interest income is ordinarily fully taxable in Canada, not a capital gain.
What *can* be spread is the **capital gain on the sale price** via the capital gains reserve when
proceeds arrive over several years — a different mechanism producing a somewhat similar
"stretch it out" benefit, which may be what the advisor is compressing.

**Question for a Canadian tax accountant:** for a retiring owner taking a five-year VTB, what is the
actual tax treatment of (a) the interest, and (b) the principal instalments? **Do not use this
argument in a negotiation until it is confirmed.** Getting it wrong in front of a seller's accountant
would cost Jay the credibility that Lesson 3 says the whole deal runs on.

### 1.2 ⚠️ The 66.67% capital gains inclusion rate

Some 2024-era Canadian tax content in the corpus reflects the proposed increase in the capital gains
inclusion rate to 66.67% above a threshold. **That proposal was subsequently abandoned.** Any
after-tax proceeds arithmetic in a source recorded in that window is unreliable.

**Question:** confirm the inclusion rate, the LCGE limit and the indexation currently in force for
the year Jay actually transacts. **Assume every tax number in this corpus is stale.**

### 1.3 ⚠️ CSBFP limits and eligibility

The programme's caps changed in 2022 (adding the $150K intangibles/working-capital class) and the
figures in this corpus come from secondary commentary rather than from ISED.

**Question for a CSBFP-active lender, not a website:** on a real target of Jay's size, what is
actually financeable, what is excluded, how is goodwill treated, and what does the 25% personal
guarantee cap mean in that lender's own documents?

### 1.4 ⚠️ Production rates in janitorial: sources contradict each other

The corpus gives production rates from **1,000 to 4,000 sq ft per hour** — a four-fold spread that
changes any bid by a factor of four. See §1 of [`OPERATORS.md`](OPERATORS.md).

The disagreement is probably real (building type, finish, fixture density, scope) rather than one
source being wrong. But it means **no production rate in this corpus can be applied to a specific
building sight-unseen**, and therefore neither can any seller's.

**Question for a target:** produce the actual hours clocked per site for the last twelve months, and
divide by the square footage yourself. Do not accept a rate; derive it.

## 2. 🇨🇦 Where the corpus is American and Canada is different

The corpus is roughly **80% US-sourced.** These are the places where that matters most and where
the corpus provides no Canadian answer.

### 2.1 Successor rights and employment continuity in an asset purchase

Flagged repeatedly in [`OPERATORS.md`](OPERATORS.md) §1 as the most under-appreciated Canadian
difference for a janitorial buyer — Ontario's building-services provisions in particular — but **no
source in the corpus is Canadian on this point.** Everything written here is a flag to check, not a
statement of law.

**Questions for an employment lawyer in the target's province:**
- On an **asset** purchase of a cleaning company, do the employees carry accrued service, notice and
  severance entitlements to Jay?
- Do Ontario's building-services successor provisions bind a new contractor at a specific premises,
  and how does that interact with buying the *company* rather than winning the *contract*?
- Does the answer differ between Ontario, Quebec, Alberta and BC?

### 2.2 Worker classification exposure

The corpus contains one contractor-vs-employee discussion, and it is US-framed. CRA applies its own
multi-factor control test and the exposure — retroactive CPP, EI, penalties, interest — **follows a
share purchase.**

**Questions:** how does Jay size this exposure during diligence on a target using contractors? Is it
insurable? Can it be carved out by indemnity, and is that indemnity worth anything from an
individual seller who has spent the proceeds?

### 2.3 Experience rating and WSIB/WCB on a change of ownership

Named in the glossary as an asset that may not survive an asset sale. **The corpus does not answer
whether it does.** Provincial, and worth real money on a labour-heavy target.

**Question for each relevant provincial board:** does the experience rating transfer on (a) a share
sale, (b) an asset sale?

### 2.4 Pay-when-paid enforceability under Canadian prompt-payment law

The single best cash-flow-trap document in the corpus is a US operator reading a US national's
subcontract aloud.
[`Landscaping-snow-operators/Snow-Contracts-EXPOSED-Read-THIS-Before-You-Sign.md`]
Ontario's Construction Act and comparable legislation elsewhere may make some of those clauses
unenforceable — **or may not apply to snow and landscape maintenance at all**, which is the actual
question.

**Question for a Canadian commercial lawyer:** does prompt-payment legislation reach snow and grounds
maintenance subcontracting in Ontario? In Alberta? If not, is pay-when-paid simply enforceable?

### 2.5 Cosmetic pesticide restrictions and the 70–80% fertilization margin

The most-cited margin figure in the landscaping chapter is a US number, and fertilization is exactly
the service line Ontario and Quebec restrict.

**Question:** what is actually applicable, by whom, under what licence, in the target's province —
and what does that do to the 70–80% margin the whole "buy fertilization revenue" thesis rests on?

### 2.6 BDC's actual current products

Named as the layer above CSBFP on the strength of general knowledge, not on the strength of any
source in this corpus. **Verify directly.**

### 2.7 Provincial pest control licensing

Flagged in [`OPERATORS.md`](OPERATORS.md) §2 that licences attach to *individuals*, not companies —
which would make an exiting owner-operator with the licence a genuine deal-breaker rather than a
transition problem. **This needs provincial confirmation.** If it is true, it is the most important
single fact about buying a small Canadian pest control business.

### 2.8 Canadian broker quality and where deals actually come from

The corpus has a good taxonomy of bad brokers (the shopkeeper, the dentist, the clown) but nothing
on **where a Canadian buyer actually finds deals** — how much flows through business brokers, how
much through accountants and lawyers, how much must be sourced directly. Direct outreach is the
consensus answer in US content; whether the Canadian market is thin enough to change that is unknown.

## 3. 🔍 What no source in this corpus covers

### 3.1 The five-month dead season, from someone who has lived it

The corpus has excellent snow *pricing* content and excellent landscaping *margin* content, all from
US operators in comparable climates. What it does not have is a **Canadian operator describing how a
grounds-maintenance business survives November to April** — what the payroll decision actually is,
who gets laid off and who gets carried, what the operating line does in February, and what the
business is worth to a buyer who has to fund that gap in year one.

**This is the most important missing item in the corpus for Jay's stated thesis.**

### 3.2 Post-close reality in the first ninety days

Plenty of sources on buying; very few on the first quarter of ownership. The day-two problem is named
repeatedly but described concretely almost nowhere.

### 3.3 Failed acquisitions

Survivorship bias runs through the whole corpus. There is one genuinely excellent post-mortem
[`Acquiring-Minds/Buying-Then-Losing-a-2.1m-HVAC-Business-Dan-Burnside-Interview.md`] and one
five-landmine failure story — against dozens of success narratives. **Assume the base rate of failure
is materially higher than this corpus implies.**

### 3.4 Buying a business with a partner or a spouse

One source mentions a partnership breaking down between twin brothers. Nothing covers how to
structure one, what the shareholder agreement needs, or how to exit it.

### 3.5 Immigration, TFW and labour supply in Canadian service trades

Labour supply is identified in [`OPERATORS.md`](OPERATORS.md) §5 as *the* binding constraint across
every industry chapter. The Canadian dimension of that — TFW streams, LMIA, provincial nominee
programmes, and what a target's compliance history looks like — is **completely absent.**

### 3.6 Insurance, bonding and the cost of being a new corporate owner

Bonding history is named as something an asset sale destroys. What that actually costs Jay in year
one, and whether it can be rebuilt, is not covered.

### 3.7 What a Canadian bank actually says no to

The corpus explains that Canadian banks dislike certain industries and that a large VTB is the
workaround. It never gets a Canadian credit officer on record about what fails and why.

## 4. Known shortfalls of this build

Stated plainly rather than buried.

| Target | Achieved | Why |
|--------|----------|-----|
| ≥150 videos | **61** | YouTube is blocked at the network layer in this environment; every transcript was fetched one at a time through a single MCP tool rather than by `yt-dlp` in bulk. The long-form-only rule then removed 21 already-collected videos. |
| ≥50 Track B rows | **30** | Same throughput ceiling. Track B was prioritised in the second half of the run, which is why the split is near-even (31/30) rather than Track-A-heavy. |
| ≥8 distinct videos per industry chapter | **3 of 4** | Cleaning 15, landscaping/snow 9, home services 8 — **pest control 5.** Only three dedicated pest-control videos exist in the corpus, plus two crossover teardowns. The chapter was left honest rather than padded with sources that do not actually discuss pest control. |
| ≥15 distinct videos in `KNOWLEDGE.md` | **40** ✅ | |
| Every `done` row has transcript + extract | **61/61** ✅ | |

**What would fix the gaps:** running `./run.sh` on a machine with direct YouTube access and
`yt-dlp` installed. The pipeline is resumable — `manifest.csv` carries the state, and a re-run picks
up where this one stopped rather than re-fetching. The pest-control gap specifically needs
roughly six more dedicated operator videos before that chapter deserves the same confidence as the
cleaning chapter.

## 5. The five questions to answer first

If Jay only chases five of the above, these are the five that change what he buys:

1. **Do employees and their accrued entitlements come with an asset purchase in the target's
   province?** (§2.1) — changes the price of every janitorial deal.
2. **Do pest control licences attach to the individual?** (§2.7) — may disqualify an entire industry
   from an owner-operator purchase.
3. **What is actually CSBFP-financeable on a real target?** (§1.3) — sets the ceiling on what Jay
   can buy at all.
4. **How does a Canadian grounds-maintenance business fund November to April?** (§3.1) — the
   corpus's largest blind spot on Jay's own thesis.
5. **What is the real tax treatment of a VTB for the seller?** (§1.1) — the lever Jay will use in
   every negotiation, currently resting on a claim that looks wrong.
