Patrick Moore with Ironclad Wealth Management. Today we're going to talk about structuring, specifically what's the difference between an asset sale and a stock sale, because understanding this will inform you as to how much you're going to pay in taxes as well as which strategies may be available to you. Why does it matter? Your structure impacts your taxes, bottom line. When you're selling a business you're on the opposite end of the aisle from the buyer.

You are really not in a position to take advice from them. Generally buyers prefer an asset sale and sellers prefer a stock sale. Depending on the give and take of a negotiation it may actually influence the sales price and several other factors in the deal. In an asset sale the buyer purchases the assets. Say you're doing toy manufacturing — you've got your company and inside the company you've got a factory, your supplies, your equipment.

In an asset sale the buyer isn't buying the company, they're buying those assets from the company and then putting them in their own company. In that scenario you actually hold on to your old LLC or C Corp. The buyer gets a fresh start and full depreciation benefits on the tax side, and — probably the most important part for most buyers — they don't assume the old liabilities that you incurred when you were running the company, because they're in that new corporate shell. A stock sale is different because the buyer is buying your whole company. They are just paying you for the stock of that company, and because of that they're assuming the liabilities.

For you as a seller it's much simpler and you receive capital gains tax treatment on the sale, whereas in the asset sale you could have a mixture of taxes. In an asset sale you're selling your property, your equipment, your supplies, and all of those can have different tax treatment. Some can have ordinary income, some can have depreciation recapture — if you've written things off in the past, that means you don't have any basis in it and you have to pay taxes on the full amount. That's all agreed upon in the sale: how much is each of those things worth, that's how much of the sales price is associated with each asset, which then informs what your taxes are. Goodwill — value not associated with an asset — you do get capital gains for that in an asset sale, but it's typically not going to be the full amount of the transaction, which means you might wind up paying more than the capital gains rate.

In a stock sale you get capital gains treatment on the stock of your business based on how long you've owned the business. Because the top ordinary income bracket is 37% and the top capital gains bracket is 20%, it means that typically people like to save that 17%, and a stock sale is preferred by a seller. Things can get complicated at the state level. Capital gains and ordinary income in most states are similar — same rate applied to both categories — but there are exceptions. If you're in Georgia you're probably going to prefer a stock sale because it doesn't make a difference at state level so you save that money on the federal side.

But if you lived in Washington State, they actually have no ordinary income tax and a 7% tax on capital gains over a certain threshold. So depending on the parameters of the sale, it may be worth a little less fight trying to get that stock sale — if the difference is only 2 or 3% instead of 17, maybe you can afford to give a little of that and get some more back on the purchase price. This informs your negotiation. The buyer side: if you do an asset sale you can get depreciation on the fixed assets. It's like you've bought them new.

If it's something that has a 5-year life you can depreciate it over five years or use bonus depreciation if it's eligible. That gives you extra oomph in those early years for tax deductions, which means you might wind up paying less in taxes and have higher cash flow in those early years where you may be hurting for cash flow. Whereas in a stock sale you have to amortize your purchase price over 15 years. Compared to depreciating a lot of stuff in year one, it really stretches those benefits — so an asset sale is worth a lot more to you as a buyer. There are ways to do a stock sale and have it treated as an asset sale.

It's called the section 338(h)(10) election. You need a tax attorney to look at that one. For the seller it's from a tax perspective treated as an asset sale, the buyer gets to take that depreciation, and you're still just buying the stock of the company. It's a middle ground where both parties can benefit, but because there are negative tax aspects for the seller there may need to be some adjustments to the purchase price. Example one — a service business selling for $10 million, no basis.

In the asset sale, what assets are there? It's a service-based business, all they have is some office equipment, all been depreciated. It's section 1245 equipment which means it's going to be recaptured at ordinary income rates — $100,000 at ordinary income rates. The remainder is goodwill, which is $9.9 million, and that's all taxed at capital gains even in an asset sale. That results in $2.017 million in taxes.

In the stock sale they're just buying $10 million of stock; the difference is only 17% of $100,000, which is another $17,000. So obviously in a vacuum you'd prefer to keep $17,000, but why would you fight in a transaction over that? You can afford to say okay, what do you need to do, and then fight over other deal parameters. Example two — contrast with the manufacturing business. They've got inventory, accounts receivable, equipment and real estate, goodwill, stock.

All of that equipment, accounts receivable, and inventory is subject to depreciation recapture and ordinary income. Applying a 37% ordinary tax rate to those and 20% capital gains: whereas in a stock sale the seller would have paid $2 million in taxes, in an asset sale they would have paid $3.19 million. There is a major difference in how they're going to be taxed — that's a million dollars less that they would have. So if somebody is going to insist on an asset sale, maybe you need to negotiate for a higher sales price. Summary: if somebody comes to you to buy your business, they are directly at odds with you.

They're not trying to help you. They're trying to get the best price and they don't care about your taxes. You care about your taxes. It's not their responsibility to know about them. You need to set up the deal in such a way that you're minimizing your taxes.

Start planning early before you start negotiating. Sit down with your accountant and don't skimp on a couple of hours of work with them to say: what would I owe in an asset versus stock sale? Structure that negotiation with taxes in mind, but remember the deal should be evaluated in totality — use those taxes as a negotiating lever and don't focus on them to the detriment of other areas of the negotiation, because I'd rather make more money and then pay more taxes if I wind up in a net better place. It's a lot better to do this before you have an LOI signed, and really the best case is over a year before you're looking at a sale.
