I got a question from a viewer who said what if I don't have enough money to buy a business and I really want the seller to stick around for the long term.

So I thought it would be a great question to lead into the topic of staged buyouts. So if we have a business that is, you know, not the smallest of businesses, it's big enough that it has a few employees, there might be day-to-day jobs for both the buyer and the seller in this business. So I'm thinking about a business maybe that has like 10 or a dozen or more employees, right, something like a small car dealership. Okay, so the buyer comes in and uses what money he has to buy a small percentage of the company, let's say a 5% stake in the company. And so years go by and every year if the company is profitable there's a dividend issued, and of course the buyer in the summer they collect a salary for the work that they do every day.

And so they collect a dividend every year, but the dividend money that would normally go to the minority shareholder in fact doesn't go into his hands, it gets used to buy more shares from the seller. And so as the years go by, little by little, the buyer ends up buying more and more shares. And what this does is a couple of things. Number one, it allows the buyer to become tutored and mentored by the seller over the course of time, so they've really become informed about how the business operates. Number two, it allows the seller to get a really great value for their business, because normally when we start these kinds of deals we work out a buy-sell agreement that has a formula for value — how do we value the shares in the agreement.

So if the company continues to grow and prosper and be profitable, the value of the company is going to increase, meaning that the seller is going to get an increasingly higher price for the shares of the company. But more importantly, this seller now has guaranteed an exit strategy, which is one of the risky things about business. If you build up a business and you run it day to day and something happens — and I've talked a lot about the top five reasons people would come to me to sell a business, for those five things we're not planned for — we now have a situation where we've got our exit locked in, we know who the buyer is going to be. So if an unplanned personal crisis were to occur we could accelerate the plan, right. And if everything goes according to schedule and the idea is retirement, then we just stay on the schedule and have the buyer buy the shares.

Now in a traditional model for this kind of staged buyout, what would typically happen is the buyer would get up to about 49 percent of the shares and then have to buy the balance of the shares in one shot in order to buy out the seller. And what that would mean is going to the bank and borrowing money. Now by this time the buyer has been in the business for years, they've been running the business for years, and if there have been dividends issued it means that the business is being run profitably. Do you think that the banker would like to make a loan to this person to buy the rest of the company? Of course they would, right.

And so this kind of planned methodical exit strategy can work really well, but it requires a couple of things. Number one, it requires a real business with organization and structure so that people know what they're supposed to be doing and people have job descriptions, etc, so that the buyer and seller aren't tripping over each other all the time. And number two, it requires good business discipline in that there will be a profit every year that can be issued in the form of dividends to help the minority partner buy out more of an interest in the majority partner. So we're talking about profitable real businesses that are well-run that want to have a properly executed and planned exit strategy.
