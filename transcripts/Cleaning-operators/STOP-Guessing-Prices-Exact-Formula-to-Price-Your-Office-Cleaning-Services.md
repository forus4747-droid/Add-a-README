On this video I'm going to show you how to price commercial cleaning contracts, and also how to price post-construction jobs and move-out cleans. Since 2017 I grew my own commercial cleaning business, Mastery Cleaning, to over $6 million in revenue. There are two ways of pricing a commercial cleaning contract: based on the square footage, or based by the hour and how long it would take to clean. I personally like to price my contracts by the hour, because there can be a 10,000 square foot office and another one the same size, but one has a lot of traffic, a lot of cubicles, it gets dirty very often. So one contract could take 6 hours while the other only takes 3 to 4 hours.

We cannot charge the same just because they're the same size. When it comes to medical facilities, factories, or heavy-duty manufacturing facilities — places that get dirty, or places that require very OCD cleaning such as medical — those types of contracts will take longer. When we do a walkthrough we estimate one labor hour is going to cover anywhere between 1,000 to 3,000 square feet of cleaning per hour, and we price our contracts anywhere between $35 an hour to $100 per hour depending on the size. It's all about the volume and the type of client. When it comes to restaurants or retail they tend to have thinner margins so they're going to be a little more concerned about the price.

But most clients are not concerned about the price — they want to pick the best cleaning company that's going to provide a good service stress-free without any quality problems. Why $35 to $100? It comes down to the frequency and estimated time per visit, because we want to make it worth the drive. If there's a small contract once a week that takes 2 hours to clean, we'd charge about $60 to $70 an hour, because it's not that often and not that many hours per visit. A contract like that would be anywhere between $500 to $700 a month.

If there's a big contract Monday to Friday, 8 hours per visit, we'd charge about $35 an hour because it's a lot of hours — and for a contract like that I'd price anywhere between $5,000 to $6,000 per month. How do we come up with the total price for a recurring commercial contract? Estimate how long it's going to take per visit, then times it by the weekly frequency. If there's a contract that takes 3 hours per visit, three times a week — let's say we charge $150 per visit, which comes to about $50 per hour — this contract will pay about $450 per week. From here we have two options: invoice the client every four weeks or monthly.

I personally recommend charging every four weeks because you get to invoice the client 13 times a year. And when you tell people you're going to invoice them every four weeks they automatically think it's monthly, but it's not. So when you're giving the price to the client it will seem slightly smaller. If they require you to invoice every month, you take your weekly total times 4.33, because some months have four weeks, other months have five weeks, so on average a month has 4.33 weeks. For this contract we're pricing $450 per week.

Invoiced every 4 weeks it's $1,800, which comes to about $23,400 every year. But if we had to invoice the client every month we'd take the $450 times 4.33 which comes to $1,948, and times 12 that comes to $23,382 — the same yearly total. But instead of telling them $1,948, we tell them $1,800 invoiced every four weeks. By doing that it's going to help you make more money in your cleaning business and it should slightly improve the closing rate. With all the inflation happening I charge a little bit more.

If you're doing the entire business yourself sometimes you may be forced to charge $25 to $30 an hour, and if you hire minimum wage workers you can make a little. But me personally, I never go below $35 an hour because I pay my cleaners very good. During the closing process I tell the client why they shouldn't pick the cheapest one. I tell them what happens when you pick the cheapest provider: you're going to get unreliable workers, minimum wage workers, there's going to be a new cleaner every other week, the quality will not be consistent, it will not be safe for your facility, the company may hire illegal workers which you may be liable for in case they fall. So we tell them that picking the cheapest quote will not lead to a long-term happy outcome.

Then I tell them we have very good cleaners, we pay them good, we require at least 5 years experience, we have inspectors and a regional manager, so before any problems come up we will fix them without them even noticing. Whenever you're pricing a commercial contract you need to estimate how much profit you're going to make, and you have to ask yourself: how much would I have to pay someone to keep a reliable cleaner here happy with the pay so they're going to be here long-term? Then you calculate how much profit you're going to make for this contract. Here's how much I would pay a subcontractor. I personally like to hire subcontractors because there's no deductions off their pay.

I only pay them once a month, 10 days after they send me the invoice, so that allows me to get paid first and then I pay the subcontractors — so I'm always cash flow positive. If there's a contract that only takes 1 hour per visit I'd pay the subcontractor anywhere between $35 to $50 for that visit, because it needs to be worth the drive; usually I pay around $20 to $25 an hour, but when it's only 1 hour we have to make sure it's worth the drive for them. If it takes 2 hours per visit I'd pay them anywhere between $50 to $75 per visit. If it takes 3 hours I'd pay anywhere between $80 to $100 per visit. If it takes 4 hours I'd pay about $100.

If you have a walkthrough and you're concerned about how much to price and you don't have a mentor by your side, what you can do is call three different competitors in your city, pretend that you have an office, and tell them "we have an office, 3 hours per clean, three times a week" and ask how much their estimated quote would be. They're going to push for a walkthrough, but just try to get the price on the phone call. For all these contracts always aim for a 40% profit minimum. One-time cleaning jobs: we simply want to make $50 per hour minimum, for 5 hours minimum, just so it's worth the drive for our cleaners.

If someone calls me and says "I have an office that's 15,000 sq ft and I need a deep clean", I estimate how long it would take. If it's normal cleaning it's about 1,500 sq ft per labor hour; if it's a deep clean it covers about 1,000 square ft per labor hour. Deep clean, post-construction and post-rental would be the premium. When it comes to one-time jobs, a way to save yourself is to limit how many hours you can be at that job, and if it takes longer you're going to have to charge an additional $50 per labor hour, so that if the project takes longer than expected you're covered.

Post-construction: there are two kinds. One type is a one-time project like a big building finishing up. The other type is a builder who every month builds eight to 10 houses which need to be cleaned. For the builder making multiple houses, we clean the same house twice. Every month I'd send the total hours to the client and I'd simply charge $37 per hour, so every single month the invoice was different. By doing the same house twice it provided the best level of quality, because when it comes to post-construction the first clean doesn't usually cover everything — there's going to be inspectors walking in, touch-ups happening, dirt being tossed around, and the house cannot be ready for someone to move in under that condition.
