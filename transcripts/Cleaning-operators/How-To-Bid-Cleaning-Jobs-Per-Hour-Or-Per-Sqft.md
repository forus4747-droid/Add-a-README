When you're bidding on commercial cleaning contracts, aka janitorial contracts, should you bid per square foot or by the hour? I'm AJ Simmons, founder of the CleanBids Network. I've seen people pricing janitorial cleaning for like two cents per square foot all the way up to 20 cents per square foot. Here's why I do not like pricing per square foot: what are you basing that off? If you just pull a certain cents per square foot, where are you getting that from?

And the other thing is this: let's say you've got a 5,000 square foot building and you want to charge 10 cents per square foot to clean it. That's $500 per visit. What if you're going to that building five times a week? Now you're at $2,500 a week. Then you multiply that by 4.33 weeks in a month and you're talking about 10 grand a month just to clean a 5,000 square foot building.

I don't really like that method. Here's the method I do like — by the hour. Say you've got a 5,000 square foot building. How long should it take? The way you figure out how long it's going to take to clean is you use what's called production rates.

Say the average production rate for you to clean a regular office is 3,000 square feet per hour. So you're talking about two hours to clean at night. Then multiply that two hours by your hourly rate. What is your hourly rate? The way I like to come up with the hourly rate is simply take whatever you're going to pay somebody else to do it and multiply that by two.

So let's say you were paying people fifteen dollars an hour to do it, then we'll charge the customer thirty dollars an hour. So that's two hours a night times thirty dollars an hour — you're left with sixty dollars per visit. And that's a drastic difference from that five hundred dollars, isn't it? Again, if you're in a building that's 5,000 square feet — one building could be all hard floors, need lots of mopping, they might have some glass in there, they might have tons of cubicles, and it can take you four hours to clean it. But if you have another building 5,000 square feet, very light offices, only maybe three employees working there, then you can knock that building out maybe in an hour, hour and a half.

So you really can't bid by the square foot when it comes to commercial cleaning. Now I do bid by the square foot — cents per square foot — when it comes to special service jobs, one-time jobs such as stripping and waxing, carpet cleaning, pressure washing, things like that, and I just go off the industry standard. But typically when it comes to commercial cleaning I like to go by the hourly rate. Carpeted areas are usually faster than hard floor areas, because when you have a hard floor area you're going to have to go over it twice — you've got to vacuum it then you've got to turn around and mop it too. With carpet you're only going over once, so that makes carpeted areas faster.

Worked example done live: a four-hour job for two people. That's eight man hours. I'm going to pay them both fifteen dollars an hour, so that'd be thirty dollars an hour billed. Eight man hours times thirty dollars an hour, that's $240 a visit. Going three times a week, that's $720 a week, times 4.33 weeks in a month, and there's my price.

We can do that instantly, and you know that you're going to get a profit because the math has already worked into it — all you did was double whatever you're going to pay your employees plus overhead. If you just try to charge per square foot you're basically just winging it and you're hoping that you make a profit, and I don't like that. If you're not experienced enough to walk into a building and know how long it's going to take to clean, use a production rate calculator. Plug in your square footage, it has a production rate field (we used 3,000 earlier), and it'll tell you it's going to take 1.67 hours to clean it. You can add in TUMS — toilets, urinals, mirrors and sinks — for the bathrooms and it'll add that into the equation.

And you can separate the carpet area from the hard floor area. So: charge per hour, but you do need the square footage in order to know how long it's going to take to clean, unless you're extremely experienced and can look at a building and know off the top of your head. Most people newer in the game can't eyeball it, and even if you try you're going to get it wrong — you might under or over bid it.
