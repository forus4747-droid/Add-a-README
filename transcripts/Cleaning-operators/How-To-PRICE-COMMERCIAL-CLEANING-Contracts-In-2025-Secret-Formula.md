There are two ways of pricing commercial contracts: by square footage or by the hour. I prefer to price by the hour, because the same 10,000 square foot contract can take double the time in one office compared to another that doesn't get that much traffic. One job could take 6 hours and another job could take 3 hours. So I price by the hour so we are pricing these commercial contracts accurately. We price these contracts anywhere between $30 to $100 per hour.

If it's a small contract, one time a week, 2 hours per visit, we charge around $60 to $100 per hour to make it worth the drive. But if there's a big contract that's 8 hours a visit Monday to Friday, then we charge anywhere between $30 to $34 an hour. For the smaller contracts we need to make it worth the drive — just by showing up we need to charge a premium. Even if it's a half hour job we have to consider the driving distance, how much we're going to pay the reliable cleaner that's going to be there happy on a long-term basis, and also allow us to have a healthy profit margin. It's all about profit, it's all about what you take home.

I prefer to use subcontractors and I like to pay them a premium rate so I don't have to worry about these contracts. You can get the job done a lot cheaper if you're hiring payroll workers, but they're going to require much more management compared to using a subcontractor. You show them the place and they know exactly what they're doing with very little management. Whenever you're doing the walkthrough you want to take notes of how long it's going to take for every single section of the building. Usually most clients know the square footage of their office, so ask them.

One labor hour can cover anywhere between 1,000 square ft to 2,500 square ft. If there's a lot of cubicles very close to each other and lots of traffic, that's going to be about one labor hour per 1,000 square ft. If it's a big open space area with only floors, everything's very spread out, then you can go towards 2,500 square ft per labor hour. We also like to use Google Maps to analyze the size of each building — use the measuring tool. Example: a building measuring 195 ft by 49 ft is about 9,500 square ft.

Assume the whole building has offices and meeting rooms, no factory. Estimate about one labor hour covers about 1,500 square ft, so that's about six and a half labor hours to clean this entire building. We want a cushion, so add about 20-30% more time — times it by 1.2 — giving about 7.5 to 8 labor hours. Let's say we charge $35 an hour, so that's about $267 per visit. How often are we cleaning this building per week?

Let's assume three times a week. That's about $800 per week for that contract. What I like to do is charge every four weeks. That contract would be about $3,200 every four weeks, and when we charge every four weeks we get to invoice them 13 times a year, so this contract would be about $41,700 per year. Keep this contract for five years, that's about $288,000 — almost a quarter million just with one cleaning contract.

Most corporate buildings are expecting net 30 payment terms, meaning they have 30 days to pay after you send the invoice. Sometimes you can negotiate net 10, net 15; sometimes with smaller contracts you can even get paid up front, but for bigger corporate contracts the standard is net 30. A way to combat this is to simply send the invoice right away — you don't have to send the invoice at the end of the month. Then the subcontractor sends us their invoice at the end of the month and we pay them 10 days later, so the contractor is on net 10 terms. The goal is to get paid first and then we pay the subcontractor, so we're always cash flow positive.

I like to charge every four weeks because it makes the invoice seem a lot smaller. In the past I lost multi-six figures by charging every month. By charging every four weeks you get to be a lot more consistent with the invoicing and it appears like a more attractive number. If you invoice once a month you need to times your weekly total by 4.33, because some months have five weeks and some have four; on average a month has 4.33 weeks. So invoicing monthly you'd invoice almost $3,500 compared to $3,200.

Remember the first time always takes double the time, but once you build a schedule everything becomes a lot easier. Whenever we're giving the final number to the client we always like to round down a little bit — so if it's 3,000 we're going to do 2,995; if the total price is 3,500 we do 3,495. Psychologically this number will look a lot better. Sample pricing per visit: a 1-hour contract per visit we charge about $80 to $100 per visit. 2 hours, about $115 per visit.

3 hours, about $150 per visit. 4 hours per visit, about $170. 5 hours, about $200. Anything over 6 hours per visit we charge $35 per hour. That is a rough estimate; it can change based on which specific city you live in, and with inflation these prices will rise.

Depending on where you are — Australia, Canada, the UK, US — usually we look at the minimum wage in our specific city and times that by three. So if the minimum wage is $15 we always want to aim for a $45 average rate. Before we give the final price to the client we estimate our expenses. Cleaning supplies are about less than $500 per contract depending on which vacuum you buy, but the ongoing cleaning supplies are very cheap — like one or two percent of the contract. Floor solution or disinfectant, you buy a big gallon for $10-20 and that lasts you a while.

Usually we tell the subcontractor to restock those cleaning supplies and they can cover that, or if they're being annoying about it we tell them give me the receipt and I'll add it to the next payment. Always estimate how much you have to pay a subcontractor to keep them there happy on a long-term basis. Make sure they live near the contract — that helps a lot with retention. If it's only a one or two hour contract we have to pay them a premium rate just to get them to drive out there; if it's lots of hours that's when we can go lower on the hourly rate. We always want to aim for a 30 to 50% profit margin.

Remember 95% of your costs is going to be labor, unless you're doing marketing which is a whole different category. For every commercial contract the most expensive thing is going to be labor. We use subcontractors because we pay them a fixed rate without any deductions, meaning the cleaner gets paid a lot more at the end of the day because they're not being deducted with payroll taxes and income taxes, so they're going to be a lot happier. We pay them once a month; we get their invoice and write that off as a business expense. One final tip: if you're doing a walkthrough for a twice a week contract, always suggest to the client more frequencies.

Why not three times a week? If it's one time a week, why not two times a week? If it's bi-weekly, why not once a week? Whenever we're submitting proposals we send two separate proposals — one for the original estimate and the other for a suggested higher-frequency estimate, with a little discount.
