In this video I'm going to give you the exact same bidding formula that I use to win highly profitable commercial cleaning contracts. I have successfully used this exact formula to build my personal commercial cleaning company beyond seven figures in less than 5 years. Three things we have to know before we start getting that price: you need to know the length of time it will take to clean the building, you need to know your true hourly cost — not just what you pay somebody — and you need to know which profit margin to target for this account. To really know how long it's going to take you or your crew to clean a facility you've got to know the size, the density and the traffic. Size: get the square footage of the facility.

Density: how many offices, exam rooms, bathrooms and toilets in those bathrooms are inside that facility, because some facilities have very open areas while others are crammed full of exam rooms and offices and cubicles. Another factor with density, which you get at your walkthrough, is how frequently each of those spaces are cleaned. If you're cleaning a large office building then maybe each of the desk areas only needs a serious cleaning once per week with just a daily check, as opposed to a medical facility where it's a full clean everywhere every day. Traffic: ask your prospect how many visitors or patients or customers they have per day. You can look at the parking spaces while you're there, or from Google Maps.

A string of facilities all under one roof with four to six parking spaces per door is very different from a facility with one building, one customer, and a lot of parking spaces — a lot of traffic for that space. That gives you an idea of how dirty it's going to be. Production rate example: for the office range, 3,000 to 4,000 square feet cleaned per hour. The upper end — the fastest rate of 4,000 per hour — would mean the density is probably on the lower end and the traffic is on the lower end. The slower cleaning speeds are because there are more desks, more offices, maybe we need to do more than just once a week at each desk, and more traffic coming into the facility.

Take the total facility size and divide it by the rate per hour for your facility type. Is this an exact science? No — this method helps you get a good idea and a good range and a good starting place. Now find your actual labor cost. The formula: your average market rate, plus 10%, and then you add on your payroll liabilities.

To find average pay for a cleaner in your market, go on Indeed and look at what other companies are offering. There's also a company called PayScale, costs about $100 last time we used them, that pulls together market reports and shows you the average pay in your market — high, low, medians. In my operation our average market rate here in Asheville, North Carolina is about $13 per hour for people going out and cleaning buildings in the evenings. We add 10% on top of that, so it's $1.30 per hour, which gives us $14.30. Why are we adding that 10%?

Because we want to attract good people — we don't want to play at the bottom of the barrel. That's not the end of it. You have payroll taxes, state unemployment insurance, federal unemployment insurance, workers comp insurance — all these other costs because you have employees. That's what's called your payroll liabilities. For us that means adding 14.11% to our base labor costs.

When we do that our true labor cost really shows that it's $16.32 per hour. Now figure out how much profit to charge. The technical term is gross profit, also known as gross margin — how much money is left over after paying cleaners and your staff, so that it can go to paying for your office, your salary, overhead, marketing. We're not talking about net profit. Our markup chart: the hours listed are weekly hours.

The bigger the account, the longer the cleanings take per week on average, the lower the margin. Why? Smaller accounts take almost just as much management to take care of as larger accounts. If there's a $10,000 a month account and a $500 a month account, is the $10,000 per month account going to take 20 times the amount of management effort? Absolutely not.

Both may receive a once a month inspection; there might be some extra emails and coordination with the $10,000 account, but the extra time, resources and energy is not 20 times. This is economies of scale, and larger facilities know this. If you go into a large facility and bid it at a profit margin of a small facility, the odds of you winning it are going to be very low. To get your profitable hourly rate: take your true hourly cost — ours was $16.32 — divided by one minus the profit margin you selected. Example: an account at the 35% profitability range, which is above 66 hours per week in our market.

$16.32 divided by (1 - 35%) = $16.32 divided by 65% = $25.11 per hour. This is now the hourly rate that we charge to get that 35% gross profit margin into the account. Final formula: efficient cleaning time (how long the facility takes) times your hourly rate that has profitability baked into it, multiplied by how many times per week the cleaning is completed, multiplied by 4.33 (how many weeks there are in one month). Alternatively multiply that whole top line by 52 for weeks per year then divide by 12. Either way you end up with a monthly price.

Why aren't supplies baked in here? Supplies typically run 2 to 3% and they're typically included in the consideration of that profitability markup. If you want to add a little bit you absolutely can. This is the exact bidding formula that I have used over and over again to win consistent large, medium and small commercial cleaning contracts, and built our business to multiple seven figures in less than 5 years.
