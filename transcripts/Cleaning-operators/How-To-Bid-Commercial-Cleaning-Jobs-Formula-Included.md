Hey cleaning family, it's Carolyn Arellano, your cleaning business mentor, and in this video I'm going to teach you how to price commercial cleaning jobs. First I'd like to start off saying that there is no wrong or right way to price a commercial cleaning job or contract. We all have different ways of doing things. Some companies quote by the square foot, some companies quote by the hour. I just happen to quote by the hour — that's how I was taught.

Some of the jobs that I do happen to price by square foot are typically specialty jobs: carpet jobs, tile jobs, VCT stripping and waxing. However with our routine janitorial and commercial cleaning we do price by the hour. There are three steps: walkthrough, bidding, and then sending the proposal. The very first part of quoting a commercial cleaning contract is doing the initial walkthrough. During that walkthrough you're going to gather all the information that you need — square footage, types of floors, the scope of work, the frequency that the client wants you there — and just really understand what it is that you're going to be tasked with.

Two very key pieces of information you need to quote by the hour. The first is your production rate. Your production rate is how long it actually takes you to clean certain items: how long does it take you to clean a toilet, how long does it take to clean a sink, how long does it take to vacuum carpet, what is your production rate with mopping. I also want to point out that if you're just starting out in the commercial cleaning space, you are going to underbid yourself. That is just inevitable.

And if you don't, you either had some assistance from someone experienced, or you did a really great job, or you guesstimated well. For the most part, including myself, you are probably going to underbid yourself, and that's okay — that is part of the learning process. Just start timing yourself. Start keeping track of how long it actually takes you to complete each task. There are different bidding apps available for commercial cleaning companies, and there is also the ISSA clean times, which can be found for the most part online with a little bit of research.

They also have a book that breaks down the exact clean times per task. I have purchased that book and although I do utilize it, I still go by how long I know or think it's going to take to clean compared to other accounts or other cleans that we have actually done in the past. The second piece of information is your hourly rate that you're going to charge your client. You want to make sure to include the cost of labor, consider the difficulty of the job, definitely consider your scope of work, and there are other expenses you can consider including such as the cost of transportation, any other fees, and equipment. After you have that number you're going to want to mark it up so that you can profit.

I've seen companies multiply this number anywhere from 20 to 50%. The mathematics: take your hourly rate and multiply that by the hours per visit, multiplied by the number of employees, multiplied by the number of days per week, times 52 for the amount of weeks in a year, and then divide that out by 12. That number is what you're charging your client. We also happen to charge our clients for the monthly supplies. What we do is take that number from the equation and multiply by 3% — that's about the average of what your monthly supply costs are going to be.

Now you have your base rate and your supply cost, add that up, and that is the total amount you are going to be charging your client on a monthly basis. Sample equation: our hourly rate is $25 an hour. $25 times hours per visit, which is four. Then multiply by the number of days — this is a 5 day a week account. Then multiply by 52 and divide by 12.

We have $2,166.66. That is our base rate. Now account for supplies: multiply by 3% and we have $64.98. Add that supply cost and the base rate, and that is what we present to our client for the monthly charges. Although we did do the math in total amount of man hours, you're not going to present it to your client like that.

You're not going to say "here's my proposal, it's $2,300 a month and that gets you x amount of man hours." We don't want to say that. What we just do is give them that base rate, we charge for the supplies, and we say this is what it's going to cost for the entire month — and of course you want to make sure to let them know how many visits.
