# How to qualify for the $1,016,836 lifetime capital gains exemption

The 2024 lifetime capital gains exemption is now over $1 million — to be exact it is $1,016,836. This applies to qualified small business corporation shares (QSBC), which basically means they are a Canadian controlled private corporation: a corporation that is a resident in Canada whose shares are not traded on a public stock exchange and which is not controlled by non-residents or a public company. That's starting point number one. Number two, you need to ensure it is a share sale, not an asset sale. A lot of business owners end up doing an asset sale and that would make you basically ineligible to use this lifetime capital gains exemption.

The sale of your company has to be a share sale. There's also what's called a 24-month holding test. What this means is the seller, or a person related to the seller, must be the only person to have held the shares in the 24 months leading up to the sale. So you can't issue new shares or buy new shares today and try to use the lifetime capital gains exemption tomorrow. There has to be a 2-year ownership period where you own the shares and then you're eligible to use the exemption.

Another test is the 50% test. In the 24 months prior to sale, more than 50% of the fair market value of the assets of the business must be used principally in the active business of the corporation carried on primarily in Canada. At least half of the assets in the corporation for 24 months prior to the sale has to be considered active business assets. It can't be passive income — you can't have stock portfolios or rental properties, basically assets that have nothing to do with the business, at more than 50% of your total assets. Then very importantly there's also a 90% test.

The 90% test means at the time of sale all or substantially all of the value of the corporation's assets must be used principally in the corporation's active business carried on primarily in Canada. At the point of sale 90% of the assets in the corporation have to be considered active assets. The CRA generally interprets "substantially all" as meaning 90% or more of the value of the corporation's assets. So if you have passive assets in the corporation they could throw you off. You need to look at ways of purifying your operating company, perhaps using a holding company to transfer assets into the holdco, which is something you'll own for the rest of your life, but you can still sell the opco and the shares of the opco.

Example: let's say you sell shares of a small business corporation which is eligible for the lifetime capital gains exemption for $1.5 million. Without the lifetime capital gains exemption you would have to pay taxes on half of this amount, so you're paying tax on $750,000. With the LCGE you can subtract the $1,016,836 from the sale and essentially only pay tax on (1.5 million minus the LCGE) times 50%, so you're only paying tax on about $241,582, not that $750,000. It's very costly to not qualify for it and very beneficial when you do qualify. Can you multiply the LCGE?

In other words, can other family members or partners use the LCGE? The answer is yes. With strategic planning you can utilize other family members' LCGE to help mitigate taxes on the sale of your Canadian controlled private corporation. You can implement for example a trust, and you can have multiple beneficiaries of the trust, and you can in turn use their LCGE to offset the sale of the business. So if you had say five family members and each had about a million dollars, you could in theory qualify to offset over $5 million of a sale of your corporation — if you're using every single family member's LCGE and obviously they haven't used it in the past so they have the full amount eligible.
