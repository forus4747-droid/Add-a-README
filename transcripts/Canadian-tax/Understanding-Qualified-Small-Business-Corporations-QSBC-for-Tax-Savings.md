# Understanding Qualified Small Business Corporations (QSBC) for Tax Savings

Today we are diving into the qualified small business corporation, or QSBC. In simple terms, QSBC shares allow you to access the lifetime capital gains exemption (LCGE), which can reduce or even eliminate the taxes you would pay on the capital gain when you sell those shares. But to qualify, a corporation and its shares need to meet certain conditions. There are three key conditions a share of a corporation must meet to be considered QSBC shares. Condition one — small business corporation.

At the time of sale the share must be part of a small business corporation, meaning 90% or more of the company's assets must be used in an active business primarily in Canada. This could include assets like real estate, equipment or even intellectual property. Condition two — holding period for shares. You or a related party, such as your spouse, common law partner, or a partnership of which you are a member, must have owned the shares for at least 24 months. Throughout this time the corporation must have been a Canadian controlled private corporation (CCPC) and at least 50% of its assets must have been used in an active business.

Condition three — ownership exclusivity. The shares must not have been owned by anyone other than you, your spouse or a related party throughout the 24 months before the sale. The QSBC 90% rule explained. At the time of sale, 90% or more of the corporation's assets need to be used primarily in an active business carried on in Canada. That includes things like your office space, equipment, inventory and any other business related assets you use to generate income.

But if your corporation is holding too many passive investments like stocks or extra real estate that is not used in your operations, you might not meet the rule. Example: imagine you own a small manufacturing business. You have assets like machines, tools and raw materials that are all used in the daily operations of the company. The building where your factory is located is also considered an active business asset. Together these assets make up 90% of your corporation's total assets, which means you would meet the 90% rule.

But let's say your corporation owns a separate building that you rent out to another company, or some stocks you are holding for investment purposes — these would be considered passive investments. If those passive investments start to make up more than 10% of your corporation's total assets, your business may not meet the 90% rule. The CRA's reason for the 90% rule: the purpose is to make sure that the tax benefits from the lifetime capital gains exemption go to true small businesses actively operating in Canada, not corporations holding too many passive investments. Special rules and exceptions. If the corporation issued shares to you or a related party after June 13th 1988, the CRA generally considers that an unrelated person owned the shares just before they were issued.

To meet the 24-month holding requirement the shares must have only been owned by you, a related party, or a partnership you were part of after they were issued. But there are exceptions: if the shares were issued as payment for other shares, as part of a stock dividend, or when certain assets used in the business were transferred to the corporation in exchange for shares. Example of special rules: imagine you own a bakery and you decided to incorporate it a few years ago. You transferred most of the bakery's assets like ovens, equipment and supplies into the corporation in exchange for shares. According to the CRA's special rules, since you received shares in exchange for your bakery assets, the 24-month holding period for QSBC shares begins right after you got those shares.

You still qualify for the holding period requirement because you transferred active business assets into the corporation. Exception rule example: let's say the bakery needed to raise more funds and instead of selling shares directly the corporation issued new shares to you as a stock dividend. Since the shares were issued as part of a stock dividend the usual rules do not apply — you can still meet the 24-month holding period rule even though these shares were just issued to you. The capital gains deduction. If you sell qualified QSBC shares and realize a capital gain you could claim the LCGE.

For the 2024 tax year this could be up to $1.25 million. That means if your gain falls within this limit you won't pay any tax on that amount.
