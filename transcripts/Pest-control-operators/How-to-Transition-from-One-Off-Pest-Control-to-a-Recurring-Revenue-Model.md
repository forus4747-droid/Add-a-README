# How to Transition from One-Off Pest Control to a Recurring Revenue Model

Most pest control business owners really love the summer and fall months where their phone is ringing off the hook, trucks are moving, cash is flowing. But when it hits winter and moves into the end of fall, a lot of the bugs go dormant and your phone stops ringing, and suddenly you're stressing out about how to cover your payroll and how to make those truck payments. This is the problem when you solely rely on one-off pest control services instead of building a recurring revenue machine. If your business relies entirely on people calling you only when they see a bug, you don't have a scalable business, you have a high-stress seasonal job. In today's day and age your company isn't valued based on how many trucks you have or how good you are at killing bugs — it's determined by one key metric, and that's monthly recurring revenue.

The first part of this process is understanding the math of lifetime value. Most owners have their pricing model focused on one-off services, maybe something like $250 a service. In this case it takes maybe an hour to service this customer, they're happy, and you go home. But there's a lot of hidden costs. This is where it becomes important to understand your customer acquisition cost, or CAC.

On top of that you need to factor in labor and materials. Every time you acquire a new one of these customers, you're going to have to spend that marketing effort just to replace that customer. If you look at a subscription model, you want to move into offering quarterly protection plans. It might vary depending on what you offer, but that could range from $45 a month up to $100 a month, or around $135 a quarter up to $300 a quarter. If you look at the low end of this, that could be bringing in an extra $540 a year.

Or if you look at total lifetime value, and say your average retention of a client is 3 years, that means each customer is going to bring in about $1,600 worth of lifetime value per customer on one of these quarterly prevention plans. But the cool thing is, in most cases your cost of acquisition is going to be exactly the same as one of those one-off services. So instead of bringing in $250 per transaction, you've actually built an asset that's worth over $1,600 in lifetime value. If you had 500 homeowners on this basic $45 a month plan, that's over $22,000 in recurring revenue hitting your bank account every single month before you even started sending out trucks for services. The second part is building out your package tiers.

You don't want to just sell pest control, you want to sell peace of mind. Build a tiered structure. A foundation package focused around exterior services — a barrier treatment around the outside of the home, getting rid of spider webs or wasp nests. The next level up might include a whole home protection plan: exclusion, helping to prevent mice and rodent issues, setting up traps — that tier might range from $60 a month. Then your ultimate plan, the premium package, includes all the other things but maybe also inside treatments for prevention, mosquito treatments, and more — that package could range from $75 to $100 or more a month.

One cool aspect: your foundation package is exterior only, and the great benefit is you don't need the homeowner to actually be there to perform these services. If you have a quarterly service that takes place around the exterior only, your technician can simply pull up to the house, do that treatment, clean off the spider webs, make sure there aren't any wasp issues, leave a sign in the yard, set up an automated follow-up process, and move on to the next house. This allows you to eliminate the challenges with scheduling with homeowners, avoid the hassle of dealing with no-shows or locked doors or homeowners not being home. The third part is having an effective script to convert one-off customers into recurring revenue. The easiest time to sell this type of package is immediately after you've solved a pest issue.

They're already excited that you've gotten rid of their problem, their guard is down, they know you produce results. You need to start training your technicians to stop thinking like laborers just providing a service and instead shift their mindset to start thinking like consultants. One transition that works well: after the service call, instead of charging them immediately for that one-off emergency visit, pitch them on getting started with your protection plan. For example, if you had an ant removal service, instead of charging that one-time $250, you move them into that $45 a month plan which covers getting rid of ants on the exterior of the home. Also let them know that this type of treatment only lasts for 90 days, so they'll need a quarterly plan to make sure their home is protected.

Then leave it up to them. They can either pay the full price of that one-time service, which is still a win, or get locked into the quarterly protection plan. As a bonus you might consider offering your technicians a commission structure where they get bonuses if they get someone started on a recurring revenue plan versus just the one-off services. The fourth step is to focus on building route density. When you're focused on only one-off jobs, your technicians are scattered around the city traveling from place to place, wasting a lot of time, effort, energy, fuel, and resources going from home to home.

Instead, if you focus on building route density, you can send a technician to a small area to service several homes at once in a short window. One great way to expand this is through door-to-door sales or close-knit outreach, where once you've tapped into a few homes in a local service area you can send out reps to expand that area by offering the protection plan to those in the neighborhood at a special discounted rate. This increases density within an area and saves your technicians time in the long run. Transitioning to this recurring revenue model is not an overnight process. It does take time.
