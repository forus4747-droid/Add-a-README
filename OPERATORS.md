# OPERATORS.md — What It's Actually Like to Run These Businesses

**Built from 61 long-form YouTube transcripts. Every number below is cited to the video it came from.**

This is the Track B half of the knowledge base: not how to *buy* a business, but what the person
you're buying it from actually does all day — the margins they really earn, the staff who don't show
up, the contracts they lose without ever being told why, and the reasons they eventually put it on
the market.

Read `KNOWLEDGE.md` for deal craft. Read this before you believe a single number in a listing.

**How to read the citations.** Every figure is followed by a source in `[brackets]` pointing to a
file in `extracts/`. Where operators disagree — and they frequently do — both numbers are shown.
**Disagreement is information: it usually means the two businesses are not the same business.**

**A standing caveat.** 59 of the 61 videos are American. Wage rates, payroll burden, tax treatment,
licensing and seasonality all differ in Canada, and each chapter ends with the Canadian adjustments
that matter. The *operating physics* — production rates, route density, turnover, how contracts are
won — transfer almost unchanged.

| **3,000 sq ft/hr** | Schools, including mopping and dust mopping | `Cleaning-operators/Filthy-Rich-Cleaners-E21-Breaking-into-Commercial-Cleaning-Cold-Calls-to-7-Figures.md` |
| **1,000–2,500 sq ft/hr** | Dense cubicles + high traffic = 1,000; open floors = 2,500 | `Cleaning-operators/How-To-PRICE-COMMERCIAL-CLEANING-Contracts-In-2025-Secret-Formula.md` |
| **1,000–3,000 sq ft/hr** | Normal ≈ 1,500; **deep clean drops to 1,000** | `Cleaning-operators/STOP-Guessing-Prices-Exact-Formula-to-Price-Your-Office-Cleaning-Services.md` |

**These do not agree, and that is the single most important fact in this chapter.** A 10,000 sq ft
office is 3.3 hours of work at one operator's rate and 10 hours at another's. The difference is
**scope, density and traffic** — not opinion. Anyone who prices from square footage alone without
walking the building is guessing.

> "If you bid a 10,000 sq ft office and it's just enclosed offices, that takes a lot quicker to clean
> than 10,000 sq ft in cubicles." — `Filthy-Rich-Cleaners-E21`

**Other time benchmarks:**
- **ISSA "3× rule" for bathrooms:** toilets + urinals + sinks, × 3 = minutes. 8 toilets + 2 urinals + 2 sinks = 36 minutes. [`Filthy-Rich-Cleaners-E21`]
- **A 2-sink / 2-mirror / 2-toilet restroom: 7 minutes** for a trained tech; 10–15 for a new one still reading the checklist. [`UpFlip/How-to-Make-1-917-Day-Doing-This-cleaning-business.md`]
- **Common areas run a quarter to a third of the whole facility.** [`Filthy-Rich-Cleaners-E21`]
- **The first clean of any building always takes double the time.** [`How-To-PRICE-COMMERCIAL-CLEANING...`]

### The cost stack

**True labour cost — the build every amateur skips:**

| Step | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Market wage for evening cleaners (Asheville NC) | **$13.00/hr** | `Cleaning-operators/Price-Commercial-Cleaning-RIGHT-Cleaning-Bid-Calculator.md` |
| **+10% deliberately, to be above market** | → $14.30/hr | `UpFlip/How-to-Make-1-917-Day-Doing-This-cleaning-business.md` |
| **+14.11% payroll burden** (payroll taxes, state + federal UI, workers' comp) | **→ $16.32/hr true cost** | `UpFlip/How-to-Make-1-917-Day-Doing-This-cleaning-business.md` |
| Bill at 35% gross margin | $16.32 ÷ 0.65 = **$25.11/hr** | `UpFlip/How-to-Make-1-917-Day-Doing-This-cleaning-business.md` |

**The gap between the wage and the true cost is 25.5%.** Bid off $13 believing you have a 35% margin
and your real margin is roughly 16%.

**Overhead is not a rounding error.** One operator allocates **$0.004/sq ft against $0.0067/sq ft of
labour — 37% of total cost.** [`Pricing-Commercial-Cleaning-Jobs-by-Sq.-Ft.`] His overhead list:
advertising, owner salary, insurance, office, vacation, admin, printing, Wi-Fi, computer, tools.

> "If not properly done this will poke a hole in your ship and you will start bleeding."

**Supplies are genuinely small:** 1–3% of contract value across three independent sources
[`How-To-PRICE-...`, `Price-Commercial-Cleaning-RIGHT...`, `UpFlip/How-to-Make-1-917-Day...`], with a
**<$500 per-contract startup cost** (mostly the vacuum). **Labour is ~95% of cost.** [`How-To-PRICE-...`]

## 1.2 Staffing playbook and turnover realities

**The recruiting funnel is brutal and nobody discloses it in a listing:**
- **100 applications → 1 hire.** "We would always be recruiting, always running interviews." [`10-Years-of-Cleaning-Business-Advice...`]
- **4,200+ applications → "less than half a percent"** tested and retained (~20 people). [`UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future.md`]
- **Post-offer melt is severe:** candidates accept and never show; others work two or three jobs then start arriving late, develop an attitude, or refuse feedback. [`10-Years-...`]
- **The two-day wall:** "We don't really suffer getting people in. **The problem is once they go in for a couple of days, that's when people realise maybe it's not for me.** House cleaning sounds easy — it's physical. After eight hours of cleaning you'll be like, *wow*." [`UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month...`]

**Hire for soft skills, not experience.** One operator recruited five-star-hotel housekeepers for their
technical skill; his best hire dismissed client feedback and became defensive. He reversed the policy:
attitude, punctuality and response to feedback over experience. [`10-Years-...`] A second operator
**refuses to hire from other cleaning companies at all** — "they have their own ways of doing things."
[`UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month...`]

**Train on tasks, not buildings.** The most valuable staffing idea in the chapter:
> "We train our techs based on **tasks** — how to clean windows, toilets, restrooms, the proper way to
> vacuum and mop. **Once they have these techniques down, they can apply it at any account.**"
> — `UpFlip/How-to-Make-1-917-Day-...`

Checklists reference the task ("glass"), so a tech who knows glass applies it to whatever glass exists.
**Site-specific training means every departure costs you an account. Task-based training makes staff
interchangeable — and that is a transferable asset you are buying.**

**Third-shift staffing is the structural problem.** Clients want the work done nights and weekends, so
you are staffing part-time, second-job employees. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]
> "As soon as it hits the fan, **which job are they not going to show up for? Yours.**"
> "You're pinning your whole reputation on the guy with the second side hustle."

**The systems answer, not the heroics answer** [`...173.md`]: GPS check-in within 15 minutes of the
scheduled time → automatic alert to a **dispatcher, not the owner** → call at 15 minutes → standby crew
dispatched at 30 → photo proof of completion. *"It is not hard to be unimpeachable here as having done
the job on time."*

**Retention mechanisms that demonstrably worked:**
- **Pay 10% above market deliberately.** "We don't want to play at the bottom of the barrel." [`Price-Commercial-Cleaning-RIGHT...`]
- **Work anniversaries, not Employee of the Month.** One operator lost **four consecutive Employee-of-the-Month winners** to resentment from colleagues before switching to tenure-based recognition — which nobody can dispute, and which then won him two seven-figure data-centre contracts when posted on LinkedIn. [`Cleaning-operators/Scaling-Your-Cleaning-Business-Boosting-Profits-Efficiency-in-Janitorial-Services-Ep.-149.md`]
- **Objective competency ladders for raises.** "You can't have a system where *Joe got a raise because Matt likes Joe*." [`2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions...`]
- **Hire subs who live near the contract** — "that helps a lot with retention." [`How-To-PRICE-...`]

**And the classification landmine.** One operator converted her entire W-2 workforce to 1099
subcontractors, doubling their nominal hourly rate while eliminating vehicles, storage units,
overtime, benefits and supplies — cutting overhead to **under $2,500/month on ~$500K revenue.**
[`Cleaning-operators/Independent-Contractor-vs-Employee-Cleaning-Business.md`] **Everything she describes as a control
system — mandated checklists, photo documentation, a published no-refund policy, withholding payment
for 24 hours, unpaid return visits — is evidence of control.** See §1.7 for the Canadian exposure.

## 1.3 How contracts are won and lost

**Won:**
- **Answer the phone.** The single most cited growth factor. "If you try to call the top 10 competitors in your city, **seven of them won't even pick up.**" Hard numbers: answering within a couple of rings → **50–60% close rate**; calling back a few minutes later → **16.5%.** [`UpFlip/This-Cleaning-Business-Makes-23-013.69...`]
- **Walk the building, and never price on site.** "As soon as you announce your price, they're going to judge you on price for the rest of the walkthrough. **They're not going to listen to a word you say.**" [`Filthy-Rich-Cleaners-E21`]
- **Ask the pain questions:** *What do you like best about your current cleaning service? What do you like least? If we could change one thing tomorrow, what would it be?* They always give three different answers. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]
- **Never criticise the incumbent's work.** He lost a bid saying "this cleaning company did a bad job on these floors" — the facilities manager replied, *"I did those floors."* [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]
- **Pick a niche.** He counted ~30 viable ones. Office buildings mean bidding against 10–20 companies in herded group walkthroughs; **churches and private schools have one or two bidders, some of whom never submit.** [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`] "Commercial is not a niche, it's an ocean." [`Why-Clients-Complain-...`]
- **Sell against the cheapest quote explicitly:** unreliable minimum-wage workers, a new cleaner every other week, inconsistent quality, facility security risk. [`STOP-Guessing-Prices-...`]
- **Promise imperfection.** "Our goal is not perfection — **if we never made a mistake you couldn't afford us.** I promise you, if you stick with me long enough I will make a mistake, and I'm going to fix it." Every competitor is promising flawlessness and none are believed. [`Why-Clients-Complain-...`]
- **Send two proposals** — the requested frequency and a higher one at a small discount. [`How-To-PRICE-...`]
- **Culture as marketing.** A data centre manager who had refused him for eight years reached out after seeing his staff-anniversary posts: *"We love the staff, we love them like family — **we just don't like the company that they work for.**"* The incumbent was **cheaper**. [`Cleaning-operators/Scaling-Your-Cleaning-Business-Boosting-Profits-Efficiency-in-Janitorial-Services-Ep.-149.md`]

**Lost — and this is the part that matters for diligence:**
> "A lot of times when clients come in from the weekend, they see their office — whether it was cleaned
> correctly, the trash changed, the toilet cleaned. **And a lot of times they don't let you know. They'll
> just kind of move on to another cleaning company.**" — `UpFlip/How-to-Make-1-917-Day-...`

**Commercial cleaning clients do not complain their way out. They leave.** Corroborated from the
opposite direction: *"No complaints are not good. I'd rather have someone complaining than someone who's
not — because they're complaining to someone. If they're not talking to you, **they're talking to your
competition.**"* [`2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions...`]

The incumbent in the data-centre story lost seven figures because **the crews had been out of gloves for
three months.** [`Cleaning-operators/Scaling-Your-Cleaning-Business-Boosting-Profits-Efficiency-in-Janitorial-Services-Ep.-149.md`]

**Sales cycle:** 2–3 months minimum for decent commercial accounts; **6–9 months for the good ones.**
"You're not selling for now. When — not if — they're unhappy with their cleaner, instead of going to
market they just call you." [`Losing-a-Major-Client-...`]

## 1.5 Why these businesses die

- **Underpricing at the start, structurally.** No margin → no money for marketing, recruitment, an office manager, or the owner → no growth, ever. [`10-Years-...`]
- **Revenue growing faster than margin.** Every added employee added a vehicle, insurance, fuel, tolls, overtime and a storage unit. Years 3 and 4 were bigger than years 1 and 2 **and paid the owner less.** [`Independent-Contractor-vs-Employee-...`]
- **Customer concentration.** The hard rule: **if any client exceeds 15–20% of revenue, start selling against it now.** The listener in that episode was losing a client representing **70% of revenue and staffing.** [`Losing-a-Major-Client-...`] The Walmart pattern: year one you quadruple revenue on thin margins; year two *"they know they own your company"* and dictate a 10% cut — and you can't walk, because you hired the people and rented the space.
- **One-time work as a rescue plan.** Post-construction and move-outs are *easy to get precisely because nobody good wants them*: you front all labour and materials, there's no credit check, a thousand contract clauses excuse non-payment, and *"we need 300 hours this week and then no work for six weeks."* — **"I've never met a multi-million-dollar post-construction business."** [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]
- **Deferred price increases.** Five years unraised becomes an increase no board will approve. [`2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions...`]
- **Being a generalist.** Residential + commercial + windows + carpets = no pricing power in any of them. "The market will set the price." [`10-Years-...`]
- **Partner departure.** [`Independent-Contractor-vs-Employee-...`]

## 1.6 Seasonality & cash-flow map

Janitorial is **the least seasonal of the four industries** — its central cash-flow risk is *terms*, not weather.

- **Corporate clients expect net 30.** Sometimes net 10–15 is negotiable; small contracts occasionally pay up front. [`How-To-PRICE-...`]
- **The float, run deliberately:** invoice the client immediately (not month-end), pay the subcontractor 10 days after their month-end invoice. *"The goal is to get paid first and then pay the subcontractor, so we're always cash flow positive."* [`How-To-PRICE-...`, `STOP-Guessing-...`]
- **Invoice every 4 weeks → 13 invoices/year instead of 12.** One operator claims to have "lost multi-six figures by charging every month." **A monthly invoice must be weekly × 4.33, not × 4 — billing at ×4 is a silent 7.6% revenue leak.** [`How-To-PRICE-...`]
- **December is soft** — enough to miss an annual target. [`UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month...`]
- **Winter helps in snow markets** — more tracked-in dirt, and it lets you cross-sell plowing and re-striping. [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]
- **Vacation-rental cleaning is the exception:** violently seasonal, headcount swinging **7 → 15–20**, with a same-day 10am-checkout/4pm-check-in window that turns one call-out into a crisis. [`UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future.md`]

## 1.7 Seller BS Detector — janitorial

**The gap between what owners live and what listings claim. Ask these, in this order.**

**On the accounts**
1. **"Give me the account list with square footage, frequency, hours allocated, hours actually worked, and monthly value."** Then compute the implied production rate yourself. **Above 4,000 sq ft/hr means the crew is being run into the ground or the scope isn't being delivered — and the complaint arrives on your watch.**
2. **"Which accounts are under 3 days a week or under $1,650/month?"** That's revenue you may have to shed. Model the decline.
3. **"Show me margin by account, sorted by weekly hours."** Big accounts at small-account margins were mispriced and are at renewal risk. Small accounts at big-account margins are losing money on management time.
4. **"Which accounts have you never margin-tested?"** *"I just found out, after servicing this account for 18 months, that we've been losing $200 a month on it. It just got lost in the client list."* [`Cleaning-operators/Scaling-Your-Cleaning-Business-Boosting-Profits-Efficiency-in-Janitorial-Services-Ep.-149.md`]
5. **"What's your largest client as a percentage of revenue?"** Over 15–20% is a liability, not a trophy. Get revenue by client for three years. [`Losing-a-Major-Client-...`]
6. **"What share of revenue is one-time work?"** Deep cleans, post-construction and move-outs carry premium hourly rates that flatter the blended margin — **and they do not renew.**

**On churn — the critical section, because clients leave silently**
7. **"List every account lost in the last 36 months and why."** A clean complaint log means nothing. Silent churn is this industry's characteristic failure.
8. **"When did each client last sign a contract?"** Retention means **renewals signed**, not clients who haven't cancelled. A client who hasn't re-signed in three years is unattached. [`2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions...`]
9. **"What complaints did you receive last quarter and what did you do?"** If the answer is "we don't get complaints," that's a data-collection failure.
10. **"Has any client been promised a specific cleaner — or you?"** Those accounts don't transfer. [`Why-Clients-Complain-...`]

**On pricing**
11. **"When was each account last repriced, and by how much?"** Unraised books hold real recoverable margin — and real churn risk when you fix them.
12. **"What's your payroll burden rate?"** Reconcile it to the P&L. Labour recorded at wage-only means unrecorded liabilities, or contractors who should be employees.
13. **"Which specialty services does each client buy, and which don't they?"** The gap is quantifiable upside — on their own numbers, **9–12% versus a 3% COLA.** [`2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions...`]

**On the workforce**
14. **"Walk me through your recruiting funnel: applications, interviews, offers, no-shows, 30-day survival."** If they can't produce it, there is no hiring system — only the owner personally persuading people to show up, and that leaves at closing.
15. **"Is training task-based or site-based?"** Task-based is transferable. Site-based means every departure costs an account.
16. **"How many jobs do you turn away for lack of staff?"** If demand exceeds capacity, your growth plan is a *hiring* plan, not a marketing plan.
17. **Walk the sites and ask the cleaners what they're short of.** Out-of-gloves is what a starved account looks like from the inside — and it's a leading indicator of a contract about to be lost.
18. **"Are your subcontractors properly classified?"** See below.

**On the owner**
19. **Run the delegation ladder** [`Why-Clients-Complain-...`]: do they clean? run hiring? answer every text? build the schedule? buy supplies? do payroll? **Every "yes" is a job you're buying.**
20. **"How many walkthroughs a month, and who does them?"** Above ~30 with the owner doing all of them means the sales function *is* the owner, and it stops at closing.

### 🇨🇦 Canadian adjustments — janitorial

- **Payroll burden is higher than the US 14.11% figure.** CPP + EI (employer portions) + EHT (Ontario, over threshold) + WSIB/WCB + statutory vacation and stat holiday pay typically run **15–20%**. Rebuild the burden rate provincially before applying any US pricing formula.
- **⚠️ Worker classification is the single biggest landmine in Canadian cleaning.** The CRA applies a control/integration/tools/chance-of-profit test, and a cleaner who services the company's clients, on the company's schedule, to the company's checklist, with the company's guarantee, is very likely an **employee** regardless of the contract. **Reclassification brings back unremitted CPP and EI (both portions), interest, penalties, plus WSIB premiums and vacation/stat pay — and it can attach to the business and to directors.** Get a CRA statement of account, a payroll compliance review, each sub's business number, GST/HST registration, WSIB clearance certificate and own insurance. **If a Canadian janitorial business shows impossibly good margins, check the payroll line first.**
- **Employee liabilities travel with the business — including in an asset sale.** Most Canadian jurisdictions deem continuity of employment on a sale; accrued service, notice and severance follow the employees to you. Ontario severance can run to **26 weeks** for long-service employees at qualifying employers. Price it, indemnify it, hold back against it.
- **Building-services successor rights.** Several provinces have specific protections for building-services contract workers on a change of contractor, which can require an incoming contractor to offer employment and recognise prior service. **Verify provincially before assuming you can re-staff a site.**
- **Google Local Services Ads** — the highest-intent channel named by three independent operators — has different coverage and Google Guarantee eligibility in Canada. Verify for the target city and category.
- **Condo and apartment common-area cleaning contracted through property managers is a large, under-appreciated Canadian niche** — with the caveat that property managers are a long-cycle relationship everyone is already chasing.

### The customer-value math

- A **$900/year customer** staying 4–5 years at a 10–20% margin is worth **$500–800 of profit over their entire life.** "That's a lot of work to keep a customer for five years." [`HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth`]
- **The move that matters: bundle mowing + cleanup + fertilization + trimming to take a $1,500/year customer to $3,000/year** — doubling revenue with zero acquisition cost.
- **500 customers at $3,000 beats 1,500 at $1,000** — identical revenue, but the second needs far more office staff, trucks, trailers, employees and drive time. *"You'd much rather have 20 great customers that pay you $100,000 a year than 2,000 customers that pay you a thousand."*

### Snow pricing — two fully disclosed models

**Model A — four quadrants on a 45-inch market (Detroit)** [`Landscaping-snow-operators/How-WE-Price-SNOW-REMOVAL-100-TRANSPARENT-PRICING.md`]

| Quadrant | Rate | Events | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plow | **$245** base scrape (1.5–3 in) | 15 | **$3,675** |
| Sidewalks | **$45**/shovel | 15 | **$675** |
| **Bulk salt** | **$240/ton applied** | 30 | **$7,200** |
| **Ice melt** | **$30/bag applied** | 30 | **$900** |
| | | **Seasonal total** | **$12,450** |

**Salt and ice melt are $8,100 of a $12,450 bid — 65% of the revenue.**
> "Obviously you make more money in your salt than you do your snow."

- Salt costs **$80–100/ton**; charge **2–2.5×**. His industry callout: *"Please stop putting bulk salt down at $100, $125, $150 per ton applied."* Floor should be **$180–200**, preferably **$240–300**.
- Ice melt: stop at $12–15/bag; **$22–25 minimum, $30 preferred.** One bag ≈ 1,000 sq ft.
- **Salt runs ≈ 2× plow events** (he prefers 2.5–2.75×).
- **Depth tiers with a 1.5× multiplier:** $245 → $365 → $550 → $82.50/inch above 10".
- **Production: a 9-ft-2-in plow covers ~1–1.25 acres/hour.**

**Where the $245 comes from — the best cost-accounting insight in the chapter:** the summer
lawn rate is **$80/man-hour** using *multiple* overhead recovery. Winter uses **single overhead
recovery over a 4-month window** — same overhead, a fraction of the hours to absorb it — **so the
rate roughly triples to $245.**

**Model B — tiered seasonal partnership** [`Landscaping-snow-operators/2025-Snow-Removal-Pricing-Secrets-You-Need-To-Know.md`]
- Sold as a season, **divided over five equal monthly payments**, never per-push.
- Three tiers: **10 plows + à la carte salt** (client takes salt-price risk) · **10 plows + 25 salts** (locked price) · **10 plows + 35 salts** (full protection). *"I pre-buy, you pre-buy."*
- **Ceiling: after 40 inches the contract reverts to per-push and per-ton.** The operator's protection against a catastrophic winter.
- **Lot staking: $4.50/stake**, or $200 per 200 stakes.
- Snow labour ≈ **$35/hour** loaded, coded separately from landscape labour.
- Market rate can exceed cost-plus: *"your numbers say $171, but you're going to do it for **$300 a ton applied** because that's market rate."*

**Model C — depth-tiered per-event with a T&M ceiling** [`Landscaping-snow-operators/How-to-run-a-Snowplow-Business-Red-flags-pricing-systems-and-what-not-to-do.md`]
Base price on a **2–3 inch event**; **+10–20% for 3–6 in**; raise again at 6–9 and 9–12; **over 12
inches switches to time and materials.** *Attach the weather report to the invoice showing inches
fallen* — "customers seem to like that."

**Seasonal contract sizing:** Minnesota averages **12–15 snow events**; he bids on **17–19**. *"That
way I'm protected. And that is literally how we do it inside the industry."* Season defined
**November–March**; October and April snow is **billed on top as bonus revenue.**

**Ice control is a separate and larger business:** *"In Minnesota, commercial ice control can be
almost double what you would experience with snowplowing and snow removal."* [`Landscaping-snow-operators/Snow-Contracts-EXPOSED-Read-THIS-Before-You-Sign.md`]

## 2.2 Staffing playbook and turnover realities

**The single best line in the corpus about landscaping crews** [`Lessons-from-a-Founder-...`]:
> "One thing that isn't talked about a lot for landscaping is **how close to the knife's edge most your
> employees are.** Saying *hey, we'll get you your cheques tomorrow* even though they thought they were
> getting them today — **that's like a death sentence.** These guys are on the knife edge, they have no
> savings, nothing. When you look a guy in the eye and say *you're getting your cheque Friday* — **I
> don't care if you have to pawn your car, you better get the money there.** Because that speaks
> volumes, and that consistency builds trust."

**Payroll timing is not a cash-management variable in this industry. It is the entire employment
relationship.** Size the working-capital reserve accordingly.

**What 95% retention actually took** [`Lawn-Care-Podcast-...`]:
- **A 10-year company truck for personal use** — for crew members who never went into management — plus a **$250 fuel card**, with registration, inspection and maintenance covered. *"All I hear walking around now is: I only have five years left."*
- **Financial education as a retention tool.** A peer-panel-administered hardship fund evolved into a company-wide personal-finance programme. The story that lands: a young fleet mechanic entered his budget into the projection tool and **"$5.6 million showed up on the screen. He said — is that a comma at the end? Why is there three zeros?"** The stated mission: *"My plan is to make landscape millionaires."*
- **Structured onboarding:** application → recruiter call → placement matched to skill set → training modules → dispatch → **recruiter follows up again** to confirm the new hire feels confident, safe and equipped.
- **QR codes on jackets, snow plows and trucks** linking to the application page; a **full-time in-house recruiter**.
- The counter-example: a foreman who quit **over a single health benefit.** *"If there's no vision, guys are going to jump ship pretty quickly."*

**The non-billable time system — the cheapest margin fix available** [`Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`]:
- **~$3,000/day wasted** if crews stand around five minutes morning and evening across 50–75 people.
- **Fuelling moved to nights only. No morning gas-station stops.**
- **The gate system:** crew leaders punch in behind the gate and pull the truck up; **crew members arrive 15 minutes later and wait outside the gate, off the clock**, punching in when they board. *"That gives the crew accountability, because the crew leader has two guys who aren't on the clock yet waiting for him."*
- **Dispatch at 7:00; most trucks out by 7:07.**
> "**Non-billable time is where a lot of the net profit in the landscape industry disappears.**"

*(⚠️ The gate system needs legal review in Canada — see §2.7.)*

**Key-person retention costs less than you think.** On an acquisition an hour away, the account manager
was wavering: *"In my mind — you're an hour away, I need you. So I overpaid him, gave him like a **$6
[an hour] increase.** You're not going anywhere. I can't afford for this manager to leave in the first
year."* He still works there. [`Lessons-from-a-Founder-...`]

**Equipment quality is a staffing issue.** *"If you're deploying your people into some subpar stuff
that's frustrating to work on, the production is going to be down, your profitability is going to be
down — it all ties together."* [`Lawn-Care-Podcast-...`]

## 2.5 Why these businesses die

- **The owner-operator price list that never got repriced for crews.** (24 → 14 lawns/day.)
- **Uncapped seasonal contracts in a heavy winter.** Without the 40-inch or 45-inch reversion clause, a seasonal contract is an unhedged weather short. [`2025-Snow-Removal-...`, `How-WE-Price-SNOW-REMOVAL-...`]
- **The under-minimum refund clause.** One operator holds a contract where **plowing fewer than 10 times means refunding money.** The bonus clause for exceeding the event count *"a lot of times won't kick in, because you just don't get enough snow to activate it."* **The refund bites more often than the bonus pays.** [`How-to-run-a-Snowplow-Business-...`]
- **The receivable that melts.** *"Make sure you invoice and expect to be paid immediately upon completion... never extend credit further than [a month]."* His case: **a $10–12,000 end-of-season bill; the snow melted, the association dispersed, the complex was sold, and "Mr. Snowplower was left with no money in his pocket."* [`Acquiring-Minds/One-Acquiring-Minds-Guest-Buys-Another-Adrian-Pinto-Mike-Botkin-landscaping.md`]
- **Pay-when-paid subcontracting.** A national broker's agreement produced **$3,900 owed from January–March, still unpaid in August** (~$5,400 with late fees). And the structural point: *"They're betting that 20% of us don't use the app, 20% don't take real-time photos... somebody has run the numbers saying we know we're going to have $10 million right to the bottom line because people aren't going to report correctly."* **The compliance clauses are a revenue line for the broker, not an oversight.** [`Snow-Contracts-EXPOSED-...`]
- **Installation exposure to the housing cycle.** *"In 2007/2008 that's why so many landscape install companies got wiped out — the ones that remained were the companies that did a lot of maintenance."* [`HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth`]
- **Skill concentration.** If 20% of revenue is hardscaping only the owner can do, **a buyer must write that 20% off the top line.**
- **Overhead under-recovery in winter.** Pricing snow at the summer man-hour rate under-recovers by roughly 3×. [`How-WE-Price-SNOW-REMOVAL-...`]

## 2.6 Seasonality & cash-flow map

**This is the defining feature of the industry in Canada.**

| Month | Landscape | Snow | Cash position |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jul–Aug** | Peak maintenance | **Snow contracts sold and signed** | Strong; pre-season pricing forcing deposits |
| **Sep–Oct** | Fall cleanups | Equipment prepped; plows mounted early to shake out faults | Pre-season price rise Oct 1 (+20–30%) |
| **Nov–Mar** | Dead | **Peak** — 5 equal monthly instalments Nov–Mar | Nov and Mar cost only **$200–600** to service against a $2,490 billing |
| **Apr–Jun** | Spring ramp | Shoulder snow billed as bonus | Weakest — collections fight "end of October, getting them back to us" |

- **The hub-and-spoke design, stated explicitly:** *"I always appreciated the hub and spoke mentality of a business **to even out the seasonality** of what we do. We're in the snow belt up in New England, so we transition from landscape services over to snow and back... mid-season we might face drought, so your landscape maintenance might be slowing down but your irrigation's picking up."* [`Lawn-Care-Podcast-...`]
- **The December/January invoice shock, and the fix:** *"Companies have real budgets, they don't want $5,000 invoices in December and January. I wouldn't either."* → **five equal monthly billings November–March.** *"That's called making sure that we are guaranteed income."* Current mix ~**40% flat rate / 60% per push**, deliberately migrating toward flat. [`How-WE-Price-SNOW-REMOVAL-...`]
- **Contracts solve winter cash flow *and* survive a change of ownership** — signed 9- or 12-month agreements "take away the edge from the customers when they hear the business has been acquired." [`HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth`]
- **Divisionalise the accounts.** *"You could run a business where half of it's losing you money and the other half's making great money, and you need to know that."* [`Lawn-Care-Podcast-...`]

## 2.7 Seller BS Detector — landscaping & snow

**On pricing and margin**
1. **"When was this price list set, and what was crew productivity then?"** The 24 → 14 lawns/day decay is the silent killer. Get route times per property and compare to the billed price.
2. **"Give me gross margin by division — maintenance, enhancement/construction, irrigation, turf care, snow — separately."** A profitable division can hide a loss-making one.
3. **"What's the maintenance/construction revenue split by year, for five years?"** Enhancement work is lumpy ($80K, $150K, $700K on one route) and does not recur. **Normalise it.**
4. **"What's your applied price per ton of salt and per bag of ice melt?"** Single numbers that instantly reveal whether the book is at industry standard or given away. **At $240/ton vs $125/ton on 30 runs, that's a $3,450 difference on one site.**
5. **"How much salt do you actually apply versus bid?"** One operator bids 2,000 lb on a 1-acre lot that takes 800–1,000 and says so on camera. **That padding is real margin — and it disappears when a client audits it or a new owner services the site honestly.**
6. **"How is winter overhead recovered?"** A combined lawn-and-snow business pricing winter at the summer man-hour rate is under-recovering by ~3×, and its snow division is being subsidised by lawn.
7. **"What's your revenue per man-hour, by contract?"** One disclosed estimate achieved **$951/hr**. [`EXPERT-Snow-Removal-...`]

**On the contracts — the highest-risk area**
8. **"What's the flat-rate versus per-push mix, in dollars?"** Per-push-heavy books are weather bets; flat-rate books are annuities.
9. **"What snowfall assumption was each seasonal contract priced on, and is there a cap clause?"** **Uncapped seasonal contracts in a heavy year are how snow companies lose money on their best customers.**
10. **"Read me every under-minimum refund clause."** A contingent liability the seller will not volunteer, and it lands on you in a light winter.
11. **"Show me every subcontractor agreement with a national or property-management broker."** Hunt for **pay-when-paid**, real-time reporting forfeiture, all-damages-whatever-the-cause, first-service-failure voiding everything after, all-fines-including-the-customer's, one-way indemnification, and sole discretion to prorate or deny. **Revenue under these terms should not be valued at face.** [`Snow-Contracts-EXPOSED-...`]
12. **"Give me snow-event counts per season for 5+ years alongside revenue."** A seller marketing off a heavy-snow year is selling the top of a weather cycle. Normalise to the local average.
13. **"Do you do ice control, and what's it worth?"** Potentially double the snow revenue — either upside or a licensing/equipment gap.

**On cash and equipment**
14. **"Show me the aged receivables, specifically season-end balances, and the collections history."** The $10–12K melted-receivable story is the industry's characteristic bad debt.
15. **"Build me the cost per hour of every machine"** using the ten inputs. **If they can't, they don't know which contracts are profitable — and the replacement reserve nobody funds is the largest adjustment in most equipment-based deals.** Get age, hours and remaining life on every unit.
16. **"What's your non-billable time percentage?"** If they've never measured it, assume it's large — and treat it as the cheapest post-close margin improvement available.

**On the people and the owner**
17. **"When did you last take two consecutive weeks off, and what broke?"** The absentee-ownership test, doubling as diligence. *"If you can stay away for two solid weeks and everything goes fine — you're setting yourself up for a very successful sale."* [`HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth`]
18. **"Where are you on the field → office → estimator → CEO ladder?"** *"If they're in any of those first three phases, their company's value is much, much lower."* A buyer can replace a CEO; they cannot replace an owner who is also the estimator holding every customer relationship.
19. **"How much of the crew is loyal to you personally?"** One owner estimates **a third of his team would leave if he sold.**
20. **"Do you have site measurements and production rates on file for every account?"** If pricing lives in the owner's head, you're buying an estimate you cannot reproduce.

### 🇨🇦 Canadian adjustments — landscaping & snow

- **Rebuild every event count from Environment Canada snowfall normals.** The 45-inch Detroit and 12–15-event Minnesota assumptions are close to Toronto (~48 in / 122 cm) but **far below Ottawa, Montreal, Quebec City and snowbelt Ontario.** This single input drives the entire seasonal bid.
- **Seasonality is far more extreme than in most US source markets.** A Canadian route bills roughly **7–8 months of maintenance** and must pair with snow to survive. **The winter contract book is not an add-on — it is half the business.**
- **⚠️ Slip-and-fall liability is a distinct Canadian exposure.** Ontario's *Occupiers' Liability Act* imposes a **60-day written notice** requirement for snow/ice injury claims, and insurance requirements vary provincially. **Inadequate salting is the liability exposure — and it's exactly the line item a seller under-prices.** Verify current provincial rules and the target's coverage.
- **Salt supply is contracted pre-season in Canada** and shortages in heavy winters are real. Verify the seller's supply agreement and price exposure before valuing salt margin.
- **⚠️ The off-the-clock gate system needs legal review.** Provincial employment standards on when time becomes compensable, and on travel time, vary and are enforced. Verify with a Canadian employment lawyer before copying it.
- **Company vehicles for personal use are a taxable benefit in Canada** (standby charge and operating benefit under the ITA). The 10-year truck programme creates a T4 benefit — structure and communicate accordingly.
- **Fertilization margins may not transfer.** Ontario and Quebec **cosmetic-pesticide restrictions** materially limit residential lawn applications. **The 70–80% margin line is the most attractive number in this chapter and the one most likely to be unavailable in Canada — verify before underwriting it.**
- **Temporary foreign workers.** Canadian landscaping crews frequently include SAWP/TFW-stream workers whose status, housing and pay obligations are regulated. **Verify LMIA compliance and worker status in diligence** — a real, findable liability a US checklist would miss entirely.
- **⚠️ The asset-sale-to-shed-liabilities structure does not work the same way here.** One US operator fired everyone Friday and rehired Monday to avoid workers' comp exposure. In Canada, **employment is generally deemed continuous on a sale of a business — including an asset sale** — and WSIB/WCB liabilities can attach to a purchaser. Get a clearance certificate and Canadian employment advice.
- **Pay-when-paid clauses** are enforceable in Canada subject to provincial prompt-payment legislation; **Ontario's Construction Act prompt-payment rules may bite on some broker agreements.** Confirm with a Canadian commercial lawyer.
- **LMN**, the budgeting and job-costing platform used in two of these videos, is an Ontario company widely used by Canadian contractors — the tooling transfers directly even where the numbers don't.

## 3.3 How contracts are won and lost

**The density doctrine — the defining strategy of this industry:**
> "**You don't need to dominate the entire city your first year. You need to dominate your zip codes.**"
> — `Pest-control-operators/How-to-Start-a-Pest-Control-Business-450k-per-year.md`

Three reasons named: reduces drive time, increases visibility (customers see the truck constantly), and
**visibility builds trust.**

**His actual ramp on a new location:** day 1 = **3 accounts**; day 2 = **7**; week 2 = **~50 jobs**;
**end of month one = 200–300 jobs, all in one subdivision** — a full quarterly route built in a single
neighbourhood, with no ad spend.

**The tactics:**
- **The Law of 100** (credited to Alex Hormozi): 100 conversations, 100 doors, 100 door hangers, 100 mailers, 100 texts, 100 emails — **every day, six or seven days a week.** Thirty days = 2,400 touches per channel.
- **"Nine-arounds":** after every job, hit **two houses left, two right, five across the street.** Script: *"We're servicing your neighbour Sally Smith today, and because my truck's in the area I'll do this first service for XYZ — saves you money, saves me time."*
- **Yard sign on every lawn**, including friends' and family's.
- **Ask for five referrals before leaving.** *"More than likely they'll give you one... a lot of times they'll call them for you, they'll walk you to the neighbour's house."*
- **Paid channels come after the first 100–150 customers**, to amplify density — not to create it.
- **Sell the plan immediately after solving the pest problem:** *"They're already excited that you've gotten rid of their problem, their guard is down, they know you produce results."* The script: instead of billing the $250 emergency call, offer the $45/mo plan covering that same pest, and mention the treatment only lasts 90 days. **Either outcome wins.** [`How-to-Transition-...`]
- **Property management companies are the growth lever:** *"If you can get in with the property management company, do so — **they are money, man. They're going to send you work.**"* [`How-much-can-you-make-...`]
- **Demand generation is entirely local and unglamorous:** be present on social media, collect reviews, great customer service, be involved in the community. *"People just need to know who you are."* [`Pest-control-operators/How-much-can-you-make-as-a-pest-control-owner-operator.md`]

## 3.5 Why these businesses die

**The four killers, named by a 19-location operator** [`How-to-Start-a-Pest-Control-Business...`]:

1. **Underpricing.** The mechanism is precise: a new operator finds an incumbent at **$89/quarter** who "has been doing it since 1950 and never raised their prices," and undercuts at **$39/quarter.** Now they're the cheapest in town, **driving across the city, losing money on the drive and on the job.**
2. **Not collecting payment information up front.** *"You can't go get fuel and just be like — yeah, I'm just going to pay you a month later."* **Card on file from day one. If they're not home and there's no card on file, don't do the job.**
3. **Too many services at once.** Pest plus construction plus landscaping plus wildlife. **Don't add services until at least $1M.**
4. **Quitting too early.** Most get 25–100 customers and burn out.

Plus: **decide premium vs cheap on day one.** *"You can't have a thousand customers and then decide you're going to go to your premium model."*

**And the structural death: the one-off book.** Seasonality plus fixed truck payments is the trap.
*"Truck payments do not go dormant."*

**The recurring plan is the seasonality fix, not a nice-to-have.** A quarterly-billed book converts a

important number in a pest control acquisition.**

## 3.7 Seller BS Detector — pest control

**The valuation line, stated by an operator, unprompted:**
> "**Your company isn't valued based on how many trucks you have or how good you are at killing bugs —
> it's determined by one key metric, and that's monthly recurring revenue.**"
> — `How-to-Transition-...`

**A seller leading with truck count, tech count or gross revenue is leading with the wrong metric on
purpose.**

**On the book**
1. **"Give me the recurring vs one-off revenue split, in dollars, by month, for 36 months."** A pest business with a big summer and a dead January is a one-off book no matter what the seller calls it.
2. **"Does 'recurring' mean auto-billed?"** Plans on file with no payment method, no auto-renew and no signed term are **a mailing list, not MRR.**
3. **"Give me real retention data, by cohort, from the CRM."** Not the 3-year rule of thumb. **Recurring revenue with 18-month churn is a treadmill; with 4-year retention it's an annuity. This number moves the multiple more than anything else.**
4. **"What's your actual average annual revenue per customer?"** Compare to the **$400–500 median.** Below it means either a pricing problem (fixable upside) or a service-mix problem (not).
5. **"When were prices last raised across the whole book, not just for new customers?"** The $89-since-1950 problem is endemic. Unraised books hold real upside — and real churn risk when you fix them.
6. **"Give me the price distribution, not the average."** *"All my customers are right around 120"* concealed a tail at **$75–85.**
7. **"What's your re-service/callback rate, in stops per hundred?"** One in eleven stops on the disclosed day earned **$0**. A book with 10–15% callbacks has a service-quality problem you'll pay for in labour hours and churn.

**On the route**
8. **"Give me the customer address list."** Then **map it.** *Density is the difference between $400/hour and a loss.* A 1,000-customer book scattered across a metro is not the same asset as 1,000 customers in eight subdivisions, at any price.
9. **"How many trucks, and how many services per quarter?"** **~250 services/quarter is the honest limit for one truck.** If a seller claims 1,200 customers on one truck, either service is being skipped or the numbers are wrong.
10. **"What share of revenue is your single largest account?"** The apartment complex in the disclosed day was **~24% of that day's revenue** — one property-manager relationship, likely undocumented and personal to the owner.
11. **"Are the property-management agreements written, assignable on change of control, and who is the relationship?"**

**On the money and the owner**
12. **"Strip your own labour out."** SDE on a one-truck route is mostly the owner's own work. **Subtract a fully-burdened technician's wage for the routes the owner drives.** A seller presenting $240K/year of "profit" from a one-truck route is presenting a salary. Note the **industry median net margin is ~10%** — closer to what a business with hired technicians actually earns.
13. **"Is payment on file?"** A book billed in arrears carries receivable risk and a collections workload the P&L won't show.
14. **"How many services do you offer?"** A pest company also doing landscaping and construction has no specialisation, no pricing power, and probably no accurate per-line margins.

## 3.8 What the deal teardowns add

Two Acquisitions Anonymous episodes review businesses with pest control revenue, and both surface
risks the operator videos don't.

**The multi-service small-town operator** [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`]
— a 51-year-old Florida business combining well drilling, irrigation and water treatment **with lawn
fertilization, home pest control and weed control**, at $12M revenue and $2.4M EBITDA across **five SIC
codes**.

- **The margin history is the tell:** revenue rose steadily (9.8 → 11 → 12) while margin swung
  **20.4% → 15.5% → 20.0%.** *"It's hard to analyze a business like this when you've got such disparate
  lines... probably the mix changes a lot from year to year."* **Demand revenue and margin by service
  line for at least three years.**
- **⚠️ Licences are held by individuals, not the business** — here, three of them (well drilling,
  irrigation, pest control). *"Typically it is the owner of the business who is licensed and everybody
  else operates underneath his licence. If he retires and leaves, you the new buyer need to achieve
  those licences."* With three separate licences, *"it raises the degree of difficulty by quite a bit."*
- **The counter-argument, which is the more interesting one:** in a small market the mish-mash is a
  moat. *"There isn't enough demand to have lawn fertilization and home pest control as standalone
  businesses, but there is enough demand for one company that does everything. **And you don't have this
  army of private-equity-backed pest control companies coming after you in small-town USA.**"*
- **The hedged transition sentence to distrust:** *"Owner would like to retire but is willing to stay on
  in some capacity."* — *"That's like 'owner likes pizza but also hates cheese.' **This guy does not want
  to stay on at all — he's hedging so it doesn't scare you away.**"*

**The e-commerce pest business** [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/A-1M-Pest-Control-Business-Ecommerce-Buzz-or-Bust-AA-297.md`]
is off-thesis (Australian bug zappers sold on Amazon) but contributes one durable warning: **a
below-market multiple is a question, not a discount.** At ~1.6× cash flow, *"there is a reason this
thing is priced under two times... **this will start feeling like 10 times cash flow inside of a
year.**"* If a Canadian pest control book is offered at 1.5× SDE, **find out what the seller knows about
the next twelve months that you don't.**

### 🇨🇦 Canadian adjustments — pest control

- **⚠️ Seasonality is longer and deeper than anything in these US sources.** The Sun Belt model assumes ~3 dormant months; **a Canadian book faces 5–6.** That makes recurring revenue *more* essential, makes exterior-only winter service partly impractical, and means **annual revenue per customer must be earned in fewer visits or held by prepaid annual plans.** Do not apply the $418–500/customer figure to a Canadian target without rebuilding the visit schedule.
- **⚠️ Licensing is provincial and attaches to individuals, not the business.** Ontario has structural exterminator licence classes; Quebec operates under the *Code de gestion des pesticides*. **A solo owner-operator's licence generally does not transfer with the business** — the buyer or an employee must be licensed *before* closing. This is a closing condition, not a diligence footnote. Confirm the class needed and who holds it in week one.
- **⚠️ Ontario and Quebec cosmetic-pesticide restrictions** limit residential outdoor products, changing both the service mix and the achievable revenue per customer. The US product set behind these numbers is not fully available here.
- **The recurring-revenue thesis is the acquisition thesis.** Buy the book, not the trucks — and price any one-off-heavy Canadian business as *a seasonal job with a payroll problem*, which is exactly what the operators call it.

## 4.2 The plateau framework — the best growth map in the corpus

From `From-Plumbing-Tech-to-CEO-John-Wilson-...` — growth happens in **plateaus**, each one a period
of reinvestment that hurts:

| Stage | What you must add |
|---|---|
| **$0–1M** | An office person / CSR / dispatcher — **or you work 14-hour days** doing books, booking calls and managing the field at once |
| **$2–3M** | A field manager to manage the revenue producers |
| **$3–5M** | A second manager for a different department |
| **Up to $15M** | You can *sell* your way there on a lean structure — **out of $15M you might be "ripping two to three million of cash," because overhead is barely different from a $5M business** |
| **$15M → $20M** | **The wall. "You have to add $2 million of payroll to get over that hump."** Real accounting team, real HR, a five-person marketing team, **one manager per 8–10 people.** *"That is when businesses get their butts kicked — during the reinvestment."* |

**The diagnostic:** *"If you're feeling capped, if there aren't enough hours in the day — welcome to the
plateau. That's a good thing, because it means there's an answer, and the answer is some type of
reinvestment."*

**The warning:** at ~$3M the business throws off $300–500K of free cash flow, *"and that's when people
start doing dumb stuff."*

**And the honest one:** *"I'd rather go zero to one than one to three and a half — that has been a
freaking nightmare. You're investing in all the overhead and leadership so **margins actually go
down**, you don't have pricing competitiveness from suppliers yet, and you're still competing with the
bigger guys. And you've got to hire technicians who are getting paid more elsewhere — **why join us?**"*
[`How-we-bought-3-Multi-Million-Dollar-HVAC-Businesses`]

## 4.3 Staffing playbook

**"Show me the comp plan and I'll show you the outcomes."** [`How-we-bought-3-...HVAC-Businesses`]
- **Every technician acquired has received a raise.** A defined model allocates set percentages of revenue to technicians, commissions and uniforms. *"I want to pay for the best because we want the best."*
- **Technicians do not care about NPS.** Pay a bonus when a tech is **named in a five-star Google review** — *"they can't point to an 86 NPS to a friend, but they can say look, we've got 1,500 five-star reviews."*
- **Lead-handoff incentive:** a tech who spots a replacement gets a kickback for introducing the sales advisor, plus a slice on close.
- **Recognition is not one-size-fits-all.** From leading a union team at Lockheed Martin: one member grabbed him by the collar — *"If you ever recognise me in front of everyone again, I'm going to kick you in the shin. Happy to be recognised — one-on-one."*
- **Identity precedes recruiting:** *"It took us more than a year to figure out who we are... **once you know who you are, it makes it a lot easier to attract who you want.**"*

**Licensing redundancy — worth stealing outright** [`He-built-a-100M-HVAC-business`]:
- **Pay employees to sit licensing exams in other jurisdictions**, then pay a **monthly stipend** to anyone whose licence the company uses.
- **Deliberate redundancy:** *"We don't want just two Ohio plumbing licences. We want three or four."*
- The mental hurdle, named: *"You're literally paying someone to eventually compete with you."* — *"Yeah, it helps to have that to operate."*

**Onboarding at scale** [`Owned-and-Operated/From-Plumbing-Tech-to-CEO-John-Wilson-on-Scaling-a-26M-Home-Service-Company.md`]:
- **New branch managers work out of head office for three months before their location exists.**
- Technicians onboard centrally, **every other Monday, for three days.**
- **A "direct field support" department virtually rides along with each new tech for their first 30–60 days.** The alternative he rejects: *"Send Bill with Jimmy, and Jimmy teach Bill everything, let me know when he's ready — **oh boy, this is a recipe for disaster.**"*
- **Two in-house recruiters.**

**The technician-economics gap** [`Owned-and-Operated/Buy-vs.-Build-Start-or-Acquire-Your-First-Business-220.md`]: staff are astonished to learn they must
generate more revenue than their own wage. *"I covered my wage, I'm good."* — *"What about the gas that
got you there?"* **A workforce that doesn't accept a revenue expectation will resist every attempt to
raise productivity, and that resistance is what turns a 6-month integration into a clear-out.**

**"Marketing ≠ lead generation."** *"This person's a marketer — doesn't mean anything, 0%, if they
don't understand **local service lead gen.** They know what SEO is, but what's the strategy to get to
number one? **The people who are best at it are the people who've just been doing it.**"* [`Owned-and-Operated/From-Plumbing-Tech-to-CEO-John-Wilson-on-Scaling-a-26M-Home-Service-Company.md`]

## 4.4 How work is won

- **The "Apex value" profile for residential home services** [`4.5M-HVAC-Business-With-33-Margins`, crediting Chris Hoffman]:
  1. **Residential service** (repair and replacement)
  2. **Under 10% commercial exposure**
  3. **No new construction**
  4. **Good tech stack** — ServiceTitan or one of two or three other top CRMs
  5. **Strong Google reviews / GMB presence**
- **Marketing is just choosing who you work for.** *"Who you're going to work for determines who you're going to hire, your compensation structure, the vehicles you drive and what's in them, your physical location, overhead staffing, tech stack. **It touches every single part of our business.**"* [`From-Plumbing-Tech-to-CEO`]
- **"The less we do, the bigger we grow."** Down to ~20 job types where most plumbing companies have ~150. He killed a **$1M/year septic line** because the gross margin was low and it took too long. [`Owned-and-Operated/From-Plumbing-Tech-to-CEO-John-Wilson-on-Scaling-a-26M-Home-Service-Company.md`]
- **Capacity boards** pulling live CRM data tell the call centre where to route and the digital team where to spend; **traditional media is fixed once placed, digital moves daily.** The strain: 10 locations × 3 trades = **"30 different companies that our marketing team is trying to build capacity for."** [`He-built-a-100M-HVAC-business`]
- **The cheap-lead thesis:** with HVAC replacement leads getting steadily more expensive, hunt adjacent services with under-competed lead pools — **duct cleaning, chimney sweeping** (*"literally probably $3 leads... there's no LSA going on in the chimney sweep game"*), **appliance repair** (a text to 8–12,000 existing customers became an **$8M service line**), and **water mitigation.** *"You've built this base — what else do they need done on their home?"* [`Owned-and-Operated/From-Plumbing-Tech-to-CEO-John-Wilson-on-Scaling-a-26M-Home-Service-Company.md`]
- **Reputation as the sourcing channel:** one operator identified his best acquisition target purely because *"anytime someone asked for a recommendation on Nextdoor or Facebook groups, they'd have 20 or 30 recommendations before another name came up. We were like — what is going on?"* [`How-we-bought-3-...HVAC-Businesses`]

## 4.5 Why these businesses die

**New construction inside a residential service business — the mechanism, told properly:**
> "Guy launches a plumbing company. I need food, I call my friends, I call my family... you need work,
> so guys get into new construction because it sort of falls in their lap. **Normally it's the stability
> of the owner's home life that gets these owners into new construction** and doing projects they just
> shouldn't. If they'd taken that same amount of time, instead of laying pipe, they could have gone door
> to door, figured out how to market. **It's almost always a lead flow problem.**"
>
> And why they keep it: *"They hang on because they're scared of the down season — 'ah, it keeps us busy
> in the winter.' **Well, you should learn how to market. That's really the answer.**"*
> — John Wilson, `4.5M-HVAC-Business-With-33-Margins`

**This maps directly onto Canadian seasonal service businesses.** Ask why the off-season work exists:
strategy, or a symptom of not being able to generate leads?

**Multi-trade too early.** Asked whether plumbing + HVAC at $4.5M is a good idea: **"It's a bad idea."**
- One peer stayed **pure plumbing until ~$30M** and became best-in-class as a result.
- Wilson on his own three-trade business in the mid-$20Ms: *"I have friends the same size but pure HVAC or pure plumbing, and **their businesses are just better than mine** — way more focused, way higher margin, way better cash flow."*
- **HVAC took seven years to become self-sufficient** at his company — 20 employees, 2 managers, $4M revenue. *"HVAC and plumbing — we use them interchangeably, but those are totally different things. Different marketing, different staffing, different phone answering."* [`Grow-and-Operate-a-Holding-Company-...`]
- **Why owners do it anyway:** *"There are these plateaus and you don't know what's next... launching HVAC looks easy because once we know the steps to something it looks easy. **They're looking for an easy-button step change instead of doubling down on the one thing.**"* And the inference: *"That's why they launched the other trade — because they couldn't figure out lead flow in the first one."*

**Accumulation without consolidation.** Nine acquisitions became **five payrolls, five accounting files
and ten bank accounts**: *"If I looked at a competitor my same exact size, **I would be a worse operator
than them, because they are more focused.** We ended up getting operationally slow."* The corroborating
data point: **Chenmark acquired ~14 companies in two or three years, then spent the next two or three
figuring out who they were.** [`Grow-and-Operate-a-Holding-Company-...`]

**Absentee ownership at small scale.** The distressed company Wilson bought had lost money **every year
for a decade** for one identifiable reason: *"The owner was attempting to be a passive owner in a very
tiny company, and the people he had equipped to manage were not capable of managing it."* [`Owned-and-Operated/From-Plumbing-Tech-to-CEO-John-Wilson-on-Scaling-a-26M-Home-Service-Company.md`]

**Subcontractor dependency.** *"Anytime you're putting somebody in somebody else's building to perform
work, things will go wrong... **we have more headaches with subs than we do with employees. Subs are
kind of like the Wild West.** This is not smart passive income."* [`2M-of-EBITDA-...AA-194`]

## 4.6 Seasonality & cash-flow map

- **8–9× peak-to-trough monthly revenue** in Texas HVAC. Canadian HVAC is inverted and just as extreme — heating peak in winter, cooling in summer, **troughs in the shoulder seasons.**
- **Electrical and plumbing are not weather-dependent** and are used deliberately to fund the HVAC ramp: *"It's nice to have all three trades that can toss leads from one to the other."* [`He-built-a-100M-HVAC-business`]
- **Restoration runs on insurer receivables:** you pay the subs before the insurer pays you, on 30–50 jobs a month, at **45–60 day cycles.** *"He is paying the subcontractor and then waiting 45 or 60 days to get paid by the insurance company."* **A business that looks asset-light needs real working capital.** [`2M-of-EBITDA-...AA-194`]

## 4.7 Seller BS Detector — home services

**On the margin and the org chart**
1. **Reverse-engineer the org chart from the margin.** A 33% margin at $4.5M means somebody isn't being paid — usually the owner is doing the service-manager job and nobody is doing marketing. **Add those costs back before you value anything.**
2. **"What's your marketing spend as a percentage of revenue, for three years?"** ~5% is the benchmark at scale. **If it fell in the year before sale, that's the story** — inflated earnings and a damaged pipeline.
3. **"Which service lines are self-sufficient and which are subsidised?"** Seven years and $4M for HVAC to stand alone. A multi-trade target may have one real business and one expensive hobby.

**On the revenue**
4. **"What's the new-construction and commercial exposure?"** Score against the Apex profile. **No new construction; under 10% commercial.**
5. **"How do you define an active account?"** 36 months for plumbing, 18 for HVAC. Then: **"How many *total historical* customers do you have, and can your CRM reach them?"** — the reactivation upside.
6. **"What's the recurring membership/maintenance-plan revenue, and what's the retention?"**
7. **Model the seasonal trough, not the annual average.** At 8–9× peak-to-trough, a business that looks fine annually can be insolvent in the shoulder season.

**On the licences and the people**
8. **"Who holds each licence, is it personal or corporate, and are they staying?"** **A target with one licence-holder — usually the owner — is a business that stops on closing day.**
9. **"What's your onboarding system?"** "Send Bill with Jimmy" is not a transferable asset, and it means your first year's hires will be materially worse than the seller's tenured staff.
10. **"Show me the comp plan."** It predicts the outcomes you'll get.

**On the deal itself — from the corpus's one fully documented failure** [`How-we-bought-3-...HVAC-Businesses`]
11. **Verify the lease with the landlord directly, in writing.** A falsified lease — believed to be three years, actually month-to-month — got them evicted and destroyed the business. *"It's really hard to have a repair shop without a shop."*
12. **Call the major customers.** Concentration plus an undisclosed fraud is what killed that deal, and both were discoverable by talking to customers.
13. **Treat a seller who resists lender diligence as a warning.** *"We didn't do bank loans, there was no SBA, so they didn't necessarily look at that."* **An underwriter's scrutiny is free diligence.**
14. **"How much of your time does this business consume?"** The best of their three targets had **an owner 95% out with a GM running it** — which is why it was worth $5.8M of revenue and integrated cleanly.
15. **"You can't do a good deal with a bad person."** Their first seller was defrauding customers and later pursued by the FBI; their third was a high-character operator who had already professionalised the business. **The character of the seller predicted the quality of the business in both directions — and it's assessable long before the financials are.**

## 4.8 Buying into home services — what the acquirers say

**Match the deal to your skill set** [`Owned-and-Operated/Buy-vs.-Build-Start-or-Acquire-Your-First-Business-220.md`]:

| Your background | What you can handle |
|---|---|
| **Technician / operator** | Can build from zero, or buy small ($1–1.5M). Can run calls when everyone quits |
| **Marketing / SEO** | **Stay away from small.** You're hostage to an operational partner — *"nine times out of ten they fail... you can't fire that person"* |
| **Finance / CFO** | Needs **$2–3M+**. *"What as a CFO are you going to do at a $3 million HVAC company? Very little strategy, because you're fighting fires"* |
| **MBA, no trade** | First problem is **finding someone to hold the licence** — and if they won't run calls, you're hiring a technician too, while cash burns |

The proof case runs in reverse: one buyer's staff **all quit after closing**, and he survived only
because he could run the calls himself. *"If I'd built, and then everybody quit — phone's not ringing,
and I'm not learning or running. **Now I'm just screwed.**"*

**The financing arithmetic for a home-services deal of any size**
[`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-12M-Well-Drilling-Pest-Control-Business-AA-298.md`]:
an SBA 10-year loan supports roughly **3.75× adjusted EBITDA.** On $2.4M EBITDA that implies **$9M of
supportable debt against a $5M cap — a $4M gap** to be filled with equity, a seller note, or both. And
the compounding problem, from an actual lender: **"conventional lenders don't like to lend into a
situation where there's that much key-man risk. They'd rather lend into businesses that are
professionalized, have a management team."** *The deal that most needs conventional debt is the least
likely to get it.*

**And the culture cost nobody prices:** *"The biggest headache is cultural... **if I walked into an
acquisition, it was four people, and I started to sense [problems] — we would fire the whole team
immediately.**"* Note *why* the team is usually broken: *"The reason the smaller companies are trading
is because **the owners are tired** — and the tiredness is caused by them mismanaging companies,
letting the techs run all over them, letting the techs not sell."*

**The distressed acquisition template** [`Owned-and-Operated/Grow-and-Operate-a-Holding-Company-w-John-Wilson-MI320.md`]:
a plumbing company **losing money every year for a decade**, $750K revenue and shrinking, 12–14 staff,
bought for **$110,000 — exactly the debt needed to stop it going bankrupt** — with a **seller note plus
~$30,000 cash.** It **doubled his headcount overnight** (12–14 → 27) and took the group from $1.6–1.7M
to $2.9M in two years.

**The critical judgement was diagnosing the *cause*:** *"It was badly managed. **The owner was
attempting to be a passive owner in a very tiny company**, and the people he had equipped to manage were
not capable of managing it."* **That is a fixable cause. "The market is tough" is not.**

**Two warnings from the same source.** First, on partial ownership: *"The business was very small —
**incapable of supporting two owners.** A million dollars of revenue. He was breaking into his 70s,
putting on the brakes; I was willing to take on more risk."* Four years handcuffed as a minority partner
followed, and even at 98% ownership *"**cap table was irrelevant in the face of family.**"* **Any deal
leaving the seller with ownership and an operating role needs a defined timeline and role, in writing,
at closing.**

Second, on sourcing: **100 letters sent, 5 responses, one deal.** That ratio holds in Canada, where
broker-listed deal flow is thinner.

### 🇨🇦 Canadian adjustments — home services

- **Boilers and hydronic heat are a strong Canadian analogue.** The installed base across Ontario, Quebec and Atlantic Canada is large and ageing, and the **$15–17K ticket** is broadly comparable.
- **⚠️ Union exposure matters more in Canada, not less.** Construction and trades unionisation is significantly higher than in the US, and **provincial successor-employer rules bind a purchaser to an existing collective agreement — often in an asset sale as well as a share sale.** One US operator escaped a union obligation only because attrition dropped the bargaining unit below the threshold; that route is far harder here. **Get certification status in writing early.**
- **⚠️ Trade licensing is provincial and individual.** Ontario: certified plumber, **TSSA gas-fitter registration**, **ODP certification** for refrigerant handling. Confirm who holds what and whether the company's ability to operate depends on a departing owner. **The licensing-redundancy practice is more valuable in Canada, not less.**
- **The financing ceiling is far tighter.** A $4.5M-revenue business with $1.4M EBITDA is comfortably SBA-financeable in the US; in Canada, with **CSBFP capped at $1.15M and asset-only**, expect a much larger equity plus VTB requirement.
- **Seasonality inverts but does not soften.** The operating line is the deliverable.

# 5. Cross-Industry: The Five Things That Are True Everywhere

Across 61 videos, four industries and roughly 40 operators, five findings recur regardless of trade.
**If you remember nothing else from this document, remember these.**

---

## 5.1 Labour supply, not demand, is the binding constraint

Every industry, every scale:

- **"Business is out there, we constantly get calls — but we don't have the people to do them, so we turn them away."** — cleaning, $1.4M [`UpFlip/Do-This-to-Make-140K-Month...`]
- **100 applications → 1 hire**, and permanent recruiting. [`10-Years-of-Cleaning-Business-Advice...`]
- **4,200 applications → ~20 usable contractors.** [`UpFlip/Remote-Cleaning-Businesses-are-the-Future`]
- **"It's harder to scale 'cause you need more people and it's a people-based business."** — pest control [`How-to-Start-a-Pest-Control-Business...`]
- **"You've got to hire technicians who are getting paid more elsewhere — why join us?"** — HVAC [`How-we-bought-3-...HVAC-Businesses`]
- The counter-example proves it: **95% retention and a 30-person hiring waiting list**, achieved through above-market pay, a real training pipeline, a 10-year truck programme and teaching crews personal finance. [`Lawn-Care-Podcast-...`]

**Buy-side consequence:** any acquisition thesis that assumes growth by adding crews must price a
**permanent recruiting function**. And a target with genuine retention is worth a premium that will not
appear anywhere in its financial statements.

---

## 5.2 Recurring beats one-off, everywhere, by a wide margin

| Industry | One-off | Recurring | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pest control | $250 transaction | **~$1,600 LTV** at identical CAC | `How-to-Transition-...` |
| Cleaning | $500–600 move-out | **~$3,000 gross profit** over 2 years | `10-Years-...` |
| Cleaning (commercial) | — | **~$288,000** over a 5-year contract | `How-To-PRICE-...` |
| Landscaping | Install: *"you never hear from that customer again"* | **"Unlimited potential for 10, 20 years"** | `HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth` |
| Snow | Per-push (weather bet) | **Seasonal contract, 5 equal payments** | `How-WE-Price-SNOW-REMOVAL-...` |

**And one-off work is worse than it looks**, because it consumes management attention out of all
proportion to revenue: *"A three-bedroom house across town takes about the same time as a three-bedroom
house here; two post-construction jobs can differ wildly. There were just far more variables that could
go wrong."* [`10-Years-...`]

**The distinction that decides the price:** *recurring* (contracted, auto-billed, auto-renewing) versus
*reoccurring* (customers who happen to call every spring). **Sellers will not make this distinction for
you.** [`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Analyzing-Software-Businesses-for-Sale-...`]

---

## 5.3 The owner-operator price list is the most common hidden defect

The mechanism, seen in three industries:

- **Landscaping:** the owner cut **24 lawns/day**; hired crews do **14**. A ~40% labour cost increase that may never have been repriced. [`Lawn-Care-Podcast-...`]
- **Cleaning:** a first bid to a church **omitted labour taxes, supplies and any net profit line** — and they signed instantly. **A signed contract proves nothing about whether it is priced correctly.** [`Filthy-Rich-Cleaners-E21`]
- **Pest control:** the **$89/quarter incumbent "who has been doing it since 1950 and never raised their prices."** [`How-to-Start-a-Pest-Control-Business...`]
- **Cleaning again:** *"You can't give me a 22% increase because you didn't do it for 5 years."* [`2026-Cleaning-Business-Resolutions...`]

**Buy-side consequence:** **re-price every contract from scratch during diligence.** Unraised books hold
genuine recoverable margin — but recovering it causes churn, and commercial clients on annual budgets
cannot absorb five years of deferred increases at once. **Model the recovery over years, and model the
churn.**

---

## 5.4 SDE is not your money, and it hides the capital bill

**The three claims on SDE** [`Acquisitions-Anonymous/Do-you-want-to-own-a-cleaning-company-Acquisitions-Anonymous-173.md`]:
> **(1) Debt service — paid first, not in the tipping order. (2) The buffer before decline hits your
> pocket. (3) The owner's comp — last in line.**

The worked example: **$213K SDE − $122K debt service = ~$90K/year**, which is *both* your salary and
your entire margin of safety. *"If one client cancels you go from making 90 grand a year to 60 grand a
year"* — on one $2,500/month customer.

**And the capital gap on top of that** [`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-...`]:
> "SDE and EBITDA **specifically don't include depreciation and amortisation, which means there's no
> allowance in there for capital investment.** If you forget that machines wear out and have to be
> replaced, or that to grow you might need more trucks, **then all of a sudden the cash flow you thought
> was there really isn't there.**"

**In every one of these industries — mowers, plows, trucks, sprayers, vans — the replacement reserve is
the largest single adjustment most buyers never make.** The ten-input equipment cost model in §2.1 is
how you quantify it.

---

## 5.5 The owner is usually the product, and nobody discloses it

The clearest illustration in the corpus, from a fried chicken franchise sold **three times** — the first
two deals collapsing at the eleventh hour [`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-...`]:

The numbers were excellent, which is exactly why they attracted the wrong buyers. The reason it
performed: **the owner was there from first light to 4pm, and his son-in-law ran it 4pm to 10pm.** He
**personally cleaned the French fry slicing machine every day** rather than risk an employee's fingers
and a workers' comp claim, and ran a **four-day rotating deep-clean regimen** he supervised himself.

> "People who worked at banks and government offices came to look at the business **because of the
> numbers.** When they sat down with Tony and heard these stories, they quickly realised that **if they
> were going to have that cash flow continue under their stewardship, they were going to have to spend
> 50 hours a week there.**"

**The two questions every buyer must ask:**
1. **"What's the cash flow?"** — that determines the price.
2. **"Will the cash flow continue under my stewardship?"** — that determines whether you should buy it at all.

The same finding, from the other side of the table:
- *"It's really not great when [the owner] is the one they want — then we don't have a business, we have a job."* [`Why-Clients-Complain-...`]
- *"There are companies I've looked at where it's all just the job for the business owner. **There's no value attached to that entity — the person buying is buying a job.**"* [`HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth`]
- *"The founder was happily wearing three or four hats that would otherwise need three or four individuals... **but when that founder exits, you need to replace those work streams with singular individuals.**"* [`In-The-Trenches-Divitkos/Searcher-to-CEO-...`]

**The counterweight, which is equally true:** at $1–2M of EBITDA, owner-embeddedness is not a
disqualifier — it's the price of admission.
> "**It's very hard to find a small business to buy with one or two million bucks of EBITDA that does
> not rely a little bit on being the owner in the market. It's just hard to not do that at this size.**"
> — Bill D'Alessandro, `Mix-and-Match-in-Florida-...AA-298`

**So the job is not to find a business without owner dependency. It is to (a) find out exactly what the
owner does, (b) price the replacement, and (c) structure a long enough transition to absorb it.**

---

## 5.6 The two-question test, and the one exercise nobody does

**The absentee test, which works as both an operating goal and a diligence question**
[`HOW-MUCH-is-a-Landscape-Business-Worth`]:
> "Try to leave your business for a day. Then seven days. Then two weeks. **If you can stay away for two
> solid weeks and everything goes fine — sales still come in, the business is profitable, people still
> show up — you're setting yourself up for a very successful sale.**"

**Ask a seller when they last took two consecutive weeks off, and what broke.**

**And the exercise Barnett says almost nobody does** [`David-C-Barnett/SMB-Expert-Series-...`]:
> "If you're going to buy a business in this industry, **have you ever considered getting a job in this
> industry — even part-time, evenings, weekends?** A person who's not a plumber might still get a job at
> a plumbing company doing something in the office. **No one does that. But it would mitigate a lot of
> risk** — and potentially maybe this wasn't for you after all."
